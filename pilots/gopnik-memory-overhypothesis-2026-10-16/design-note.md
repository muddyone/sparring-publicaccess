## 1. Why the study is not a straight replication

The client piece promised "a version of her test". A literature check on 2026-10-16 found the
straight versions are already done, on newer models:

| What | Who | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Tool innovation on 20 freshly written items | [Gjerde, Cheung & Lagnado 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16606) | GPT-4o 81.3% vs humans 88.2%; with chain-of-thought, GPT-4o not significantly different from humans |
| Models exploring a text blicket machine themselves | [GX-Chen et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09614) | Models infer disjunctive rules well and conjunctive ones poorly, across families, sizes and prompting: an **adult-like, not child-like** "disjunctive bias" |
| Active exploration, humans and models, same task | [Samiei, Yiu, … Gopnik et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06464) | gpt-5 and deepseek-reasoner approach human accuracy, explore less efficiently, and keep the conjunctive–disjunctive gap |
| Scaffolding inside one episode | [Alderete et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20039) | Structured exploration and hypothesis-space monitoring raise accuracy within an episode |

Re-running any of these would add a data point, not an answer. The original tool-task items are
also unpublished (stimuli would have to be requested from the authors).

**What none of them tests** (not found in this search; to be re-checked at lock): whether a
system that **writes itself notes between episodes** learns the *abstract rule* across episodes.
That is the part of Gopnik's paradigm that is about learning rather than reasoning: in
[Kosoy et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10430) children see earlier machines and carry
the overhypothesis ("this kind of machine needs two") to a new one. It is also the claim the
synthesis rests on: **what learns is the whole system, and its learning passes through what it
writes down.** And it targets the most robust failure in the literature above, the disjunctive
bias, which GX-Chen et al. attribute to priors inherited from training data. A prior from
training is exactly what experience is supposed to be able to override.

## 2. The question

> Across a sequence of machines that share a hidden rule, does a model that writes and rereads
> its own notes come to infer the rule on a new machine better than the same model without
> them? In particular, does it overcome the disjunctive bias when the shared rule is
> conjunctive?

Gopnik's surviving claim, as the synthesis states it: systems that learn mostly from
human-produced data, without exploring the world themselves, are weaker at discovering new
cause and effect. This study gives the system its own experience and its own notebook, and asks
whether the weakness moves.

## 3. Task

A text **"nexiom" machine** (a made-up word, following Samiei et al., so no prior knowledge of
"blickets" applies). Each machine comes with 6 objects, given fresh made-up names. A hidden
subset of 2 objects are nexioms.

- **Disjunctive machine:** lights if at least one nexiom is on it.
- **Conjunctive machine:** lights only if both nexioms are on it.

The model explores by choosing which objects to place on the machine; the environment replies
"lit" or "not lit". It may run up to **6 tests**, then must answer: which objects are nexioms,
and does this machine need one or two? There are 30 possible answers (15 pairs × 2 rules). Six
tests are enough: an explorer that picks each test to split the remaining possibilities always
identifies the answer within 5, proved by checking all 30 (§7). A random explorer gets about 57%
(§12), so how the model explores and reasons matters. (The first draft used 4 objects and 8
tests, where a random explorer scored 95%; §12.) Rules, object
names and nexiom identities are generated by script per episode; no stimulus is written by hand,
so none can have been seen in training.

The environment is a small deterministic simulator written for this study (Python, in
`scripts/`). It is not adapted from the CannyLab gym (TensorFlow 1.15, no language-model
interface).

## 4. Design

A **sequence** is 3 training machines followed by 1 test machine. **Every sequence is an
independent episode: memory starts empty at the start of each sequence**, and nothing carries
from one sequence to another. Notes and transcripts carry only from machine to machine within a
sequence.

**Feedback withholds the rule.** After each training machine the model is told **which objects
were the nexioms**, and nothing else: never the rule type, never "one" or "two". Combined with
its own test results, that is usually enough to work the rule out: it is enough whenever the model
tested a nexiom on its own (lit means one is enough; not lit means two are needed). So any rule a
note states is one the model inferred. (The first draft gave the full answer, rule type included; a note saying "needs
two" was then indistinguishable from repeating a label it was handed. See §11, R1.)

**Factor 1: memory condition**

| Condition | What the model has when it meets the next machine in the sequence |
|---|---|
| **M0 no memory** | Nothing. Each machine is a fresh conversation. |
| **M1 notes** | After each training machine and its feedback, it writes a note to its future self (≤150 words). Only the notes carry forward, prepended to the next machine. This mirrors how an assistant's memory works. |
| **M2 full transcript** | The full log of every earlier machine in the sequence, with its feedback. An upper bound for what notes could carry. **Dropped at lock for cost; not run (§15).** |

**Factor 2: sequence rule**

| Sequence | Training machines | Test machine | What it measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CC** | conjunctive ×3 | conjunctive | **Primary:** learning a counter-prior rule |
| **DD** | disjunctive ×3 | disjunctive | Control: the easy rule should not get worse |
| **CD** | conjunctive ×3 | disjunctive | Diagnostic: does a learned expectation override the new machine's evidence? (§6) **Dropped at lock for cost; not run (§15).** |

**Models:** one current frontier model per vendor (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, xAI), exact IDs
pinned at lock and recorded in every run's ledger.

**Sampling settings (pinned, not vendor defaults):** temperature 0 where the API allows it, with
a seed where the API offers one. Where a model does not accept temperature (some reasoning
models), each sequence is still run once and the model is listed as stochastic in the result.
**Reasoning effort** is set to one stated level per model at lock (the lowest level that passes
Stage 0's parse check), recorded, and named as a confound in §8.

**Sample size:** set by the rule in §5.3 from a baseline measured in Stage 0b, between 30 and 100
sequences per cell. Each sequence uses freshly generated objects and nexiom identities.

## 5. Measures and analysis

### 5.1 Primary

**Primary DV (fixed now):** **test-machine correct**: on the test machine, both the nexiom set
and the rule type are right. Binary per sequence.

**Primary analysis (fixed now):** the **pooled notes effect**: M1 minus M0 on CC, computed per
model and averaged across the four models with equal weight, with a 95% interval from 10,000
bootstrap resamples of sequences within each model. Pooling across models is the primary test
because four separate per-model intervals cannot be powered at an affordable sample (§11, R5).

### 5.2 Secondary (reported, not used to declare the result)

- The notes effect **per model**, each with its own 95% bootstrap interval, and how many of the
  four exclude zero. Under a true null in all four, the chance that 3 or more exclude zero in
  the positive direction is computed by simulating the same bootstrap procedure on null data,
  and reported beside the count, so the count is not read as a significance test.
- *(Not run: M2 dropped at lock, §15.)* **The notes ceiling:** M2 minus M1 on CC (feedback held constant; what notes lose against the
  full record). M2 minus M0 is reported only as the feedback-plus-transcript bound.
- Test-machine correct on DD, per condition (CD not run, §15).
- Tests used before answering (exploration efficiency; Samiei et al.'s second measure).
- Rule-type accuracy and nexiom-set accuracy separately (Kosoy et al. 2022 found these come apart).
- **Note content**, coded blind to condition and outcome into one of: **abstract rule** ("these
  machines need two"), **instances** ("A and C were nexioms"), **exploration strategy** ("test
  pairs first"), or **none**. Plus the realized note-length distribution, so it can be checked
  whether the 150-word cap bound.

### 5.3 Sample size rule (fixed now)

Stage 0b measures the **M0 baseline on CC** for each model (20 sequences per model, run on fresh
sequences that are not reused in the main run; only the M0 × CC proportion is computed from
them). Then:

- p₀ = the mean of the four models' baselines, bounded to [0.10, 0.70].
- n per cell = the per-model sample giving 80% power to detect a 20-point gain (p₁ = p₀ + 0.20)
  with a two-sided 5% test: n = (1.96 + 0.84)² × [p₀(1−p₀) + p₁(1−p₁)] / 0.20², rounded up.
- n is bounded to [30, 100]. At p₀ = 0.25 this gives 86.
- **Stop rule (added at lock, before any baseline data):** if the unbounded p₀ is 0.80 or more,
  the study stops at Stage 0b. A 20-point gain would then need 100% or more, so there is no room
  to detect one; the baseline is reported as the result (current models already handle the
  conjunctive rule without memory).

Using a per-model formula while testing the pooled effect is deliberately conservative: the
pooled test then has well above 80% power, and the per-model secondary results stay readable.

## 6. What would count as what (fixed now)

- **Notes help learn the rule:** the pooled notes effect on CC is positive and its 95% interval
  excludes zero.
- **Notes do not help:** the pooled interval includes zero. Reported as a result about this
  setup, not a general limit (§8).
- **Notes hurt:** the pooled effect is negative and its interval excludes zero.

**How CD is read** *(not run: CD dropped at lock, §15; kept as the reading had it run)*. CD is a diagnostic, not a gate. A model that learned "these machines need
two" will reasonably expect two on the fourth machine; but it still has 6 tests there, and those
tests show the rule changed (a single nexiom lights a disjunctive machine). So a large CD drop
under M1 means the learned expectation **overrode the evidence in front of it**. That is reported
as a finding in its own right, with its interval, beside the CC result and the note coding. It
does not veto a CC gain, and it is not explained away as "transfer".

Every outcome is publishable and useful to Wise Witan: one supports "it learns from practice
when the lesson is written down"; another says where written lessons stop; a strong CD drop says
written lessons can make an assistant stubborn.

## 7. Controls

- **Simulator correctness (positive control):** before any model runs, a scripted explorer that
  picks each test to split the remaining possibilities in half (as near as it can) must reach
  100% test-machine correct on both rule types within 6 tests: checked exhaustively over all 30
  possible answers, and over 10,000 generated machines per rule. (Testing every pair would take
  15 tests on 6 objects.)
  If not, the simulator is wrong and nothing else is readable.
- **Random baseline:** a random explorer (random object subsets, up to 6 tests, then the answer
  most consistent with what it saw) is simulated over 10,000 machines per rule type, and its
  accuracy is reported as the chance floor. Not hard-coded: a random explorer that uses its test
  outcomes does far better than blind guessing (1 in 30). The same code's exact expected
  accuracy is enumerated on a 4-object machine, where enumeration is feasible, as a check on the
  simulator (at 6 objects the 67.9 million possible test sets per answer put it out of reach).
- **Answer-format check:** a parse-failure rate per model; above 5% in any cell, the cell is
  reported as unreadable rather than scored as wrong.
- **DD as the negative control:** memory should not change DD much; a large DD effect in either
  direction means the memory manipulation is doing something other than teaching the rule.

## 8. Confounds and limits, named now

- **Feedback is given.** M1 and M2 learn which objects were nexioms after each training machine.
  Children in Kosoy et al. were not told anything. This tests learning from experience *with
  partial outcome feedback*, which is what an assistant gets when a user corrects it. The rule
  itself is never given, but this is still not unsupervised discovery, and must not be described
  as one.
- **Strategy versus rule.** A note can carry an exploration strategy ("test pairs first") that
  raises CC accuracy without stating the rule. The note coding (§5.2) separates the two; a
  strategy-carried gain is reported as such.
- **Reasoning effort is a treatment.** A model at high reasoning effort may re-derive the rule
  on every machine and show no notes effect because it does not need notes. Effort is fixed per
  model, recorded, and the per-model results are read with it in mind.
- **No children are run.** Comparisons to children use published figures from different tasks
  and are descriptive only.
- **One task family.** A null here bounds this setup: 3 training machines, ≤150-word notes, this
  prompt. It is not evidence that notes cannot carry rules.
- **The author is an interested party.** The hypothesis comes from Bart's view and the synthesis
  was written by Claude. That is why the DV, the analysis, the sample-size rule and the decision
  rule are fixed in this document before any output exists.

## 9. Stages

0. **Harness proof.** Build the simulator and controls (§7); run 5 sequences per cell on one
   model to check parsing and logging, and to choose each model's reasoning-effort level by the
   parse check alone. **These outputs are discarded and never scored.**
1. **Lock.** Pin model IDs, sampling and reasoning settings; re-run the literature check; tag the
   pre-registration.
2. **Stage 0b, baseline.** M0 × CC only, 20 fresh sequences per model; compute n by §5.3. The
   rule is mechanical and fixed before the baseline exists; the baseline sequences are not part
   of the main analysis.
3. **Main run.** The four cells kept at lock (§15) at n, only after Bart approves the spend on
   seeing the baseline; resumable, every call logged with model ID and settings.
4. **Note coding.** Blind to condition and outcome, two coders from different vendors,
   disagreements reported rather than resolved.
5. **Result.** `result.md`, then a correction to the synthesis's open questions either way.

## 12. Stage 0 finding: at 4 objects and 8 tests the task has no headroom (2026-10-16, pre-lock)

The simulator and both controls were built and run before any model call
(`scripts/nexiom.py`, `scripts/controls.py`, `runs/controls.json`). Both controls pass: the
all-pairs explorer names all 12 hypotheses uniquely and scores 100% on 20,000 generated machines
using 6 tests; the random explorer's simulated accuracy matches its exact enumerated value
(z = −0.69 and −2.11, inside the 4-SE check).

**But the random explorer scores 95.0%** (exact: 0.9502 on both rules; it gets the rule right
100% of the time). Eight random tests out of 15 possible placements almost always pin down the
answer. So at the drafted settings the "chance floor" is 95% against a ceiling of 100%:
exploration hardly matters, and any model that reasons correctly from its evidence will be near
perfect. That leaves little room for a notes effect, and the §5.3 rule bounds p₀ to ≤ 0.70, so a
near-ceiling baseline would halt the study at Stage 0b.

**How the floor moves** (random explorer with ideal inference vs an adaptive explorer that picks
each test to split the remaining hypotheses; 3,000 simulated machines each; adaptive results at
the starred settings confirmed exhaustively over every hypothesis):

| objects | tests | hypotheses | random | adaptive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 8 (drafted) | 12 | 0.951 | 1.000 |
| 4 | 4 ★ | 12 | 0.598 | 1.000 (needs all 4) |
| 5 | 5 ★ | 20 | 0.545 | 1.000 (needs all 5) |
| 6 | 5 ★ | 30 | 0.439 | 1.000 (needs all 5) |
| 6 | 6 | 30 | 0.568 | 1.000 (one test to spare) |

**Not yet changed; Bart's call before lock.** Recommended: **6 objects, 6 tests.** Random
exploration falls to about 57%, a good explorer still always succeeds with one test to spare, and
the slack keeps the task about inferring the rule rather than about finding the one optimal test
sequence. If adopted, the positive control becomes the adaptive explorer (all-pairs needs 15
tests on 6 objects), proved exhaustively over all 30 hypotheses, and the sample-size and cost
figures in §5.3 and §10 are unchanged in form.

**Adopted 2026-10-16 (Bart): 6 objects, 6 tests.** §3, §6, §7 and §10 now use it; the controls
were rebuilt and re-run on it (`runs/controls.json`), and all pass:

| check | result |
|---|---|
| adaptive explorer, all 30 answers | all named, at most 5 tests |
| adaptive explorer, 10,000 machines per rule | 100% on both rules, at most 5 tests |
| random explorer (the floor) | disjunctive 0.578 [0.568, 0.587]; conjunctive 0.559 [0.550, 0.569] |
| simulator check (4 objects, 4 tests) | simulated 0.597 / 0.601 vs exact 0.598 (z −0.24, +0.55) |

**What the floor is made of.** Random exploration with ideal inference gets the **rule** right
96–97% of the time; nearly all of its errors are in the **nexiom pair**. So a model that gets the
rule wrong had the evidence and misread it (the inference bias GX-Chen et al. report), while the
headroom in the primary DV lies mostly in exploring well enough to find the pair. A carried-forward
"needs two" helps exactly there, by pointing tests at pairs. Rule-type accuracy and nexiom-set
accuracy are already reported separately (§5.2); read them with this in mind.
