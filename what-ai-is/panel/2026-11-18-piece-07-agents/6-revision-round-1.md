## For Bart

1. **The title.** Was "A smarter AI isn't a safer one. What you let it do is what counts." Rosetta: keep the
   first half, change the second to "Ask what it can touch". Julian: "A smarter agent isn't a safer one.
   Ask what it can touch." (alternative: "Before you let an AI act for you, ask what it can touch.").
   Miriam and Vera: keep the first half as worded, read as "isn't safer by being smarter", which the new
   scope paragraph now states. Nadia: works as was. **Done:** changed to Julian's wording, on the drafter's
   ruling mid-revision. **My recommendation:** keep it. It says "agent", it carries the rule, and the
   scope paragraph backs "isn't a safer one".

2. **The bolded position paragraph** (unchanged; I found no factual error in it). Proposals:
   - Rosetta 2, Julian 4: end on the rule's one wording ("ask what it can touch, not how smart it is")
     instead of "what am I letting it do?". It is now the only place the rule is worded differently from
     the title, step 1 and the close.
   - Miriam 10: how far it goes before anyone looks doesn't set the limit; it decides how much goes wrong
     before someone can stop it. The body now says this ("three settings"), so the position and the body
     disagree.
   - Rosetta 3, Nadia 12: "for good or ill" doesn't fit a limit on *harm*. Rosetta's target: "set the limit
     on what it can do, harm included. How capable it is decides how much of that it actually does, good
     and bad."
   - Rosetta 18: "In the working sense" comes before the sense is defined; she suggests "In everyday use".
   - Miriam 3: "So a smarter model isn't a safer one" is unscoped; with the body's scope paragraph she
     would keep it.
   **My recommendation:** "My position: the same AI model, set up differently, is a different worker. An
   'agent', in the sense this essay uses, is an AI you've let act for you. That's a role you give it, not a
   measure of how clever it is. What it's allowed to touch sets the limit on the harm it can do. How
   capable it is decides how close it comes to that limit, and how far it goes before anyone looks decides
   how much goes wrong before you can stop it. So a smarter agent isn't a safer one, and the question to
   ask before you hand an AI a job is not 'how smart is it?' but 'what can it touch?'" That matches the
   body, the title and the rule.

3. **The worked example** (unchanged). Proposals:
   - Julian 1: move it to the opening, in past tense ("Earlier this week…"), and leave a short callback at
     the bottom; give one sentence of why the public step waits for you, in your words.
   - Nadia (flag 7) and Rosetta 15: "Today" is false on Friday; say "this week". Nadia: if the push is still
     waiting at publication, say so, since it shows the rule has a cost; say "a public page"; add one
     sentence on what could have gone wrong.
   - Julian 12: cut "That's the point."
   - Miriam 6: say which kind of limit it is. If the agents can't publish because the tool won't let them,
     the example is perfect; if the hold is a rule they follow, it illustrates obedience, the weaker kind.
     **This now matters more:** the revised body says, in "What it's allowed to touch", that an instruction
     is text the model reads and can be overridden, and that a limit is a setting that takes the ability
     away. As written, the example ("They aren't allowed to") describes a rule plus your sign-off, which
     the body now says is not the kind of limit to rely on.
   **My recommendation:** reword it to say what is true (the agents could technically push; what holds it
   is a rule and your sign-off), and turn that into the example's point: this is the weaker kind of limit,
   it held because the agents followed it and a second one checked, and the step that matters is one you'd
   move into the tool's settings if the stakes were higher. Past tense, "this week". I'd keep it at the
   bottom: with the enforced-limit paragraph now in the body, it reads best as the essay applied to itself,
   and moving it to the top would open on a nuance before the reader has the distinction.

4. **The "done" moment** (unchanged; kept, as directed). Asks:
   - Julian 7: one concrete instance, what the tool said it had done and what you found. No count, no
     record reference. (The brief's note on the kinds of failure is withheld from this copy: it points to an
     unpublished record.)
   - Miriam 17: bridge from "tools" to "agents" ("if plain tools can report 'done' falsely, an agent's
     'done' deserves the same check"), and "not proof" for "not evidence".
   **My recommendation:** add one of the two instances in a sentence (the edit tool is the closer fit to
   an agent's "done"), add Miriam's bridge, and keep "not evidence" (it is brief 13's own wording and a
   reader hears it as "doesn't show", which is right).
