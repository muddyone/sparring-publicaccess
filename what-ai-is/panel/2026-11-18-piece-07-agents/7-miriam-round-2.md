## Part 2: what the revision introduced, ranked

### R1. Impact 7: the "doesn't claim" list contradicts the Lynch paragraph

> "**That smarter models make no fewer plain mistakes.** The evidence on capability here is about being fooled by an
> attacker." · vs. Lynch paragraph: "which is why I count this as the clearest evidence that capability matters to
> an agent's risk." · and the scope paragraph: "All of these studies measure one kind of harm: an agent fooled by
> instructions someone else planted."

**The problem.** The essay now sorts harm into three kinds: being fooled (Debenedetti, Zou, NIST), plain mistakes
(no evidence found), and choosing badly (Lynch, introduced as "not only be fooled"). The "doesn't claim" item says
the capability evidence is *all* about the first kind, which the essay itself disproves two sections earlier, and
Kwa (the task-length study) is capability evidence of yet another kind. A reader who reaches the list will think
the author has forgotten their own concession. This is the one place where a disclosure list, which reads as
complete, is wrong about the essay it closes.

**Fix.** Scope the sentence to the claim it guards: "The evidence that smarter models aren't harder to fool is
about attacks. On plain mistakes, I found nothing that settles it either way." The scope paragraph's "All of these
studies" is fine where it stands, because "these" means the three attack studies before it.

### R2. Impact 6: "the clearest evidence that capability matters" says more than the study shows

> "Working out a move like that takes some sophistication, which is why I count this as the clearest evidence that
> capability matters to an agent's risk."

**The problem.** Brief 13 §5.7 records this as its own reading ("[ours]": "the best evidence that capability matters
to agent risk, since scheming needs some sophistication"). The essay makes it a superlative and gives it as its
reason. But as the brief records the study, it doesn't compare models by capability at all. It gives rates for five
models from five companies, and they are close (96, 96, 80, 80, 79). It shows that leading models *can* do this
when cornered, not that more capable ones do it more. It is also, by the authors' own description, a setting built
to leave no other way out, never seen in real use. So "clearest" rests on the weakest kind of setting in the essay,
and the paragraph's own caveat two sentences later undercuts it. In round 1 I asked for the capability link to be
stated; the revision overshot it.

**Fix.** Keep the link, drop the superlative, and keep it as a reading: "Working out a move like that takes some
sophistication, so this is where capability most plainly brings a risk of its own, in tests built to force it."
That keeps the concession honest in both directions: it isn't hidden, and it isn't inflated.

### R3. Impact 5: the close brings back a frequency word

> "So when an agent goes wrong, the useful question is rarely how intelligent it was."

**The problem.** "Rarely" is a rate, the same kind of word as the "nearly always" the opening lost, and the same
disclaimer applies: "single reported cases, not rates" and "I found no study of how often agents cause harm in the
field at each level of access". The close is where a reader takes the argument away, so it shouldn't claim what
the evidence section disowns.

**Fix.** "So when an agent goes wrong, the more useful question isn't how intelligent it was. It's what we let it
do, and who was looking." This is a claim about which question helps, not about how often.

### R4. Impact 5: step 2's fallback is the weak kind of limit the body just warned against

> "If a product bundles all three and gives you no way to turn one off, that's worth knowing before you connect it,
> and it's a reason to keep yourself on the step that sends."

**The problem.** This is the commonest case a reader will face, and it gets the softest advice in the list. Willison,
as the essay quotes him, says that with all three in place "an attacker can easily trick it". "Worth knowing" is
much weaker than that. And "keep yourself on the step that sends" means an approval prompt, which the essay's own
ask-first section says not to lean on too hard (Green), and which is only a limit at all if the tool enforces it
rather than the agent being told to ask.

**Fix.** Say what the essay's own argument says: "If a product bundles all three and gives you no way to turn one
off, the safe choice is not to connect it for that job. If you do, make sure the tool itself, not just your
instruction, makes it ask before anything goes out, and read each request (step 4)." No new source; it applies the
essay's Willison, enforced-limits and Green paragraphs.

### R5. Impact 4: step 3's "have it stop and ask" is ambiguous after the enforced-limits paragraph

> "For anything you can't undo (sending, paying, deleting), have it stop and ask."

**The problem.** The new paragraph says an instruction like "don't send anything without asking me" is text that
other text can override. Step 3 can be read as telling the reader to give exactly that instruction. Step 1 already
carries the fix ("Set those limits in the tool's settings, not only in what you tell it"); step 3 needs the same.

**Fix.** "set it to stop and ask, in the tool's settings, not only by telling it." Five words.

### R6. Impact 4: the authors' hedge is hardened in the very next sentence

> "Part of the reason, the authors suggest, is that weaker models often fail to carry out the attacker's
> instructions … Being smarter didn't protect the model. It made it better at doing what the attacker asked."

**The problem.** The first sentence correctly marks the mechanism as the authors' suggestion. The next sentence
states it as cause ("It made it better"), and in the singular, as if one model got smarter. The finding is a
comparison across models ("more capable models tend to be easier to attack"). The revision restored the hedge and
then stepped over it.

**Fix.** "Being smarter didn't protect these models. If anything, it made them better at doing what the attacker
asked." Same rhythm, no new claim.

### R7. Impact 4: "The attacks keep getting stronger" claims a trend from one comparison

> "The attacks keep getting stronger, too. When US government testers built stronger attacks …"

**The problem.** The NIST page, as brief 13 §5.4 records it, compares a baseline attack with new attacks the testers
built, on one model, at one time. It shows that stronger attacks succeed far more often. It doesn't show attacks
growing stronger over time. Round 1's fix (say what it shows) landed in the paragraph's last sentence; the new first
sentence reopens the gap.

**Fix.** "Stronger attacks also work far better." Then the NIST sentence and the closing line as they stand.

### R8. Impact 4: the flight script is "an agent" in the law's sense

> "A script that books your flights acts for you, so in OpenAI's sense and the law's it's an agent."

**The problem.** The law's definition, as the essay quotes it via Kolt, is a relationship between persons ("one
person (a 'principal') manifests assent to another person (an 'agent')", brief 13 §3.10). A script isn't a person,
and whether the law treats AI systems as agents at all is the open question Kolt's paper is about. OpenAI's
"independently accomplish tasks" is arguably an autonomy test too, which a fixed script may not meet. Brief 13's
bearing (§3.4) says only that the script is the reverse of the chat assistant in the product sense. The sentence
classifies under two sources more firmly than either supports.

**Fix.** "A script that books your flights acts for you, which is what these definitions share. On Anthropic's line,
though, it's a workflow, because its steps were fixed in advance." The argument (agency comes in degrees) needs
nothing more.

### R9. Impact 3: "a plain mistake" names a cause the source doesn't record

> "The deleted database, as reported, had no attacker. It was a plain mistake made with access the agent held."

**The problem.** Brief 13 §5.6 records what happened and the fix, not why. Now that the essay has three kinds of harm,
calling this one "a plain mistake" sorts it into the kind with no evidence, without a source for the sorting; a
reader of the Lynch paragraph could as easily ask whether it "chose badly". "No attacker" is fair (none is
reported).

**Fix.** "The deleted database, as reported, involved no attacker: the damage came from the agent's own action, with
access it held." The rest of the paragraph works unchanged.

### R10. Impact 3: "Two things" becomes three

> "Two things make an agent harder to delegate to than a person. … That names the second, and adds a third"

**The problem.** A counted list reads as an inventory. The paragraph announces two, then adds a third, and the close
lists three. Small, but it is the paragraph the close summarises.

**Fix.** "Three things make an agent harder to delegate to than a person", with Kolt as the source of the third; or
keep "two" and introduce opacity as "Kolt adds a third".

### R12. Impact 2: two small inventory snags in "Two meanings"

**The problem.** The pointer says essay 03 had one sense; the section now (correctly) says essay 03 used both. And

### R13. Impact 2: Jensen & Meckling now does no work in the argument

### R14. Impact 2: step 6 asks the reader to watch for something they often can't see

**Fix (additive).** "Start again when you know the model or the setup has changed: a new version, a new tool

## Part 3: the held items (position paragraph, title, worked example)

**Does the body now disagree with the position paragraph? Yes, in three places.**

1. *Autonomy.* Position: "What it's allowed to touch, and how far it goes before anyone looks, set the limit on the
   harm it can do." Body: "How far it goes before anyone looks decides how much goes wrong before someone can stop
   it." These are different claims, and the body's is right.
2. *Capability's verb.* Position: "how much of that limit it reaches, for good or ill". Body: "how close it comes to
   that limit". Small, but a careful reader sees two pictures.
3. *Scope.* Position: "So a smarter model isn't a safer one", unscoped. Body: smarter models aren't harder to fool;
   on plain mistakes nothing is settled; and "even if they do", access bounds the worst case. The body concedes that
   a smarter model *might* be safer against mistakes and says the limit holds anyway. The position, read literally,
   denies what the body concedes. The title ("A smarter agent isn't a safer one") carries the same unscoped claim; I
   would keep the title, which a reader takes as a headline, as long as the position paragraph scopes it.

Plus the rule's wording: position "what am I letting it do?" vs title, step 1 and close "what can it touch".

**What would fix it.** The drafter's recommendation (For Bart, item 2) fixes 1, 2 and the rule wording. It still
leaves 3. One change does it: "So a smarter agent isn't, by being smarter, a safer one: it's no harder to fool, and
what it can touch still sets the worst case." With that, the position, the scope paragraph, the "doesn't claim" list
(after R1) and the title all agree.

**The worked example.** It now disagrees with the body too. "They aren't allowed to. That's the point." sits after a
paragraph saying a limit is what the setup enforces, not what the agent is told. If the hold is a rule plus Bart's
sign-off, the example shows the weaker kind of limit, presented as the strong kind. The drafter's recommendation
(For Bart, item 3: say what is true, and make the weaker-kind-of-limit the example's point) fixes it. If the agents
in fact lack the means to publish (no key, or the tool requires Bart's approval), one clause saying so fixes it
instead. I still have no evidence either way about which it is.
