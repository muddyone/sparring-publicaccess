# Nadia Okonkwo: readability pass, piece 07 (agents)

_Cold read, 2026-11-20. Read once, on a phone, title to "Where I land". This time I read as an
everyday AI user: I use ChatGPT or Claude, I keep hearing agents will do things for me, and my
gut says AI getting smarter is a bit scary._

## 1. Where did I stop or stall?

I didn't stop in the bold paragraph. The title got me, because it's my worry, and the opening said my
worry "isn't wrong". That kept me reading.

I stalled at **"Two meanings of 'agent'"**. Up to that point it was about me and my inbox. Then it
turned into a conversation about the series: "Essay 03 used the first sense", "quasi-subject", "The
series overview put that as...", the Restatement of Agency, Wooldridge & Jennings 1995. On a real
phone I'd have scrolled fast here, looking for where it gets back to me. It gets back at "What it's
allowed to touch sets the limit", and from there to the end it mostly held me.

There was a smaller wobble earlier, at "Throughout this series, 'it' has meant..." That's a
housekeeping paragraph, and it tells me a section I don't want is on its way.

## 2. What did I think it was about?

After two lines: "Don't worry about how smart the AI is. Worry about what you've given it the keys
to." That's what the essay is about, so the opening did its job.

## 3. Comment or scroll?

I'd save it for the checklist. "Let it draft, and you press send" is the one line I'd actually use.
If I commented, it would be about the blackmail study, because that's the bit that scared me: *"So
96 out of 100 chose blackmail, and the takeaway is 'limit the access'? That's reassuring, I guess,
but it's the part I'll remember."* The essay does handle it fairly ("built to force that choice",
"not seen ... in real deployments"). But a nervous reader takes away the number, not the hedge.

## Was it easy to read?

Mostly, yes. The access sections, the invoice example, Willison's three questions, the "clicked
'allow' for the twentieth time" line and the to-do list are all plain and easy. The hard parts are
the definitions section and a handful of single sentences. These are the ones I read twice:

- **"That worry isn't wrong, but it watches the one thing you can't control."** "Watches"? I read
  it as the worry keeping an eye on something. It took a second pass to get "it focuses on".
- **"And being smarter doesn't make an agent safe."** (bold paragraph) I never thought it did, I
  thought the opposite. For a beat it felt like it was arguing with somebody else.
- **"A chat assistant with no tools is the mirror image of that script: an agent in the
  philosopher's sense, but not in the working sense."** Lost me. I had to go back for "that script".
- **"estimated that most of them were worth as much as training the model with more than five
  times the computing power."** Read twice to work out what was being compared with what.
- **"Now back to the worry, and to its opposite. The opposite is a hope..."** Jumpy. Which worry,
  and whose hope? It took me a moment to find my footing.
- **"In the first benchmark, the weaker models were hijacked less often mostly, the authors
  suggest, because they often failed at the work, the attacker's and yours alike."** Tripped on
  "less often mostly". Read twice.
- **"Stronger attacks also work far better."** Sounds like "of course they do". I didn't get why it
  was there until the next sentence.
- **"the length of software task a leading model could finish about half the time"** Read twice.
- **"But when one of them looked, it found nothing in the setup that would have stopped it putting
  the fix live."** Looked at what? On a first read I thought one agent was spying on another.
  Also, "The same goes for an assistant that handles your email" runs straight on with no break.
  On a phone that reads as one big block.
- **"That smarter models make just as many plain mistakes."** (under "What this doesn't claim")
  It's a negative inside a negative. I couldn't tell what he does think.
- **The third "What would change my mind" bullet.** One sentence, five lines, two "or if"s. I gave
  up halfway through and skipped to the end.

## The bold opening paragraph

It reads fairly smoothly and I understood it on the first pass, apart from "watches". It's long for
a phone, nine sentences in bold, but every sentence moved me along. The one jolt is "And being
smarter doesn't make an agent safe": nobody in my position thought it did. I'd point that sentence
at the worry instead. Something like "a smarter agent is just as easy to trick" would agree with me
rather than correct someone else.

"How far it goes before a person looks decides how much goes wrong before anyone can stop it" was
fine, if a bit of a mouthful.
