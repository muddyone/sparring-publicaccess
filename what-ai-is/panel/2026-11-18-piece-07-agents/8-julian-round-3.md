## Diagnosis

The reframe's central move is right and it is the best new sentence pair in the essay: "The pitch and the
worry are both about how capable these systems are getting. I think that puts the attention in the wrong
place." That puts the worried reader next to the industry and disagrees with both at once. Nobody is being
marked down alone. The new "What would change my mind" item and the new "What this doesn't claim" item do the
same work from the other side: they take the worry seriously enough to name what would prove it right.

The trouble is in the splices. **The concessions are vague and the corrections are specific.** The reader is
granted "something to it" (l.15) and "something in it" (l.334). Meanwhile they are told their view "watches the
wrong dial", is "half right", is held "in its usual form" only under "tests built to force it", and is answered
on a remedy ("a less capable one") they never proposed. Each is true or arguable. Together they are the
report-card texture the correction-stack pass exists to catch. **The fix is fewer, not gentler:** two of these
can be cut whole without any finding falling over, and one can be restated as a sharper claim rather than a
grade.

The rest is splice damage of an ordinary kind: "the worry" now appears twelve times as a fixed label, the same
phrase opens two consecutive paragraphs, the "What being smarter changes" lead restates the worry for the third
time and then bends into a figure ("gives the worry part of what it fears"), and the close stacks three
statements of the rule with a pointer ("ask that") that now points at the wrong sentence.

Momentum: the opening is stronger than before, because a worry is a better hook than a pitch. It holds through
the evidence section until "That is the worry, half right", where the prose stops reporting and starts
grading. It recovers at Lynch, dips at the triple restatement before "How far it goes", and the close lands
less cleanly than the old one did.

The position paragraph grew from 165 to 199 words. Length is not the finding (the cap limits packaging, never
the message); finding 2 is about what it now carries.

## Ranked findings

### 1. The title is a hook, not a position, and it changes the subject on the worried reader (Impact 8)

**Passage:** "Worried AI agents are getting too smart? Ask what they can touch."

**What's wrong.** Every title in the series states a position in two short declaratives, and the strongest
grant something before they turn: "It learns. The better question is what kind of learner it is." "We don't ask
whether friendship is safe. We ask which ones are." "Ask the AI twice. Believe the disagreement." This one opens
on "Worried …?", the form of an ad headline ("Worried about X? Try Y."). It's the hype-and-hook register Bart
doesn't write in. Worse for this reader, the question takes the worry as a hook and then steers past it. The
title neither grants nor disputes the worry. It points somewhere else, which to someone who holds the worry
reads as "never mind that". The essay does better than its title: it grants part of the worry on evidence. The
title should too. Minor: the title says "they", while the reader's rule everywhere else says "it" ("Ask what it
can touch, not how smart it is").

**Fix.** Use the series form: grant, then turn. Three options:

1. **"Smarter agents can do more harm. Ask what they can touch."** *(my pick)*
2. "You can't choose how smart it gets. You can choose what it touches."
3. "Smarter agents can do more harm. What they can touch sets the limit."

I pick 1. Its first sentence grants the worry plainly and stays inside the essay's own position ("a smarter
agent is more dangerous mainly because it does more with the access it has"). It uses "can", so it claims no
more than the evidence shows. Its second sentence is the reader's one rule, word for word, so the title is also
the thing they'll act on. Option 2 carries the essay's best new line (control), but it never grants the worry,
so it sidesteps the reader in a different way. Option 3 is accurate but swaps the action for a mechanism; this
reader is here for the action. If option 1's "they" clashes with the rule's "it", the rule in step 1 can stay
singular: a title speaks about agents in general, a step speaks about the one in front of you.

### 2. The position paragraph corrects twice where it grants once, and one correction answers a remedy the reader never proposed (Impact 7)

**Passage:** "There's something to it, but it watches the wrong dial. … A less capable one isn't the answer
either: it does less of the job you wanted, and what it can touch still sets the worst case."

**What's wrong.** Correction stack, mapped by position. The worried reader gets one vague grant ("There's
something to it") and two corrections. The first, "watches the wrong dial", is aimed at them alone. The opening
paragraph's "puts the attention in the wrong place" is aimed at the pitch and the worry together, and that's
the version that disagrees as an equal. The second, "A less capable one isn't the answer either", corrects a
remedy nobody on the page has offered. The worried reader didn't say "use a dumber model"; the sentence puts it
in their mouth in order to answer it. Ask whether the position falls over without it: it doesn't. The same
point stands whole, and symmetrically, at the end of the evidence section ("Neither 'use a smarter model' nor
'use a less capable one' is a substitute for narrower access"), where it's aimed at both sides and comes after
the evidence. Note also that "it watches" makes the worry the subject of "watches", and "dial" turns up here
before the essay has said which dial is which. Later, access is "the dial you can set" (l.313). So the image
first marks the worry as wrong, and only later explains itself.

The opening, "My position: the worry I hear most about AI agents is that …", also states someone else's view
under "My position". The reader has to get to the second sentence to find Bart's.

**Fix.** Cut the "less capable" sentence from the position. Keep it whole at l.211. Don't soften it there.
Replace the grade with the sharper claim the essay actually argues, which keeps the disagreement whole:

> **My position: the worry I hear most about AI agents, that they're getting too smart and smarter means more
> dangerous, has something to it. But it watches the dial you can't set, and not the one that sets the worst
> case. …**

Then the closing pair ("You don't control how smart the next model is. You do control what it can touch.")
pays off "the dial you can't set" instead of repeating it. That's a stronger claim than "wrong dial", not a
gentler one: it says why the dial is wrong.

### 3. "That is the worry, half right" grades the reader, and "plans of its own" corrects a version of the worry they may not hold (Impact 7)

**Passage:** "That is the worry, half right: the smarter model was the more dangerous one, but because it was
better at following someone else's orders, not because it had plans of its own."

**What's wrong.** "Half right" is a mark on the reader's paper. It's the one place the prose stops reporting
evidence and scores the reader instead, and it's where momentum drops. Then "not because it had plans of its
own" assigns the reader a specific picture of the worry (a scheming AI) in order to correct it. Four
paragraphs later the essay does the same thing again ("the worry in its usual form, a capable AI choosing harm
to serve its own ends"). So the reader's belief gets defined twice, by us, each time just before it's
corrected. Ask whether the finding falls over without the grade and the counter-picture: it doesn't. "Capable
models can also choose badly, not only be fooled" already carries the fooled-versus-choosing split, in its
right place.

**Fix.** Cut "half right" and the "not because" clause whole, and let the finding stand as a finding:

> … If anything, it made them better at doing what the attacker asked. Here, the smarter model was the more
> dangerous one, because it was better at following someone else's orders.

That grants more than the current line does, and it grades nothing.

### 4. The lead of "What being smarter changes" restates the worry a third time and then bends into a figure (Impact 6)

**Passage:** "Here the worry and the industry's hope meet. The worry is that a smarter agent is more dangerous.
The hope is that better models will grow out of being fooled. The evidence so far, from test benchmarks, doesn't
support the hope, and it gives the worry part of what it fears, by a different route."

**What's wrong.** Three problems. (a) "The worry is that a smarter agent is more dangerous" is the third time
in a few hundred words the reader has been told what they think. They know. (b) "Meet" is the wrong verb: the
worry and the hope don't meet, they're tested on the same evidence and come out differently. (c) "it gives the
worry part of what it fears, by a different route": "it" is the evidence, a worry can't fear, and "by a
different route" is a preview with no content yet. Read aloud, it's the sentence a listener would ask you to
repeat. "The industry's hope" also arrives with "the", as if we'd already met it. The opening's industry
figure was "the pitch", which was about errands, not about growing out of being fooled.

**Fix.**

> The hope is that better models will grow out of being fooled. The evidence so far, from test benchmarks,
> doesn't support that hope. It does support part of the worry, though not in the form it usually takes.

The specifics follow immediately in the next paragraph, so the preview is one clause, not a riddle. (Whether
"the hope" needs an owner at all is Ines's call; I've kept the draft's attribution level and dropped only the
presupposing "the industry's".)

### 5. The close: three statements of the rule, and "ask that" now points at the wrong sentence (Impact 6)

**Passage:** "The worry that it's getting too smart has something in it: a smarter model will do more of
whatever it's allowed to do, good and bad. But you don't control how smart the next model is. You do control
what it can touch. Before you hand one a job, ask that in advance: what can it touch, not how smart is it. Set
the limit the way you'd set it for a person in that job, …"

**What's wrong.** Before the splice, "ask that in advance" pointed at "what we let it do, and who was
looking". It now follows "You do control what it can touch", so "that" means "what it can touch", and the colon
then says "what can it touch" again. That's three "touch" clauses in about thirty words. "Set the limit" lost
its antecedent when "The limit is yours to set" was cut, and "the limit" now leans back on body text several
screens up. "The worry that it's getting too smart": "it" is grammatically "an agent" from the sentence before
(one agent that went wrong), but the worry is about AI in general. "In advance" and "before" also say the same
thing twice. The ending of the old close, "The limit is yours to set", was the better landing. The new control
pair says the same thing at more length.

**Fix.**

> So when an agent goes wrong, the more useful question isn't how intelligent it was. It's what we let it do,
> and who was looking. The worry that AI is getting too smart has something in it: a smarter model will do more
> of whatever it's allowed to do, good and bad. But you don't control how smart the next model is. You do
> control what it can touch. So before you hand one a job, set that limit the way you'd set it for a person in
> that job. Then remember how an agent is different: …

The reader's rule is already in the title, step 1 and the position. The close doesn't need the slogan form
again; it needs the act.

### 6. "The worry" has become a fixed label, twelve times, and two consecutive paragraphs open on the same phrase (Impact 5)

**Passage:** "My position: the worry I hear most about AI agents …" (l.14), then "The worry I hear most often runs
the other way" (l.26). After that, "the worry" ten more times.

**What's wrong.** Two adjacent paragraphs repeat "the worry I hear most" almost word for word, and that's where
the splice shows. It's also inconsistent ("most" / "most often"). Across the essay, "the worry" repeated as a
term handles the reader's belief at arm's length, like a specimen under discussion. That adds to the
correction-stack texture even where each sentence is fair.

**Fix.** In the opening paragraph: "The worry I hear runs the other way: …", or lead with the reader:
"Plenty of people hear that and worry the other way: …" (if Ines is content that it's still phrased as
experience). Elsewhere, vary it where the referent is clear ("that fear", "it", or name the claim itself:
"that smarter means more dangerous"). Keep "the worry" where it's the subject of a finding.

### 7. Lynch: the appositive runs into the bound, and the bound becomes a deflation (Impact 5)

**Passage:** "This is the closest the evidence comes to the worry in its usual form, a capable AI choosing harm
to serve its own ends, and it took tests built to force it."

**What's wrong.** The appositive and the "and it" clause are joined by commas, so "and it took tests built to
force it" reads as part of the description of the worry. Its "it" can be read as the AI. The bound is
load-bearing (anti-overclaim) and stays whole. But tacked on with "and", it lands as a shrug at the end of the
sentence that grants the reader the most. The force should go on the grant, and the bound should stand on its
own.

**Fix.**

> This is the closest the evidence comes to the worry in its usual form: a capable AI choosing harm to serve
> its own ends. These were tests built to force that choice.

### 8. "carried out attacks better" makes the model the attacker; "My point is" restates the thesis inside the bounds (Impact 4)

**Passage:** "In one benchmark the more capable models carried out attacks better, and in stress tests built to
force it, capable models chose harm. My point is that access is the dial you can set, and the one that sets the
worst case."

**What's wrong.** This item is one of the reframe's best moves: a bound that refuses to call the worry
groundless. Two craft slips weaken it. "Carried out attacks" reads as the models attacking. The body says they
carried out an attacker's planted instructions. And "My point is …" is the only bound in the list that argues;
the others state a limit and stop. It's the fourth statement of the control thesis.

**Fix.** "In one benchmark the more capable models were better at carrying out instructions an attacker had
planted, and in stress tests built to force it, capable models chose harm." Then end the item, or keep the last
sentence only if Miriam wants the bound tied back to the thesis.

### 9. Smaller splice notes (Impact 3)

- **"and I'd say so" twice** (l.319, l.323) in a four-item list reads as a template. Drop it from the new item
  ("…then capability would be the dial to watch first.") or vary it.
- **Section title "What being smarter changes"** is a topic label in an essay whose other headings say
  something ("Same model, different worker"; "What it's allowed to touch sets the limit"). Option: "Smarter,
  but no harder to fool". It's the section's finding, from Debenedetti 2024 and Zou 2025, and it doesn't argue
  with the reader.
- **"how smart the next model is"** (l.22, l.335): "model" in the reader's control line is industry vocabulary.
  "the next version" carries it for someone who uses ChatGPT. Rosetta's call more than mine.
