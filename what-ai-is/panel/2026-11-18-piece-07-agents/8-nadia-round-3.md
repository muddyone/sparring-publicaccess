# Nadia Okonkwo, cold read, round 3: piece 07 (agents)

_Read once, straight through, as an everyday AI user on a phone. I use ChatGPT and Claude at work and at
home. I keep hearing agents will do things for me soon, and honestly, AI getting smarter scares me a bit.
I came in wanting to know what to watch for before I hand one a job. I read the post only, from the title
to the end of "Where I land" and the Notes, and skipped the HTML comment._

## The three questions

**1. Where did I stop or stall?**

I stalled twice and would really have left at the second one.

- **First stall, at the second paragraph under the bold position:** "which I separate below from the sense
  behind essay 03." I haven't read essay 03. I just learned this is part of a series I'm not caught up on,
  and I'm being told that a distinction is coming. I kept going, but now I was wary.
- **Second stall, where I'd really have gone: the second half of "Same model, different worker", and then
  "Two meanings of 'agent'".** The Messages API, "developer access", system prompts, "containers or VMs",
  11 versus 18 problems in every 100, "five times the computing power", then philosophers, Chalmers, a
  "quasi-subject", the Restatement of Agency, and Wooldridge & Jennings 1995. None of it is about my worry.
  It's about how the thing is built and what the word means, and I'm not a developer or a philosopher. On a
  phone I'd have thumbed down fast looking for the bit that's for me. Here, I picked it up again at the
  heading "What it's allowed to touch sets the limit", because it repeats the title's promise.

From there to the list I read without stopping. That's the best part of the essay, and it starts about a
third of the way down.

**2. After two lines, what did I think it was about?**

The title said: you're scared agents are getting too smart; here's a better question to ask. After the
first two lines of the bold paragraph I thought: "he's going to tell me my worry is a bit off, and give me
something I can actually check." That's what it delivers. But the bold paragraph is very long for a phone,
and by "How capable it is decides how close it comes to that limit, and how far it goes before anyone
looks decides how much goes wrong" I was juggling three ideas at once. "Being smarter doesn't make it any
harder to fool" also came at me before I knew anyone was trying to fool it. The last two sentences got me
back: "You don't control how smart the next model is. You do control what it can touch."

**3. Comment or scroll?**

I'd probably save it, and I might comment with a question rather than an argument: *"Okay, so where are
these settings in ChatGPT? How do I make it draft but not send?"* The list tells me what to want but not
where to find it in the tools I actually use. Then I'd share the Willison test with a friend, because
that's the part I'll remember.

## What I was asked to say specifically

**The title.** Yes, it made me want to read. It names my actual worry in my words, and "Ask what they can
touch" is a hook: concrete, a bit odd, and it promises a tool rather than a lecture. It made me expect a
short, practical piece. The piece is longer and more academic than that, and the practical part comes late.

**My worry: taken seriously or talked down to?** Taken seriously, more than I expected. "There's something
to it", "That is the worry, half right", and the part where more capable models are *easier* to attack all
tell me he isn't saying I'm silly. He gives me the blackmail study and doesn't hide it. What he doesn't do
is stay with that long enough. A couple of the findings made me *more* worried (1 in 30 hijack attempts
worked; 11 jumped to 81 in every 100; tasks doubling every seven months; blackmail 96 times out of 100), and
each time the essay moves on to its point about access before I've taken it in. I didn't feel talked down
to. I sometimes felt hurried past.

**Did I come away knowing what to do?** Mostly. The ideas are clear, and three of them really landed:
telling it "don't send anything" isn't a limit, only a setting is; let it draft and I press send; check the
file, not its "done". But some steps assume I can do things I don't know how to do: "Set those limits in
the tool's settings", "set it to stop and ask, in the tool's settings", "Start again when you know the model
or the setup has changed". I don't know where those settings are in ChatGPT or Claude, or whether they
exist. I never know when the model changes; it just updates. So I know what to want, and not yet how to get
it.

## Findings, ranked

**1. The essay loses me before it reaches my question.** Impact 9.
Passages: "Through the developer access that programmers build products on, the model remembers nothing
from one request to the next…" and the whole "Two meanings of 'agent'" section, for example "what David
Chalmers calls a quasi-subject" and "In 1995, two computer scientists separated a 'weak' notion of agency".
What happened: I skimmed fast and nearly left. Neither part answers "is this thing dangerous, and what do I
do?" A reader who came for the title has to get through a third of the essay on API details and word
meanings first.
Fix: Move "What it's allowed to touch sets the limit" and "What being smarter changes" up, straight after
the opening. Keep "same model, set up differently, is a different worker" as one short plain paragraph with
the coding-tool warning (it's the one example in there I could follow). Move the meanings of "agent" further
down or into a short aside. The ideas can stay; they just shouldn't stand between me and my question.

**2. The steps don't say where the controls are.** Impact 8.
Passages: "Set those limits in the tool's settings, not only in what you tell it." / "set it to stop and
ask, in the tool's settings" / "Start again when you know the model or the setup has changed".
What happened: I agreed, then got stuck. I use ChatGPT and Claude. Where is this? Does it exist for me? How
would I know the model changed?
Fix: Give one or two real examples of what this looks like for an ordinary user: what to look for when a
product asks to connect to your email or files, what "read only" or "ask before acting" looks like when it's
offered, and what to do when it isn't offered (which step 2 already half says: don't connect it). Say
plainly that models often change without telling you, so assume they have and re-check now and then.

**3. The scary findings go by too fast.** Impact 7.
Passages: "More than 60,000 of those attempts worked, about 1 in 30" / "rose from 11 in every 100 to 81" /
"it has doubled about every seven months since 2019" / "chose blackmail in 96 runs out of 100".
What happened: each one raised my worry, and the essay didn't tell me how to hold it. Is 1 in 30 a lot? If
an attacker keeps trying, is it a sure thing? Should doubling every seven months scare me?
Fix: After each one, a plain sentence of what it means for me. For example: "1 in 30 sounds small, but
anyone can try thirty times." Then the access point lands as an answer, not a change of subject.

**4. The opening bold paragraph is a wall on a phone, and the three-part idea is hard to hold.** Impact 6.
Passage: "What it's allowed to touch sets the limit on the harm it can do. How capable it is decides how
close it comes to that limit, and how far it goes before anyone looks decides how much goes wrong before
someone can stop it."
What happened: I had to slow down to keep three things straight, and the same three-part sentence comes
back twice more ("What an agent is allowed to touch sets the limit…" near the end of "What being smarter
changes", and again in spirit at "Where I land"). By the third time it felt like a refrain, not news.
Fix: Shorten the bold opening to the worry, the turn, and the last two sentences. Introduce the three
settings once, where they're explained, ideally as a short list of three.

**5. Series references assume I've read the others.** Impact 5.
Passages: "which I separate below from the sense behind essay 03" / "In essay 02 I called the model the
engine and the system the car" / "Part one's question applies here".
What happened: each one told me I'd missed something. The engine-and-car one I could follow on its own;
the essay 03 one I couldn't.
Fix: Keep a reference only when it explains itself in the same sentence, as the engine-and-car one does.
Drop the forward pointer to "the sense behind essay 03".

**6. "Fool" arrives before I know anyone's fooling it.** Impact 4.
Passage (bold opening): "being smarter doesn't make it any harder to fool."
What happened: I wondered who's fooling it. The answer (text it reads can carry instructions) is the most
eye-opening thing in the essay, and it's down in the third section.
Fix: Give it half a sentence up top, for example: "anything it reads can carry instructions, and being
smarter doesn't make it any harder to fool."

**7. The personal example is a developer's world.** Impact 3.
Passage: "I have several AI agents working on a project at once: one builds, one checks the builder's work,
one keeps the plan."
What happened: I can't picture that life, so it reads as "this is for people who code". But the line it
ends on is great: "The hold was a rule they follow, not a key they lack."
Fix: Keep it, but add a sentence that brings it home to me, the email-and-calendar user: telling your
assistant "ask me first" is a rule; switching off its ability to send is the key.

**What worked, so it doesn't get cut:** "An agent that can't send email can't leak your files by email,
however clever it is." The prompt injection paragraph. Willison's three-part test. "text, as we've just seen,
can be overridden by other text. A setting that takes away the ability to send is a limit." The Replit
database story. "Anyone who has clicked 'allow' for the twentieth time in an hour can guess." "Nobody calls
a spending limit an insult to the new hire." "'Done' is a claim, not a result." These are the essay.

## Verdict

Needs another round: the middle and the list are right, but a reader like me meets a third of the essay
before reaching my question, and the reordered opening should be read cold again.
