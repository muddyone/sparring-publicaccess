# Julian Crest: craft review, essay 07 (first panel read), 2026-11-18

_Substrate: Claude (subagent as Julian Crest). Draft `drafts/2026-11-18-07-agents.md` as first drafted (2026-11-18).
Read against `positions/00`, `02`, `03` and `06` for series fit, and brief 13's headings only. Consulted my
persona and memory (the async-content and nearest-antecedent entries; my 06 review's close and title
findings, which recur here). Did not read the options file (`positions-drafts/13-agents-options.md`), so

## Diagnosis

The argument is in good order, and the section sequence mostly works. The definition section hands off
cleanly into risk ("If an agent is defined by what you let it do, its risk is too"), and "Compared to
what?" already sits where my 06 review had to move it, right before the routine. Several sentences are
the best in Part two so far: "Nobody calls a spending limit an insult to the new hire"; "Anyone who has
clicked 'allow' for the twentieth time in an hour can guess"; "The agents are capable enough to publish
it. They aren't allowed to."

The craft problems are four. **The essay's thesis-in-a-scene is buried** near the end, while the opening
is a generality. **The access-not-intelligence beat is repeated about ten times**, so by the middle it
reads as a refrain the prose leans on, not a point it earns. **The reader's one rule comes in three
wordings and the close states none of them.** **The central image (limit, ceiling, reach, dial) is
mixed**, at exactly the sentence the position rests on.

Momentum rises through "Same model, different worker", dips at the unbridged turn into "Two meanings",
climbs through the risk sections to its peak at the Willison test and the incidents, sags in section 4's
overloaded second paragraph, recovers in "Compared to what?", and then drains through a recap close.

Measured: about 3,000 words of body; 17% of sentences under eight words (target about 30%). The prose
is connected and flows, so the ratio itself is not a finding; flow outranks it, per the brief.

## Ranked findings

### 1. The real opening is at the bottom of the essay (Impact 8; flag 7)

**Passage:** the opening, "Everyone in AI seems to be talking about "agents"." And, at the end of "What
I'd do with this": "Today one of them fixed broken links on a page of mine that anyone can read. … The
agents are capable enough to publish it. They aren't allowed to. That's the point."

**Problem.** The opening is a generality with a hedge in it ("seems"). It is the essay's weakest sentence
type: it could open any article on agents. Meanwhile the scene that *is* the position, capable but not
allowed, comes after six numbered steps, where it reads as an illustration of step 3. By then the reader
has the argument and the scene has nothing left to do. Every Part one essay has a moment the reader can
picture; 06 had none at the top and I flagged it. This essay has one and spends it late.

There's a timing defect too. "Today" is false by Friday whatever happens to the push, and the drafter's
note worries that the sentence breaks if the publish is approved. It doesn't need to. The claim is that
the agents *couldn't* publish it, and that stays true after Bart publishes it. Past tense fixes both.

**Fix.** Open on the scene, in past tense, and let the pitch paragraph follow it. Sample (no new facts;
the drafter rewrites):

> Earlier this week, one of the AI agents I run on my projects fixed some broken links on a page of
> mine that anyone can read. A second agent checked the fix. Neither of them could publish it. Changing
> what the public sees waits for me. They're capable enough to publish it. They aren't allowed to.
>
> Everyone in AI is talking about "agents". The pitch is that the assistant you chat with will soon
> book the trip, …

Then, at the bottom, keep the setup detail (one builds, one checks, one keeps the plan) as a short
callback tied to steps 1 and 3, not a second telling of the scene. Drop "That's the point." (see
finding 12). If Bart has a reason the public step waits for him (for example, that a page people have
already read can't be quietly unread), one sentence of it would give the scene its stakes. That reason
is his to state, so I'm not writing it in.

Is the example concrete enough? It's concrete about the arrangement and thin on stakes: the reader
doesn't learn what would go wrong if an agent could publish. The sentence above supplies that, if Bart
confirms it. Does it land the point? Yes, as an opener. At the bottom it lands a point already made.

### 2. The opening claims a rate the essay later says it doesn't have (Impact 7)

**Passage:** "But when an AI agent does real damage, the story is nearly always about what it was allowed
to do, not how intelligent it was."

**Problem.** "Nearly always" is a frequency. The essay's own limits contradict it twice: "These are
single reported cases, not rates" and "I found no study of how often agents cause harm in the field at
each level of access." A reader who remembers the opening will feel the later hedge as a retreat. It's
a craft failure (a sentence performing more confidence than it carries) as well as an argument one;
whether the claim holds is Miriam's and Vera's call.

**Fix.** Scope it to what was found, which is also more vivid: "But in the cases of real damage I could
find, the story was about what the agent was allowed to do, not how intelligent it was."

### 3. The access-not-intelligence beat repeats until it becomes a tic (Impact 7)

**Passage (in order):** "That's a role you give it, not a measure of how clever it is." · "not how
intelligent it was" · "It isn't about how smart the model is. It's about what it can reach." · "None of
these working definitions mentions intelligence." · "however clever it is" · "Every part of that test is
about access. None of it is about intelligence." · "Neither is a story about an agent being too smart." ·
"their advice is about access, not intelligence" · "separate from both access and intelligence" ·
"Neither starts by ranking the delegate's intelligence." · "rarely 'how intelligent was it?'"

**Problem.** About ten statements of the same contrast, most of them as the paragraph's last sentence.
Controlled repetition is a strength; this is past control. By the incident list the reader can predict
every paragraph's last line, so those lines stop landing. Three end with "Neither" or "None", which makes
the cadence audible. Two of them also do a reviewer's job for the reader. "their advice is about access,
not intelligence" glosses a quotation that already says so. "Neither is a story about an agent being too
smart" restates the sentence before it.

**Fix.** Keep four, the ones that do new work: the opening, the Claude Code warning ("It's about what it
can reach"), the Willison landing, and the close. Cut, each redundant with the sentence it follows:
- "Neither is a story about an agent being too smart." (redundant with "held access the job didn't need")
- "and their advice is about access, not intelligence:" → "and their advice is" (the quote carries it)
- "Neither starts by ranking the delegate's intelligence." (redundant with the next line, which says it
  better: "Nobody calls a spending limit an insult to the new hire.")
- "None of these working definitions mentions intelligence. They're about acting for someone." → "Each of
  these is about acting for someone." The contrast with intelligence is made two sentences later.

### 4. The reader's one rule has three wordings, and the close states none (Impact 7; flag 8)

**Passage:** position: "not 'how smart is it?' but 'what am I letting it do?'" · step 1: "Ask what it can
touch, not how smart it is." · title: "What you let it do is what counts." · close: "It's 'what did we let
it do, and who was looking?'"

**Problem.** The brief says the reader acts on one rule, "ask what it can touch, not how smart it is".
That wording appears once, as a step label. This is the same defect as 06's "believe the scatter" and
"believe the disagreement", and the fix is the same: one wording, everywhere it's the rule. The close's
"who was looking" is a good addition, but it's a second dial, not the rule.

**Fix.** Use "ask what it can touch, not how smart it is" in the position's last sentence and in the
close; leave step 1 as it is. Proposed close (every claim already in the body):

> An AI agent isn't a smarter assistant. It's an assistant you've let act for you, inside a setup someone
> chose: what it's told, what it can use, what it remembers, what it can touch, and how far it goes before
> anyone looks. Change the setup and you've changed the worker.
>
> So when an agent goes wrong, the useful question is rarely how intelligent it was. It's what we let it
> do, and who was looking. Before you hand one a job, ask the same thing in advance: what can it touch,
> not how smart is it. A smarter model will do more of whatever it's allowed to do, good and bad. The
> limit is yours to set. Set it as you would for a person in that job, and remember the two ways an agent
> is different. Anything it reads can talk it into things, and it moves faster than you can watch.

That keeps the current last line, which is the essay's best cadence, and puts the rule just before it.
If finding 1 is adopted, a last callback to the opening scene is available ("My agents could have
published that page. They weren't allowed to.") but I'd test it aloud first; it may be one beat too many.

### 5. "In essay 03 I said there really is an agent on the other end": essay 03 doesn't say that (Impact 6; flag 2, craft side)

**Passage:** "In essay 03 I said there really is an agent on the other end of the conversation."

**Problem.** Essay 03 says "there really is something on the other end" and calls it a "quasi-subject"
(03, l.8 and l.125). The words "there really is an agent on the other end" are the overview's summary of
03 (00, l.66). A reader who clicks through to 03 won't find the sentence being corrected. That matters
more here than anywhere, because this paragraph is the series correcting its own record. There's a
better version available, too: 03 itself uses "agent" in the working sense ("several AI agents, some
running on engines from different companies", 03, l.171–172). So the series really has used the word both
ways, and it can say so with the receipts.

**Fix.** Something like: "Here I have to clear up something this series itself muddied. The overview said,
following Chalmers, that there really is an agent on the other end of the conversation, and essay 03
described it as something that can accurately be said to have beliefs and aims. That's the philosopher's
sense. Essay 03 also called the systems I work in 'several AI agents', which is the other sense." Miriam
and Rosetta own whether the correction is fair to 03; this is only so it points at words that exist.

### 6. The central image is mixed at the sentence the position rests on (Impact 6; flag 4, craft side)

**Passage:** position: "set the limit on the harm it can do. How capable it is decides how much of that
limit it reaches". Section 4: "What an agent is allowed to touch sets the ceiling on the harm it can do.
How capable it is decides how much of that ceiling it reaches." Section 5: "There's a second dial".

**Problem.** "How much of a ceiling it reaches" doesn't make a picture: you reach a ceiling or you don't.
"Limit" and "ceiling" alternate for the same idea, and "a second dial" implies a first dial that was never
called one. Add engine, car, keys and worker, and the essay asks the reader to hold six images. The worker
and the car earn their place (the car is a callback to 02). The ceiling is the one the argument needs, and
it's the one that doesn't quite work.

**Fix.** Pick "ceiling" and give it a verb that fits: "sets the ceiling on the harm it can do. How capable
it is decides how close it gets to that ceiling." Use the same wording in the position. For "a second
dial", use the literal word the essay has already introduced: "There's a second setting, separate from
both access and intelligence". The Claude Code passage set up "one setting" in section 1, so "setting" is a
callback, not a new image.

### 7. The "done" moment is the essay's most recognisable experience, told in the abstract (Impact 6; additive)

**Passage:** "I've had tools tell me a job succeeded when it hadn't happened at all. The report said
"done". The work wasn't there."

**Problem.** Nearly every reader has had a version of this, and it's the bridge to step 5 ("Check the
work, not the report"). As written it's a category, not a moment. 06's best passage was the same kind of
thing made specific: four quotations, searched for word for word in the PDF, none there. The brief rules
out a figure here, rightly, but a figure isn't what's missing. What's missing is what the job was.

**Fix (additive, one or two sentences).** The drafter asks Bart for one instance: what the agent said it
had done, and what he found when he looked. No count, no record reference. I won't invent it. The
reader gains a picture of step 5 before being asked to act on it.

### 8. The seam into "Two meanings" has no bridge (Impact 5)

**Passage:** end of section 1: "Two people using the same model can be working with two quite different
workers." Start of section 2: "Here I have to clear up something this series itself muddied."

**Problem.** Section 1 ends on "workers"; section 2 opens on the series' history with no line between.
The reader has to guess why we've switched from setups to vocabulary. Inside the section, the Chalmers
line is introduced as fitting "this essay's car", but the car is essay 02's, and the quote's point (one
engine, many agents) is the harness point, not the philosopher's-sense point the paragraph is making.

**Fix.** One bridging sentence at the head of section 2: "The industry's name for a worker like this is
an 'agent', and here I have to clear up something this series itself muddied." For Chalmers: "fits the
engine-and-car picture from essay 02".

### 9. Section 4's second paragraph carries three moves and ends on the weakest (Impact 5)

**Passage:** "That doesn't make capability irrelevant. It cuts both ways … The same permission lets a more
capable agent accomplish more."

**Problem.** The paragraph opens on "both ways", drops the heaviest claim in the essay (leading models
choosing blackmail under stress tests) into its middle, then pivots to task length, and lands on a flat
sentence whose "more" could mean more good or more harm. The blackmail finding needs room to be read
carefully, with its "not seen … in real deployments" bound right beside it. In the middle of a paragraph
it gets neither.

**Fix.** Split into three: (a) the both-ways claim and the Kwa doubling (the good side); (b) the Lynch
stress tests on their own, with their bound and their advice; (c) the ceiling paragraph. End (a) on what
the doubling means for the reader: "The same permission, given to a more capable agent, gets more done."
That adds no words to speak of, and the alarming finding stops being a clause.

### 10. "Compared to what?" opens with 06's sentence word for word (Impact 5)

**Passage:** "Part one kept asking one question, and it applies here too: compared to what?" And the
section's shape: "Two differences cut against the AI agent … One difference cuts in its favour."

**Problem.** The first sentence is identical to 06, l.159, in the very next edition, and the against/for
pattern repeats 06's ("the comparison isn't all in the AI's favor … One difference does cut in the AI's
favor"). Readers of a series notice a template sooner than any single reader would. The "By 'it' I mean"
paragraph is a deliberate series convention and is fine; this isn't. Separately, "favour" and "defences"
are British; the series is American ("favor" in 01 and 06, "behavior" throughout).

**Fix.** Open from where the previous section ended (an agent saying "done"): "None of this is new
with AI. Part one's question applies here: compared to what? Whoever would otherwise do the job is a
person…" Keep the against/for content, but vary the frame: "Two things make an agent harder to delegate to
than a person… One makes it easier." Spell "favor", "defenses".

### 11. Two sentences claim a closer match than their sources give (Impact 5; route to Miriam)

**Passages:** "It made the agent safe without making the model any harder to fool." · "A legal scholar …
found its usual remedies strained for exactly these reasons: agents "that make uninterpretable decisions
and operate at unprecedented speed and scale""

**Problem.** The first is a good line structurally, but "safe" outruns "stopped the attacks they tested".
In the second, "exactly these reasons" claims the quote matches both differences just named. It names speed
and scale (the second) and adds a reason the essay didn't give (uninterpretable decisions); it says nothing
about text redirecting an agent (the first).

**Fix.** "It made the agent safe against those attacks without making the model any harder to fool." ·
"found its usual remedies strained for reasons that include the second:". Whether each source supports
even that is the cite-check's.

### 12. Small voice tells (Impact 4)

- "Notice what kind of warning that is." and "The reason is uncomfortable:" both tell the reader how to take
  the point. Bart states what a thing does and leaves the reaction to the reader (voice profile, the
  "doesn't tell the reader how to take it" note). Cut "Notice …"; the next two sentences make the point.
  "The reason is uncomfortable:" → "The reason:" or fold it: "Weaker models, it turns out, often fail …".
- "So here's how I'd put it." is throat-clearing before a restatement (the "Here's …" turn I flagged in 06).
  Cut; start with "What an agent is allowed to touch sets the ceiling…"
- "and that's my point" (section 4) and "That's the point." (worked example): two "point" landings. Cut both;
  the sentences before them land without help.
- "Everyone in AI seems to be talking" (handled by finding 1; if the opening stays, drop "seems").

### 13. Smaller items (Impact 2–3)

- "Before you let an AI act for you, in practice I'd suggest this:" The word order buries "in practice". →
  "In practice, before you let an AI act for you, I'd suggest this:"
- Step 4: "that's a sign it's asking about too much, or should be allowed less." The two halves sound like
  two remedies and are one. → "that's a sign it can do too much, so its requests have stopped meaning
  anything." (Rosetta may have a plainer version.)
- "The split is old: two computer scientists separated…" earns its sentence (it shows the distinction isn't
  ours), but "back in 1995" at the end of a long sentence lands on the date; move the date forward ("In 1995,
  two computer scientists…").

## Flag verdicts

**Flag 1, length.** Every section earns its place; I'd cut no section. The cuts in finding 3 save roughly
60 words, and the additions in findings 1, 7 and 9 put back about as many, so the essay stays near 3,000,
inside the series range. Under-explained: the "done" moment (finding 7) and the stakes of the worked
example (finding 1). The Lynch finding needs space more than words (finding 9). Nothing here should be
cut to bring the count down.

**Flag 7, the worked example.** Concrete about the arrangement, thin on stakes, and in the wrong place. Move
it to the opening in past tense (finding 1). Past tense also settles the timing note: "Neither could
publish it" stays true after Bart publishes, and "Today" goes. One sentence of why the public step waits
for him, in his words, would give it stakes.

**Flag 8, the title.** "A smarter AI isn't a safer one. What you let it do is what counts." Three problems.
(1) It never says "agent", in the essay the overview promised as "What is an AI agent?", for a reader who
keeps hearing the word. (2) "What you let it do is what counts" is a "what … is what" construction, which
is loose, and "counts" leaves the reader asking counts for what. (3) Its second beat is a third wording of
the rule (finding 4). It also repeats the section 4 heading almost word for word. **Proposed:** "A smarter
agent isn't a safer one. Ask what it can touch." (10 words, the same two-beat shape as 06.) The title
carries half the rule and the close carries the whole of it. **Alternative:** "Before you let an AI act for
you, ask what it can touch." Avoid any version without "agent" or "act for you" in it.

## Best and weakest

- **Best:** "We already know how to hand authority to someone whose judgment we can't fully check. We scope it
  to the job." through "Nobody calls a spending limit an insult to the new hire." It's the essay's argument
  from the reader's own life, and the last line earns its shortness.
- **Weakest:** "So here's how I'd put it." It announces the sentence that matters most instead of saying it.

## Verdict

**Ready after fixes.** One structural move (the scene to the top, finding 1), one detail only Bart can
supply (finding 7), and a rewritten close (finding 4). None of them reopens the position.
