# Cold read: essay 06, "Same question, different answer" (Nadia Okonkwo), 2026-11-14

Read once, straight through, as an everyday AI user on a phone. I have not read the series, brief 12, or any
other background. Where I say "I stalled", I mean I stopped and went back.

## What I took away

When ChatGPT gives me two different answers to the same question, that isn't proof it's broken. It's a clue.
If I ask again in a new chat and the answers don't match, the AI is probably guessing, so I should check before
using either one. If they do match, that's a good sign but not proof, because AI models can be wrong the same
way every time, and different AIs are often wrong in the same way too. Arguing with it doesn't help, because it
caves and tells me what I want to hear. Judging by how confident it sounds doesn't help either. What helps is
checking the answer against something outside the conversation: the actual source, a calculation, another AI
told to find what's wrong, or a different AI altogether. Experts disagree with themselves too, so this isn't
only an AI problem.

That's a useful message and I'd act on it. My first worry after two lines: the title is a sentence and a half,
and I didn't know what "it" was in "check it against something new". The second worry came in the middle. On a
phone I'd have started skimming at "Trusting how it sounds", which piles six studies into one paragraph. I read
on for the panel; a real reader might not.

**Would I comment?** Yes, with a question: "How different do the answers have to be before I count them as
'scattered'? It never words the same thing the same way twice." The essay doesn't answer that, and I need the
answer before I can do step 2.

## Findings, ranked

### 1. Nobody tells me what counts as "scatter", and that's the thing I'm supposed to act on

> "If the answers scatter, treat the subject as one where the model may be guessing."

> "2. **If the answers scatter, believe the scatter.**"

**What went wrong.** Every answer I get from an AI is worded differently. I can't tell whether different
wording counts as scatter, or whether it only counts when the facts or the recommendation change. The essay
touches on this once ("disagree in *meaning*, not just in wording") but leaves it inside a description of a
*Nature* study, not addressed to me. As a reader I'd either see scatter everywhere (and panic) or nowhere (and
learn nothing from the test).

**Fix.** Add two or three plain sentences where the free test is introduced, and repeat the point in step 2.
Something like: different words don't count; a different fact, number, name, yes/no, or recommendation does.
Compare what the two answers *claim*, not how they say it. This is the essay's main instruction, and it needs
more words.

### 2. The title and the closing line point "it" at the wrong thing

> "Same question, different answer. Believe the disagreement, then check it against something new."

> "So ask twice. Believe the disagreement. Then check it against something new."

**What went wrong.** Grammatically, "it" is the disagreement. I read it that way first and got stuck: how do
I check a disagreement against something? You mean check the *answer*. And "believe the disagreement" made me
think, for half a second, "believe the AI when it disagrees with me", which is the opposite of the advice in the
arguing section. These are the two lines I'm meant to remember, so they can't be ambiguous.

**Fix.** Name the answer: "...then check the answer against something it didn't have." Say whose disagreement
it is the first time: "when its answers disagree with each other, believe that."

### 3. "17.9 percentage points" (and "15%", and "76 points") don't tell me how big anything is

> "On a set of grade-school maths word problems, accuracy rose by 17.9 percentage points"

> "Their accuracy still varied by up to 15% from run to run"

> "moved one open model's accuracy by as much as 76 points"

**What went wrong.** Three numbers, three different units ("percentage points", "%", "points"), and none says
from what to what. 17.9 points from 20% to 38% is a different story from 80% to 98%. "Percentage points" is not
a unit I use; "15%" next to it made me wonder whether that was 15 *percent of* something or 15 points. The
decimal (17.9) reads as precision I can't use. I also didn't know how many times they asked to get that
improvement. It matters, because the routine tells me to ask *twice*, and with two answers there's no "most
common answer" to pick.

**Fix.** Use one convention throughout, on a scale I know: "out of every 100 problems, it got about 18 more
right." Give the before and after if brief 12 has them. Say roughly how many answers were pooled, and add a
line saying that ordinary users won't be doing a vote of many answers. This study shows that the spread carries
signal, and two answers are the cheap version of the same idea. Same treatment for the 15 and the 76 (out of
100? from what?).

### 4. The 46% shows up before it's been introduced

> "models asked "are you sure?" changed their answer 46% of the time, while those examiners changed about one
> in ten on a plain retest, with nobody pushing back and on a different task. So the comparison is loose."

**What went wrong.** This is in "Compared to what?". The "are you sure?" study isn't introduced until two
sections later, so I met 46% with no context, then met it again later and went back to check it was the same
number. And the sentence knocks down its own comparison in the same breath ("nobody pushing back... different
task... loose"). By the end I didn't know whether I was supposed to believe the AI was easier to talk round or
not.

**Fix.** Either move the "are you sure?" finding ahead of this section, or say here in a clause what it is
("in one study, when people simply asked 'are you sure?', models changed their answer 46% of the time").
Then say the point plainly, without the hedges on top: the examiners weren't being pushed at all, so we can't
compare the two directly. What we do know is that the AI caves easily under a push. Keep the honesty, but give
it a sentence of its own after the claim.

### 5. The mammogram sentence needs re-reading

> "the share of cancer-free women each one recommended for immediate workup ranged from 11% to 65%"

**What went wrong.** I read it twice. "Share of cancer-free women each one recommended" is three ideas packed
into one noun phrase, and "immediate workup" is medical shorthand.

**Fix.** Unpack it: "Among women who turned out not to have cancer, one radiologist sent 11 in 100 for more
tests straight away; another sent 65 in 100." That's the striking version, and right now it's buried.

### 6. "Trusting how it sounds" is where I'd have stopped on a phone, and half of it isn't about how it sounds

> "**Trusting how it sounds.** Asked how sure they are, models tend to be overconfident (...). Longer
> explanations made people more confident (...). Clever wording doesn't help either. Telling a model "you are
> an expert" didn't improve its factual answers (...). Politeness, tips and threats helped on some questions
> and hurt on others, and tipping or threatening a model had no significant effect overall (...). And small
> changes of format ... moved one open model's accuracy by as much as 76 points"

**What went wrong.** Seven citations in one paragraph. The heading promised "how it sounds", which is about me
judging the *answer's* tone. Then it switches to me wording my *question* cleverly ("you are an expert", tips,
threats), which is a different habit. The section opens "Two habits feel like checking", and this paragraph has
three. "Tipping or threatening a model" stopped me cold. Who tips a model? I've never heard of it, and the essay
doesn't say why anyone would. "No significant effect" is lab language. "One open model" is jargon; I don't know
what makes a model "open".

**Fix.** Split it into its own short item. Habit one: trusting a confident, long, polished answer (overconfidence
and length). Habit two: thinking the right magic words in your question will make the answer reliable (expert
role, politeness, tips, format). Give the tips/threats line half a sentence of why people try it ("some people
online claim offering a model a tip gets better answers"), but only if brief 12 supports that framing. Say
"made no real difference" instead of "no significant effect". Then the heading "Two habits" becomes three
habits, or the second one moves under its own heading. This needs more words, not fewer, so that each habit
lands.

### 7. "A reviewer with a different job": I couldn't tell who the reviewers were, and one result seems to cut against the essay

> "A reviewer told to attack it caught 87% of the serious flaws. Claude checking its own work caught 68%, and a
> neutral fresh reviewer caught 55% ... On GPT's work the self-review gap nearly vanished"

**What went wrong.** Are the reviewers AIs or people? Which AI? I assumed AI, but the essay never says.
"Self-review gap" made me go back: the gap between which two numbers? And as a reader I noticed something the
essay doesn't address. The neutral fresh reviewer *is* "something new", and it did *worse* than Claude checking
itself, which "brings nothing new". So the lesson is really about the job you give the check, not its newness.
That fits the heading ("a different job"), but it rubs against the closing claim that "what separates the checks
that work... is how independent they are."

**Fix.** Say "another AI, told to attack it" and "another AI, asked simply to review it". Replace "self-review
gap" with what it means: "on work GPT had produced, the attacker barely beat GPT checking itself". Add one
sentence that faces the 55% head-on: a fresh pair of eyes isn't enough on its own; it needs a job, here finding
what's wrong. Then check that "Where I land" says *new and given a job*, not just *independent*.

### 8. "A different model" — I can't act on it

> "Because models share errors, this helps most when the second model really is different."

**What went wrong.** How would I know? Earlier I was told that big models from *different companies* share
errors. So "really is different" means... what, to me with ChatGPT and Claude open in two tabs? The step-4 advice
("Ask a different model") goes back to being simple, so the caveat sits on its own.

**Fix.** One honest sentence: a model from another company is the easy version, it helps, but it's the weakest
of the four checks because they often go wrong in the same places, so use a source or a calculation when one is
available. If that ranking is stronger than the evidence, say only the part the evidence supports. Either way,
tell me what I'm supposed to do.

### 9. "In a fresh conversation" isn't explained

> "Ask the question again, in a fresh conversation."

**What went wrong.** I followed the instruction, but I don't know *why* fresh. A reader who skips it and asks
"again?" in the same chat gets a worse test and won't know it.

**Fix.** Half a sentence: in the same chat, it can see its first answer and tends to stick with it, so you
aren't getting a second draw. (Only if the brief supports it. If not, at least say "so it can't see its first
answer".)

### 10. "Pushing too often" parses wrong

> "So an answer you got by pushing too often just agrees with you."

**What went wrong.** I first read it as "if you push *too often*", meaning pushing a lot is the problem. The
intended reading is "an answer you got by pushing, too often, is just agreement".

**Fix.** "So when you push back and it changes its answer, it's often just agreeing with you."

### 11. The Kim 60% has no yardstick

> "when two of them both got a question wrong, they gave the same wrong answer about 60% of the time on one
> benchmark"

**What went wrong.** Is 60% a lot? If the questions were multiple choice with four options, two wrong models
would land on the same wrong option some of the time by pure chance. I can't judge 60% without knowing what
chance would give.

**Fix.** If brief 12 says what chance agreement would be, say it ("by luck alone you'd expect about X"). If it
doesn't, say what kind of questions they were. Don't add a source for it.

### 12. Small stalls for a reader without the series

> "it's worth asking the question Part one kept asking"

> "By "it" I mean what the rest of this series means: ... or a fuller setup in which an AI works on its own,
> with files and tools."

> "five models set to be deterministic" · "80 different completions" · "one open model" · "developer access"

**What went wrong.** "Part one" means nothing to me arriving from a link. The "By 'it' I mean" paragraph
interrupts the opening just as it gets going, and the "fuller setup" part never comes up again, so I wondered
what I'd missed. "Deterministic", "completions", "open model" are each a small speed bump. "Developer access" I
mostly got.

**Fix.** "A question I keep asking in this series: compared to what?" Move the "by 'it' I mean" line to the
notes, or cut it down to the assistant half. Plain swaps: "set to give the same answer every time", "80
different answers", "a freely available model", "the versions companies sell to developers".

### 13. The draft banner and the bracketed placeholder

> own cite-check: to come.]_"

I'm assuming these come out before it ships. If either goes out, it reads as internal paperwork.

## Verdicts on the drafter's flags (my lane)

**Flag 1, length / under-explained: yes, under-explained, and in the places that matter most to me as the
person doing the routine.** It's not too short overall. It's thin exactly where I have to *act*: what counts as
scatter (finding 1), why a fresh chat (9), what a "different model" means in practice (8), who the reviewers
were (7). It's also thin on the size of the numbers (3, 5, 11). Meanwhile "Trusting how it sounds" (6) is the
one place that's overstuffed, and the answer there is to spread it out, not cut it. My guess is it needs a few
hundred more words, mostly in the test, the routine, and the four checks. Nothing I flagged should be cut.

**Flag 4, title: too long, and the second sentence is ambiguous** (finding 2). "Same question, different
answer" works as a title on its own; it's exactly what I've noticed in my own use, which is why I'd click. Put
the rule underneath as a subtitle, with the "it" fixed: "When its answers disagree, believe that. Then check the
answer against something it didn't have."

**Flag 5, "17.9 percentage points": not usable as written** (finding 3). No starting point, an unfamiliar unit,
false-precision decimal, no sense of how many answers were pooled, and a tension with "ask twice" that the
essay doesn't address. Make it "about 18 more right out of every 100", give before and after if the brief has
them, and say it took many answers, not two.

## Verdict

**Needs another round.** The argument is sound and the routine is usable. But the core instruction ("if the
answers scatter") is undefined, the takeaway line is ambiguous, and the numbers that are meant to persuade
don't say what they count. A reader should see those fixed before the polish pass.
