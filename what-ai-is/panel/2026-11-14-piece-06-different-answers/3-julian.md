# Julian: craft, essay 06 "Same question, different answer" (first review)

_Substrate: Claude (subagent as Julian Crest). Draft at 0d6ae416, 2026-11-14. Read against
`positions/01-the-yardstick.md` and `positions/02-it-learns.md` for series fit. Consulted my
persona and memory (the close-check entries: name what generates a close's pull; a close needs a
turn, not a recap). Did not read the other four reports._

## Diagnosis

The argument is in good order and the sentences are mostly clean. Several of them are very good:
"I think that reading gets the facts right and the conclusion wrong"; "No expert offers that";
"Don't pick the answer you like better. Slow down and check." The craft problems are about
architecture, not lines. **One section sits in the wrong place**, so a promised handoff breaks
and the section uses two pieces of evidence the reader hasn't met yet. **The essay has no scene.**
Every Part one essay has a moment the reader can picture (Gopnik on a podcast, the nickname test,
H.M. tracing a star, the teapot). This one is all mechanism, and for a "working with it" essay
that is the main thing it lacks. **The close recaps instead of turning**, and the title spends
the close's last line before the reader gets there. **The one rule the reader will act on comes
in two wordings** ("believe the scatter" and "believe the disagreement").

Momentum rises through "Why the answers differ", peaks at "Agreement isn't proof", stalls at the
"Compared to what?" detour, drops through the citation run under "Trusting how it sounds", and
recovers in "What does". It then drains through two hedging sections stacked before the close.

## Ranked edits

### 1. "Compared to what?" is in the wrong place (Impact 8)

**Passage:** "So agreement is evidence. … That's where the second half of my position comes in."
followed by "## Compared to what? / Before going further, it's worth asking the question Part one
kept asking…"

**Problem.** Three things go wrong at once.
- The handoff makes a promise ("the second half of my position") and the next section breaks it.
  The second half (check against something new) arrives two sections later.
- The section rests on evidence the reader hasn't met. "Its tone tells you nothing about whether
  it's right" comes before "Trusting how it sounds". "Models asked 'are you sure?' changed their
  answer 46% of the time" comes before "Arguing with it", where the same 46% is introduced a
  second time as if new.
- "Before going further, it's worth asking" is throat-clearing. It announces a detour, and it
  reads as one.

**Fix.** Move the whole section to sit **after "What does: a check that brings in something
new" and before "What I'd do with this"**. There it stops being a detour and becomes the payoff.
"They build in second readers, panels and checklists" is the human version of the essay's
independence argument, and it lands right after "A check that brings nothing new mostly hands you
the same answer back." The tone and 46% points become callbacks to evidence already given, so the
46% is introduced once. And the section's last beat ("you can ask it ten times, independently, for
almost nothing. No expert offers that.") hands straight to step 1 of the routine ("ask twice. It
costs a minute."). That is the best transition the essay could have, and the current order wastes
it.

**Rewrites the move needs** (no new claims):
- Section opener. Current: "Before going further, it's worth asking the question Part one kept
  asking: compared to what? People who do judgment work for a living vary too." Proposed: "Varying
  from one answer to the next isn't peculiar to AI. Part one kept asking 'compared to what?', and the answer here is that
  people who do judgment work for a living vary too."
- The 46% clause, now a callback. Current: "models asked "are you sure?" changed their answer 46%
  of the time, while those examiners…" Proposed: "models changed their answer under "are you sure?"
  46% of the time, as above, while those examiners…" (Miriam and Vera own whether the comparison
  is fair. This only stops it being introduced twice.)
- The handoff left behind in "Agreement isn't proof". Current: "That's where the second half of my
  position comes in." Proposed: "That's where the second half of my position comes in: what
  actually makes an answer more reliable, and what only feels as if it does." Now it is true of
  the section that follows.

Fallback, if Bart wants the comparison early: put it right after "Why the answers differ" (the
facts are right; experts vary too) and move both the tone and the 46% points into "Arguing with
it". That is a weaker version of the same fix. Leaving it where it is is not an option, because
the broken handoff and the double introduction stay.

### 2. The essay has no scene, which is where it most needs MORE (Impact 8; flag 1)

**Passage:** the hook, "Ask an AI assistant the same question twice and you may get two different
answers. Most people meet this early…", and the routine in "What I'd do with this".

**Problem.** The reader is someone who has *noticed* this happen and wants to know what to do. The
essay never shows it happening. Part one's openings are specific (02 opens on a named podcast
moment in May 2026). This one opens on a generality about "most people". Authorial presence
appears only in the study passages. The routine is five abstract imperatives the reader never sees
applied, so the advice the essay most wants acted on gets the least picture.

**Fix (additive, about 150–250 words).** The drafter asks Bart for a real instance from his own
daily use: one question that gave two different answers, and what he did next. That becomes the
opening paragraph, ahead of "Ask an AI assistant…", in first person. If he has none to hand, use a
clearly **hypothetical** worked example at the end of "What I'd do with this" ("Say you ask…"),
running one ordinary question through steps 1–4. Framed as hypothetical, it adds no factual
claim. I am not writing either version here, because the first has to be Bart's own experience
and I won't invent it. This is the single largest gain available.

### 3. The close recaps; it should turn, then hand over the rule (Impact 8)

**Passage:** "## Where I land" in full, ending "So ask twice. Believe the disagreement. Then check
it against something new."

**Problem.** Paragraph one restates the first two sections. Paragraph two restates "What does".
The last line restates the title. Nothing new happens, so the ending gestures instead of landing.
Both Part one closers *turn*: "Stop asking whether it is reliable software. Ask how it compares…"
and "So stop asking. Ask whether it can find out what nobody has told it." Each swaps the reader's
starting question for a better one. This essay's turn is in its opening ("gets the facts right and
the conclusion wrong") and never comes back. The reader starts out believing that a different
answer is a reason to stop trusting it. The close should reverse that belief in so many words, and
then give the rule as the payoff. The essay's sharpest available line is also buried mid-paragraph:
"or arguing it into agreement, brings in nothing". Pushing back brings in *you*, which is the
sycophancy finding, and it is the better line.

**Proposed** (every claim is already in the body):

> An AI answer isn't a lookup. It's one draw from a range, and the range is wider on some
> questions than on others. That's what makes the second answer useful. When the draws disagree,
> you've been warned, for free. When they agree, you've learned something, but not enough to stake
> much on.
>
> What makes an answer trustworthy is what it has been checked against: a source, a tool, a
> reviewer with a real job, a different model. Each brings in something the answer didn't have.
> Asking the same model again brings in nothing new. Arguing it into agreement brings in only you.
>
> So the different answer was never the reason to stop trusting it. It's the first check you get.
> Believe the disagreement, then check it against something new.

The last line keeps the rule the reader acts on as the essay's final words, exactly as the brief
names it. The line before it is the turn that earns it.

### 4. The reader's one rule has two wordings (Impact 7)

**Passages:** the position, "believe the scatter: it's a warning"; step 2, "**If the answers
scatter, believe the scatter.**"; the title and close, "Believe the disagreement".

**Problem.** The brief says the reader will act on one rule of thumb: "believe the disagreement,
then check it against something new". The essay gives it in two forms. A rule the reader is meant
to carry out of the room has to be the same words every time it appears. Otherwise the reader
wonders whether scatter and disagreement are two different things.

**Fix.** Keep "scatter" as description ("If the answers scatter…"). Make the imperative
"disagreement" everywhere. Position: "When you ask again and the answers scatter, believe the
disagreement: it's a warning." Step 2: "**If the answers scatter, believe the disagreement.**"

### 5. "Two habits" are three, and the citation run has no landing of its own (Impact 6)

**Passage:** "Two habits feel like checking and aren't." then "**Trusting how it sounds.**", a
paragraph that runs from overconfidence and length (how it *sounds*) into "Clever wording doesn't
help either…" (expert personas, politeness, tips, threats, format changes). These are about how
*you ask*, not how it sounds.

**Problem.** The paragraph changes subject halfway and doesn't say so, so it becomes the essay's
longest stretch of stacked citations: seven sources in eight sentences. This is where momentum drops
furthest. Its closing line, "An answer's polish and its accuracy are different things", belongs to
the first half only.

**Fix.** Split it into three named habits:
- "Two habits feel like checking and aren't." → "Three habits feel as if they help, and don't."
- End **Trusting how it sounds** after Steyvers, with its own landing moved up: "An answer's
  polish and its accuracy are different things."
- New lead: "**Dressing up the question.** Telling a model "you are an expert" didn't improve its
  factual answers (…). Politeness, tips and threats… (…). And small changes of format… 76 points
  (…). How you word the question can move the answer, but not reliably toward the right one."
  (That landing sentence summarises the cited results and adds nothing beyond them. Vera should
  confirm it doesn't overreach Sclar.)
- **Arguing with it** stays third.

### 6. Two hedging sections stacked in front of the close (Impact 6)

**Passage:** "## What this doesn't claim" followed immediately by "## What would change my mind",
then "## Where I land".

**Problem.** Part one essays carry their limits inline and go from "What would change my mind"
straight to the close. Here the reader goes from the routine through six bullets of limits before
the landing, and the close has to climb out of a discount stack. One bullet is also redundant with
another on the same reader's path. "That people who check more actually decide better. I found no
field study…" and the third change-my-mind bullet ("If field studies found that people who ask
twice…") are the same gap, said twice. That is the valid cut test: redundant with X, where X is
four lines later.

**Fix.** These limits bear on the *advice*, so put them next to the advice. Fold "What this
doesn't claim" into the end of "What I'd do with this", right after "Match the effort to what's at
stake", as a short paragraph: "Two cautions about all of this. Most of the evidence comes from
benchmarks run through the companies' developer access, not the chat apps you use, and the
companies don't publish their apps' settings. And several studies used 2023 models: they show the
shape of the problem, not today's rate." Move the field-study gap into the change-my-mind bullet,
which then opens: "**No field study has yet tested whether people who ask twice and check sources
decide better.** If one found they don't…" No limit is weakened or dropped. Each is said once, in
the place where it bears on the reader.

### 7. The 17.9-point sentence ends a paragraph on a bare number (Impact 5; flag 5, craft side)

**Passage:** "The same idea works the other way: ask several times and take the most common answer,
and accuracy goes up. On a set of grade-school maths word problems, accuracy rose by 17.9
percentage points (Wang et al. 2023)."

**Problem.** "Works the other way" is the wrong hinge. It is the same signal used to *pick* an
answer, not an opposite. And the paragraph stops on a figure, against the brief's register target
of the finding first, then what it means in plain words. (Whether 17.9 points is usable as written
is Vera's and Rosetta's call. My point is that nothing after it tells the reader what it shows.)

**Fix.** "The same signal can also pick the answer. Ask several times and take the most common
answer, and accuracy goes up: on a set of grade-school math word problems, by 17.9 percentage
points (…). Put simply, the answer the draws agree on is more often the right one." The last
sentence restates what majority voting showing gains means, and adds nothing beyond it.

### 8. "Here's…" turns and a "most people" tic (Impact 5)

**Passages:** "Here's the part most people miss." / "Here's the common thread in the checks that do
work." / "Most people meet this early, and most people read it the same way".

**Problem.** "Here's the part most people miss" is the "the real point is…" turn I flag on
sight. It also tells the reader they are among those who missed it, a small correction of the
reader right after the hook has already told them their reading is wrong. "Most people" appears
three times in the first 40 lines. Whether "most people" read it that way is a claim, and
Miriam's to rule on. The repetition is mine.

**Fix.** Open the section from where the last one ended, as the brief asks. Current: "Here's the
part most people miss. **How much the answers vary depends on the question, and that variation
tells you something.**" Proposed: "So you can't count on the same answer twice. What you can count
on is that **how much the answers vary depends on the question, and that variation tells you
something.**" (Then cut the previous paragraph's identical closing clause: "But the point stands:
you can't count on the same answer twice." becomes "But the point stands.") For the second
"Here's": "The checks that do work have one thing in common. **Each one brings in something the
first answer didn't have.**"

### 9. A garden-path sentence (Impact 5)

**Passage:** "So an answer you got by pushing too often just agrees with you."

**Problem.** It parses as "pushing too often" (pushing too much) until the reader backs up. It is
the weakest sentence in the essay, and it closes the paragraph that sets up Bart's own study.

**Fix.** "So when you push back and it gives way, the new answer is often just agreeing with you."

### 10. Smaller craft items (Impact 3–4)

- **The "By 'it' I mean" paragraph breaks the hook's handoff** (Impact 3). "And what you do next
  matters far more…" should run straight into the first section. 01 puts this definition directly
  after the position block. Move it there, above "Ask an AI assistant…".
- **The reviewer bullet ends on its qualifier** (Impact 4). The landing sentence "The job you give
  the check matters." comes before "On GPT's work the self-review gap nearly vanished…". Proposed
  order: the results, then "On GPT's work the self-review gap nearly vanished, so how much a
  separate challenger adds depends on the model. Either way, the job you give the check matters."
- **The section closer brings in a new abstraction at the last moment** (Impact 3). "It's how
  independent they are" swaps "something new" for "independent", in an "isn't X, it's Y" turn.
  Proposed: "What separates the checks that work from the ones that don't isn't how many there
  are. It's whether each one brings in something new. A check that brings nothing new mostly hands
  you the same answer back."
- **"In practice, I'd suggest this:"** is throat-clearing (Impact 3). Cut it; the heading "What
  I'd do with this" already says it.
- **Step 5's landing is soft** (Impact 3). "Neither tells you much." → "Neither one is a check."
  This echoes "feel like checking and aren't", and it is the essay's own argument, not a new claim.
- **The "challenger" in Bart's study is never pictured** (Impact 4, additive). In "I had a
  challenger and the original model argue until both agreed", the reader can't see who the
  challenger is. The drafter should say, from Paper 1, what it was, in a few plain words. I won't
  supply it from memory.
- **Heading** (Impact 2). "What does: a check that brings in something new" reads as broken
  syntax. Proposed: "What does: checking against something new".
- **Spelling and comma consistency with the series** (Impact 4). The draft has British "maths"
  and "favour". Part one is American ("favor" in 01, "analyzing"). Use "math" and "favor".
  Serial comma: "second readers, panels and checklists", "Politeness, tips and threats" and
  "sources, tools and people" lack it, while "a source, a tool, a reviewer with a different job,
  or a different model" has it. The published Part one essays also mix ("writing, print and
  libraries"). Pick one and apply it here. The brief's voice note says serial comma.

## Flag 1: length (2,151 words against Part one's 2,400–3,500)

**Under-explained, yes, though not because it's short.** The essay is complete as an argument. It
is short on *picture*. Where it needs more: a scene of Bart's own, or a worked example of the
routine (edit 2, about 150–250 words); a plain-words landing for 17.9 points (edit 7); a few words
on who the challenger was (edit 10); a landing sentence for the "dressing up the question" habit
(edit 5); and a close that turns (edit 3, roughly length-neutral). Together these bring it to
about 2,400–2,500, inside the series range. That follows from the additions; don't add words to
reach the range.

## Flag 4: the title is long

**Length isn't the problem.** At 14 words it is the same size as 01's (16) and close to 02's
(12), and it follows 02's two-beat pattern: hook, then position. **The problem is that the second
beat spends the close.** "Believe the disagreement, then check it against something new" is
nearly word for word the essay's last line, so the ending only repeats what the reader already
saw at the top. Keep the hook and give only half the rule:

- **Proposed:** "Same question, different answer. Believe the disagreement." (7 words.) The title
  carries the first half of the rule, and the close (edit 3) completes it: "Believe the
  disagreement, then check it against something new." "Believe the disagreement" on its own is a
  small puzzle (believe it how?), and the essay answers it.
- **Alternative:** "Same question, different answer, and why that's the useful part." Avoid any
  version that says "the most useful thing". The body says "one of the most useful things", and a
  title that drops "one of" overstates it.

## Best and weakest

- **Best:** "So "it gave me a different answer" isn't, by itself, a case against AI." through "No
  expert offers that." The pros and cons are honest, and the last line is short and earns its
  shortness. Edit 1 moves it to where that last line can hand off to the routine.
- **Weakest:** "So an answer you got by pushing too often just agrees with you." (edit 9).

## Verdict

**Ready after fixes.** The fixes include one structural move (edit 1), one addition only Bart can
supply (edit 2), and a rewritten close (edit 3). None of them reopens the position.
