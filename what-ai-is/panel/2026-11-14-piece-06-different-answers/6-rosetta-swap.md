# Rosetta: re-read of the swapped worked example (essay 06, "What I'd do with this")

Reader: the everyday AI user, read cold through the whole section. Scope: the one paragraph beginning
"Here's a time it happened to me." No source, date or paper name is suggested; no new fact is added.

Overall: the real moment is far stronger than the made-up one. It is concrete, it is checkable in the
reader's own head (four quotes, search the PDF, none there), and it pays off step 4's "ask for the source
and read it" exactly. Two fixes, one of which a careful reader will trip on.

## Findings (highest impact first)

**1. Impact 7 — "checking the answer against the one thing it didn't have, the paper itself."**
Problem: the paragraph opens with "I had an AI tool read a research paper." A first-time reader takes
that literally: the tool was given the paper. The closing line then says the paper was the one thing it
didn't have. Those two sentences contradict each other on the page, and the reader stops to work out
which is true. It also re-words step 4 ("something new") into a claim the paragraph hasn't set up.
Plain rewrite (no new fact): "What caught it was step 4: checking the answer against the paper itself."
If the record shows the tool never actually opened the paper, the author can keep the stronger point,
but it has to be said up front, not implied at the end (e.g. "I asked an AI tool to read a research
paper and summarize it" plus, only if true, "it never actually got the paper"). Don't add that unless
the record supports it.

**2. Impact 5 — "Here's a time it happened to me."**
Problem: "it" has no clear referent. The sentence just before is step 5 (pushing back, "That's step 4,
not arguing"), so "it" could mean pushing back, disagreeing answers, or the whole section. The reader
learns what "it" was only three sentences later.
Plain rewrite: "Here's a time a confident answer was wrong, and step 4 is what caught it." (Then the
last sentence can drop its own "What caught it was step 4" or shorten to "Checking it against the paper
itself is what caught it." Keep one mention of step 4, not two.)
Alternative that keeps the reveal at the end: "Here's a time a polished answer fooled me, until I checked."

**3. Impact 3 — "I pulled the paper's own PDF"**
Problem: "pulled" is light insider shorthand; "own" is doing work only if the reader already grasps that
the summary and the paper are two different things. Most readers will get it, but it costs a beat.
Plain rewrite: "I opened the paper itself and searched it for the four quotes."

**4. Impact 2 — "an account of what the paper was about, and it read perfectly"**
Fine as is. "Read perfectly" lands the key point (the tone gave nothing away) and sets up "Nothing in
how the summary sounded gave that away," which echoes step 5's "don't judge it by its tone." Keep.

## Checks asked for

- **Understood on first read?** Yes, apart from finding 1 (the "didn't have" contradiction) and the
  loose "it" in finding 2.
- **Flow.** Five steps, then the story, then "Match the effort to what's at stake." Works: the story
  shows a high-stakes check paying off, and "Match the effort" then tells the reader when that's worth
  doing. "One draw" in the next paragraph is already defined at the top of the essay, so no gap.
- **Does "step 4" land?** Yes. The numbered list is just above, and step 4's first instruction is
  "Ask for the source and read it," which is exactly what happened. Naming it once is right; naming it
  in both the opener and the close (if finding 2's rewrite is taken) would be one too many.
- **What does "an AI tool read a paper" mean?** Clear enough for an everyday user: they have pasted or
  uploaded documents and asked for summaries. No gloss needed. The only risk is finding 1.
- **Public safety.** No exchange, date, or paper named. Keep it that way.

## Verdict

Ship after fixes (finding 1 required, finding 2 recommended).
