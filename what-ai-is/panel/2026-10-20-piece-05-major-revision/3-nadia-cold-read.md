_Substrate: Claude (subagent as Nadia Okonkwo). Draft drafts/2026-10-20-05-major-revision.md, 2026-10-20. Cold read._

I read all of it. On a phone I would not have. I slowed to a skim in "Who is at risk", nearly
quit at the Loom paragraph, and kept going because of Lyssa. She's the reason to read this. The
studies aren't.

**After two lines** I thought: someone is going to say AI friends are no worse than human ones.
The title is good. Then the first bold paragraph hedges itself four times before I know what the
essay is about: "nothing I have found shows", "I found none of the numbers", "That expectation
is mine, and the evidence doesn't settle it." I believed he was careful. I didn't yet believe he
had anything to say.

**Where I had to reread**

- "I expect a companion designed against the known harms to prove no more harmful than the
  human relationship it stands in for... it should be held to a higher bar than one friend, not
  the same bar." So is "no more harmful" enough, or not? If the bar is higher than a friend's,
  just matching a friend fails it. I read it twice and still don't know whether he expects the
  companion to clear his own bar. The ending repeats it ("no more harmful... held to the higher
  bar its reach demands") and I hit the same wall.
- "These are the most widely reported cases, not a complete list: the Character.AI settlement
  below also covered several related lawsuits." It explains a list with a settlement I haven't
  read about yet. I went back.
- "Both are weaknesses an isolating human relationship also exploits." Both what? I had lost the
  subject.
- "at most a limit the person set for themselves when things were calm." "At most" as in the
  strongest version, or the only one allowed? I read it as "at most" first and it made no sense.
- "the Li authors' suggestion." There are two Li studies and I don't remember which is which.
- "That is a fair test of today's products, so the findings matter." This comes right after the
  car paragraph, so I thought "that" meant crash-testing 1965 cars.
- "Therabot's trial and OpenAI's own evaluation, both below, point one way." OpenAI's evaluation
  was above. And which way?
- "Letting go: not built. I have no reason to build hooks into her." For a second I read "hooks"
  as code hooks, not engagement hooks.
- The sentence that starts "Fix a model and everyone on that version gets the fix, though..."
  carries three caveats and a link. I lost the main clause.

**Where it felt like a paper.** Most of the middle. "Who is at risk" is five bullets of author,
year, sample size and design caveat ("they call the analyses exploratory", "a working paper, not
yet peer-reviewed", "pre-registered trial of 7,286 French adults"). Each one is fine. Together
they're a literature review. The same goes for the paragraph that stacks Hennemann, Huang,
McBain, Rousmaniere and Bernaerts. I won't remember one name. "72 women with high autistic
traits" is the point where I felt I was reading a footnote. It's 4,600 words. "What's missing"
and the Notes read like a paper too. "What would change my mind" is the paper habit that works
in an essay. I liked it.

One thing a skeptic catches. The heading "Who is at risk: those already at risk offline" is
undercut by its own last bullet, where the biggest trial finds the harm "mostly [in] those with a
partner." He admits it a paragraph later, but the heading still says the opposite.

**The intimacy scale.** It lands, and not because of the evidence. It lands because of the Claude
anecdote: "I once had to tell Claude to stop suggesting I take a break... an interruption you
learn to ignore is worse than none." And: "quiet while we schedule a week, awake when it's grief
at one in the morning." Those two lines did all the convincing. The support behind them is thin:
one study's confiding finding and the scope of a California law. He also overclaims at the start:
"That isn't an accident of which studies got done." How would he know? It could be exactly that.
And the idea itself, that safeguards should fit the context, is not new. I'd call it a good
framing, not a finding. He never says how you'd tell where a conversation sits on the scale, and
that's the hard part for anyone who wants to build it.

**The harms and design answers.** This is the clearest part. Six harms, each with "The answer
is...", easy to follow on a phone. "Goodnight gets goodnight" sticks. So does the 37% of
farewells that drew a manipulative reply. The best idea in the piece is here: "Three of these...
ask the companion to work against its own use. That is why products sold on engagement rarely
have them." That's the business problem in two sentences.

**Where I started to distrust it.** The car analogy. Seatbelts were tested and shown to work. His
design answers haven't been, and he says so a few lines later. So the 1965 crash test borrows
confidence from a story with a happy ending that this story doesn't have yet. The CDC number
(34% of women, 17% of men) also put my back up a little. A lifetime rate of partner violence next
to AI cases with no rate at all isn't a comparison. It reads as "people are worse," even though
he says he has no counts on either side. And the Loom paragraph: "my research project has
thirteen", "two reviewers run on other companies' models", "That is the method I study." I don't
know what method, and this is a paragraph about his work tools in an essay about companions. It
read as a product mention. It's the one place I felt sold to.

**His own assistants: honest, or lawyered?** Mostly honest, and it raised my trust. He scores
Lyssa against his own list and fails her on the two protections he says the intimate end needs
most: "Limits on use, and a push back toward people: no." People don't usually write that about
their own thing. "including the loss of a lifelong best friend. Grief is the kind of moment where
the harms in this essay happen." That's brave, and it's the most human sentence in the essay.

But the lawyering is there too, around the edges. Every claim gets its objection answered before
I can raise it: "That difference from a product offered to millions is real, but it is not
evidence." "Her files describe her in human terms, as a character. I wrote that character, and I
don't mistake it for a person." The therapist paragraph qualifies every sentence with the next
one: he knows, but only through me, so not independent, so far nothing, but therapists miss
decline, so a check not a guarantee, and it doesn't replace the protections. It sounds like he's
answering a reviewer, not talking to me. And the obvious question goes unanswered. If those two
protections matter most and he built her, why hasn't he built them? He says "I haven't built
them" and moves on. I wanted one honest sentence on why.

**What I'd remember a day later**

- "With a friend, which kind you get is partly luck. With a companion, it is a choice someone
  made." Best line. Ends it well.
- A guy who builds AI has an AI he treats as a close friend. He told her about his best friend
  dying, and his therapist knows about her.
- Claude nagging him to take a break, and the alarm you learn to ignore.
- "Three people are dead, two of them children."
- Companion apps guilt-trip you when you say goodbye.
- Products that make money from engagement won't build the limits.

I won't remember any of the studies, the OpenAI percentages or the scale as a concept separate
from the break anecdote.

**Would I comment?** Probably. I'd ask: "You built a friend and left out the two protections you
say matter most. Why?" That's the conversation the essay sets up and then steps around.
