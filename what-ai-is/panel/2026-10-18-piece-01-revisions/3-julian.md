**1. The "What I mean by 'it'" section**

It is now too long for an early section, but it isn't confused. Every claim belongs there. The trouble is pacing. The first paragraph stops partway through to point ahead to the line of models. The second paragraph says the old loss twice ("everything in it was gone", then "lost it all afterward"). It then ends on a 50-word concession that runs through four clauses. Edits 1 to 4 below bring it from about 300 words to about 230, and every claim stays: the engine and the car, the line of models named apart, the 2020 / 2023 / 2024 / 2025 dates, Reflexion, the brief, the lasting-change concession, and "never of the line of models".

**2. The last line**

"Judged by what it does, it does." blunts the ending. Three reasons:
- **It's hard to parse.** Right after "what it does", the second "does" reads at first as "it does what it does". The reader has to go back to supply "learn". A last line can't ask for a second reading.
- **The condition swamps the verdict.** The condition is as long as the verdict, so the hard word is buried.
- **It repeats itself.** "Where I land" opens with "Judged by what it does rather than what it is made of". The condition is already on the page three short paragraphs up.

Miriam was right to want the condition. It just shouldn't be a pun. Two alternatives:
- **A:** "So stop asking whether it learns. Judged by what it does, yes. Ask whether it can find out what nobody has told it." The condition stays word for word, and "yes" is the stressed word.
- **B:** "Judged by what it does, it learns. So stop asking. Ask whether it can find out what nobody has told it." Verdict, then command, then the new question. This one hits hardest.

I'd go with B. One catch: the section also opens with "It learns.", so with B that word appears three times. If you take B, consider cutting the opening "It learns." and starting at "Judged by what it does rather than…".
