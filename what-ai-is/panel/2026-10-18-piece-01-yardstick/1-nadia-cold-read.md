_Substrate: Claude (subagent as Nadia Okonkwo), general-reader cold read. Piece 01 (the yardstick)._

1. **What it argues, and what "it" is.** We judge generative AI the way we judge a calculator: it must be right or wrong, always consistent, fully explainable. Yet it does work we would give an intern, so we should compare it with the person who would otherwise do the job and set checks for the ways it really differs. "It" is chatbots and AI assistants, the kind that draft, advise and analyse alongside people.

2. **Did I know what "it" meant throughout?** Mostly, but the evidence wanders off that subject. The section on harsher verdicts uses a forecasting algorithm and self-driving cars, and neither is a chatbot. I noticed it and wondered whether those findings carry over. The "scale" section switches to "decision systems" and "foundation model" without explaining either. The curiosity section ends up with agents exploring video games and Minecraft. By then "AI" had widened to mean any learning system, and I wasn't sure the yardstick still meant the assistant from the opening.

   "I" also shifts. At first it's a writer with an opinion. Then "When a model or a strategy fails, I change it" makes the writer someone who runs these systems. Then "My own study" makes them a researcher. Each was a small surprise, and I never learned who this person is.

3. **Where I slowed down, got lost or stopped believing it.**
   - "figure from secondary summaries, to check against the book before publishing." This reads like a note the writer left for themselves. It made me trust the 55% figure less, and the piece with it.
   - "We tend to assume an intern's accountability rests on goals we can see. We can't see them, either." I read it twice and still couldn't connect it to accountability.
   - Point 5 is where I lost the thread: the "intentional stance," Chalmers' "*quasi*-desires," and "the same framework Zerilli and colleagues use." It was too many named thinkers in one paragraph.
   - The writer's own curiosity study: "getting the last machine fully right 10 times out of 10 against 2 without notes." Machines, blocks, "need two." I couldn't picture it and skimmed.
   - The curiosity section as a whole feels like a second essay set inside the first. It's interesting, but it's the longest part, and I forgot the main argument while reading it.

   The one thing I almost stopped believing was the "calculator yardstick" itself. The writer admits they haven't measured it ("That part is my observation"). I give them credit for that honesty, but it means the central claim rests on an impression.

4. **Does it end on a finished beat?** Not quite. The real ending is "Judge it the way you would whoever fills the role. Never let it pretend to be one." That felt finished. Then a list of "what would change my mind" follows. It's honest, but the piece trails off into bullet points instead of landing.

   The line that stuck came earlier: "One chatbot's confident mistake becomes 'AI can't be trusted.' One person's confident mistake never becomes 'people can't be trusted.'" I'd repeat that to someone.

5. **What I'd miss if this were all I had.**
   - Three other pieces carry real weight here and I don't have them. "The same functional test that credits these systems with learning" assumes I've read and accepted the learning piece. The deception section rests on a "rare point of agreement" reported in another piece. Accountability leans on "with memory, the system also learns from correction," which is also in the learning piece.
   - Who the author is, and why they run AI systems and their own studies.
   - One concrete example of a critic actually using the calculator yardstick, a real quote or headline. Without one, the opponent feels a little made up.

Overall, the opening and the intern comparison work well, and the evidence on human inconsistency is the most convincing part. The middle gets crowded, and the curiosity detour weakens the "method" that this piece is supposed to establish for the series.
