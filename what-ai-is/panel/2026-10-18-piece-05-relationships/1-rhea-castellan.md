# Outside review: Rhea Castellan

_Substrate: openai/gpt-6.1-sol. Piece: 05-relationships-for-outside.md. Finish: stop._

## VERDICT

The argument for evaluating particular designs against realistic alternatives survives. The stronger expectation that AI relationships are generally no worse, and probably better, does not gain sufficient support from the evidence presented: the piece repeatedly moves from uncertainty about comparative harm to confidence in favorable explanations of that uncertainty.

## OBJECTIONS

### 1. The proposed comparison is not yet a well-defined test

“Nothing I have found shows that a relationship with an AI persona is more harmful than a relationship with a person, given the same circumstances.”

“Without those numbers, ‘AI relationships are dangerous’ is a conclusion drawn from one side of a ledger.”

You explicitly identify your favorable expectation as an expectation, not a demonstrated finding. Nevertheless, the comparison gives it more apparent substance than it has. “A relationship with a person” includes relationships with friends, abusive partners, therapists and strangers. These are not interchangeable alternatives, and neither are their denominators.

The strongest objection is that “harm per relationship” could produce misleading results even with excellent data. A brief chatbot interaction and a decades-long partnership are different exposures; deterioration, coercion and suicide are different outcomes. Moreover, “given the same circumstances” must mean comparable starting circumstances. Holding subsequent isolation constant could remove precisely the pathway through which a companion causes harm.

A further distinction is missing: identifying a hazard does not require establishing that a product is worse overall than human relationships. Particular designs can be dangerous for particular users even if their average net benefit is positive.

Hernán and Robins, *Causal Inference: What If* (2020, Chapman & Hall/CRC), explains why a causal comparison needs specified interventions, populations, outcomes and follow-up.

Change: qualify the opening and add concrete comparisons. Specify which role, which realistic alternative, which population, which outcomes and which time horizon would test your expectation. Do not make a comprehensive comparative ledger a prerequisite for identifying danger.

### 2. Causal caution is applied unevenly

“That counts people who **arrive** in crisis.”

“The human role closest to a support chatbot also harms a minority of the people it serves, often unseen.”

The first statement assigns timing that the reported number cannot establish. Detecting suicidal intent during conversations does not show that every affected user arrived with it. Your next sentence correctly acknowledges that the data cannot separate harm, selection and benefit; “arrive” prematurely resolves that uncertainty.

The psychotherapy passage makes the converse mistake. Ending treatment worse off is not equivalent to being harmed by treatment. Some clients would have deteriorated further without treatment; others might indeed have experienced treatment-caused harm. An uncontrolled deterioration percentage does not distinguish them.

The strongest objection is therefore not that human treatment is harmless. It is that you correctly resist attributing chatbot users’ distress to chatbots, while allowing adverse outcomes during psychotherapy to establish therapist-caused harm. That asymmetry materially strengthens your human-risk comparator.

Hannan et al. (2005, *Journal of Clinical Psychology*) examined prediction of treatment failure; that work does not turn every deterioration into an attributable treatment injury. The causal distinction is also covered by Hernán and Robins (2020).

Change: qualify both passages. Separate adverse outcomes observed during an exposure from adverse outcomes caused by it, on both sides of the comparison.

### 3. “Designed against harm” currently functions as an untested explanation

“Mostly it is AI as it ships, with nothing designed to prevent the harm being looked for.”

“But they describe AI that was never designed against isolation, dependence or agreeing too readily, and they can't tell us what a relationship with a well-designed AI is like.”

These are factual claims about the systems’ design histories, not conclusions established by observing negative outcomes. A product may contain safeguards that are ineffective, inconsistently implemented or defeated by competing engagement objectives. “Never designed against” requires evidence about its instructions, training, interface and deployment policies.

The strongest objection is that your distinction risks becoming circular: successful systems count as appropriately designed; unsuccessful ones are reassigned to the unattended-car category. The studies then cannot challenge the design hypothesis because failure is interpreted as evidence that the relevant design was absent.

The Fang study also needs a cleaner separation between randomized conditions and voluntary behavior. Randomizing modality or conversation topic does not randomize heavy use. Neither an association with usage nor a nonsignificant difference between experimental conditions establishes the account you favor.

Hernán and Robins (2020) supports this distinction between assigned interventions and self-selected exposure. For the claim that these products lacked relevant safeguards: no citation established by the material supplied.

Change: add a documented audit of the safeguards actually present, qualify “nothing” and “never,” and specify your proposed protective features before testing them. Your own systems contribute experience here, not a study of mitigation effectiveness.

### 4. Shared vulnerability does not establish the same risk

“Read together, the risk sits mostly with people who are already isolated and who lean on one relationship for everything.”

“That is exactly the risk of an isolating human relationship, given the same circumstances.”

The studies as summarized do not establish “mostly,” and they do not establish “exactly.” They identify associations and possible pathways, not the proportion of all attributable harm concentrated in a defined vulnerable group.

The strongest objection is that the same starting vulnerability can interact differently with different environments. Isolation might make someone vulnerable both to a controlling person and to an endlessly available, personalized conversational system, without making the resulting risks equivalent. Availability, commercial control, apparent responsiveness and unilateral product changes are potentially relevant differences. They need testing, not an assumption that they cancel out.

There is also a developmental gap. Findings from adults or university students do not establish safety profiles for children and adolescents. Children’s judgments about whom to trust are developing and sensitive to evidence about a speaker’s reliability. Koenig and Harris (2005, *Child Development*) demonstrates selective trust in preschoolers; it does not establish chatbot harm, but it makes developmental differences relevant rather than optional.

Change: qualify “mostly” and cut “exactly.” Separate adult, adolescent and child claims, and distinguish shared vulnerabilities from demonstrated equivalence of mechanisms or outcomes.

### 5. The developmental question is not exhausted by supportive conversation

“the authors' own explanation, that connection may need the chance to care for someone, is itself a design brief.”

That is a hypothesis for design, not evidence that the missing ingredient can safely be supplied by making a chatbot appear to need care. Simulated vulnerability could increase attachment and obligation—the very mechanisms your safeguards are supposed to constrain.

From a developmental perspective, relationships also provide opportunities to initiate action, encounter resistance, investigate disagreement and discover that another person has independent purposes. Immediate comfort and perceived responsiveness do not measure those functions.

Yiu, Kosoy and Gopnik (2023, online publication in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*) distinguish cultural transmission from innovation and truth-seeking through exploration. They do not argue that transmitted knowledge is worthless or that language systems cannot be useful. Lucas et al. (2014, *Cognition*) shows developmental differences in learning causal forms, including children’s greater openness to unfamiliar causal structures under particular conditions. Neither paper studies AI companionship, so neither establishes that companions damage these capacities.

Their relevance is to what should be measured: whether a system supports independent inquiry and effective action, rather than merely producing socially successful responses.

Change: add developmental outcomes and distinguish genuine opportunities to care for people from simulated opportunities to care for a product. Treat the proposed design solution as experimentally open.

### 6. The benefit evidence cannot carry the broader safety forecast

“The pattern matters. Most of the benefit evidence is for tools designed for the role. Most of the harm evidence is for general companion apps and general AI assistants.”

You acknowledge that these are different products. But the proposed explanation—role-specific design produces benefits while insufficient design produces harms—remains confounded with differences in participants, oversight, follow-up, comparison conditions and outcome selection.

The strongest objection is that both patterns could occur in the same product. A system could reduce average depressive symptoms over four weeks while increasing dependence over a year or causing rare severe harms. Conversely, a system associated with dependence could still provide substantial immediate relief. Mean benefit, long-term substitution and uncommon serious harm require different measurements.

Li et al. (2023, *npj Digital Medicine*) and Heinz et al. (2025, *NEJM AI*) provide relevant evidence about particular interventions and outcomes. They do not establish comparative safety for durable persona relationships, much less benefits comparable in scale to cars or domestic electricity.

Change: qualify the design explanation, keep benefits attached to their measured populations and outcomes, and cut the infrastructure-scale forecast unless you provide an independent argument for it. Explicitly allow benefits and harms to coexist within the same intervention.

### 7. Correctability is an engineering advantage, not a demonstrated safety outcome

“fix a model once, and every user gets the fix.”

“OpenAI's own evaluation found its sensitive-conversation update cut responses that fell short by 65–80%”

The narrow distinction is real: software changes can be distributed widely. But distributing a modification is not the same as eliminating a failure mode, and improving a response-level evaluation is not the same as reducing suicide, delusions, dependence or isolation.

The strongest objection is that centralized correctability also centralizes the capacity to introduce regressions and disrupt relationships. Your own discussion of distress after Replika changes illustrates that an update can itself be a consequential intervention. Improvements must also survive long conversations, personalization and deployment outside the tested conditions.

The human comparison is overstated. People can be trained, supervised, excluded from professional roles and regulated, although not patched identically at scale. “In that way” preserves the narrow software distinction; it does not support the broader implication that human harmfulness is fixed while model harmfulness is progressively solved.

Fleming and DeMets (1996, *Annals of Internal Medicine*) explains why improvements in surrogate endpoints need not establish improvements in the outcomes that matter.

Change: qualify the universal-fix language. Separate benchmark improvement, deployment reliability and demonstrated reductions in human harm. Treat update-related disruption as part of the safety ledger.

### 8. The verification account promises more than secondary-source checking establishes

“Every source was checked by a tool that looks each one up and has two AIs from different companies judge whether it supports the claim it backs.”

“the paper itself not yet checked”

A press release can support an accurate account of what researchers publicly said without establishing that the underlying study supports your interpretation. Two models agreeing does not, by itself, supply independent methodological verification.

The strongest objection concerns the causal and statistical details on which your argument depends: what was randomized, whether an analysis was exploratory, what a null result excludes, how attrition was handled, and whether a symptom or safety measure was validated. These require primary-source inspection, not just source matching.

I have not independently verified the supplied 2026 items; objections to those findings here concern the descriptions provided. For the sufficiency of your particular verification workflow: no citation.

Change: check the primary papers before relying on them for major conclusions, or keep those conclusions explicitly provisional. Distinguish bibliographic verification, accurate reporting of a secondary source, and methodological appraisal.

## MISREPRESENTATIONS

The OpenAI prevalence description turns signs observed during use into people arriving in crisis. That is more favorable to the product than the stated measurement warrants.

The psychotherapy comparison turns deterioration during treatment into harm caused by therapists. Researchers studying adverse treatment outcomes would distinguish those claims.

The unattended-car analogy mischaracterizes studies of deployed products. Such research is not missing the relevant driver merely because it does not test your preferred safeguards; it measures systems operating under actual deployment conditions.

“It is hard to imagine enough harmful AI relationships to fill a body of coverage like that” gives television programming evidential weight that the subsequent disclaimer does not undo. Different exposure histories, reporting practices and editorial markets make that comparison uninformative. Cut it.

There is no direct account of Gopnik’s position here, so no direct misstatement of it to correct. But a developmental objection should not be reduced to confusing vivid cases with rates: it can concern how an interaction changes exploration, trust, agency and learning over time. Those are empirical questions, not objections based on what the system is made of.

## WHAT THE PIECE GETS RIGHT THAT YOUR SIDE SHOULD CONCEDE

Cases alone cannot establish comparative incidence, and realistic alternatives matter. Evidence from one chatbot design does not automatically generalize to every possible design.
