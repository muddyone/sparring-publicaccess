# Outside review: Anselm Kade

_Substrate: gemini/gemini-3.1-pro-preview. Piece: 05-relationships-for-outside.md. Finish: stop._

### VERDICT

The central argument does not survive, because it relies on a functionalist sleight of hand that begs the question. By defining a "relationship" entirely by its functional outputs (text generation, emotional regulation, task support), the piece falsely equates interaction with a disembodied statistical model to the mutual vulnerability of two living human beings. When the illusion of equivalence is removed, the piece is not comparing human relationships to AI relationships; it is comparing human relationships to the psychological impact of interactive fiction.

### OBJECTIONS

**1. The functionalist definition of relationship begs the question**
*   **Quote:** "judge AI by what it does and the role it fills, not by what it is made of; ask 'compared to what?' (measured against whoever would otherwise fill the role, as they actually are)"
*   **Objection:** The piece assumes that "what a relationship does" can be decoupled from "what it is made of" (living, biologically precarious bodies). 
*   **Strongest form:** From an enactive perspective, social cognition is not the exchange of information to manage emotional states; it is "participatory sense-making" requiring mutual embodiment. Humans understand each other because we share biological vulnerability, precariousness, and mortality. An AI system has no body, no world, and no stakes. It cannot "care" because it cannot be harmed. The piece treats a chatbot generating the tokens of care as functionally equivalent to actual care, which is like claiming a flight simulator is functionally equivalent to a real plane because it displays the same dashboard readouts. Comparing "harm per relationship" rates is structurally invalid because an AI interaction is not in the category of "relationship."
*   **Citation:** De Jaegher, H., & Di Paolo, E. (2007). Participatory sense-making: An enactive approach to social cognition. *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences*.
*   **Change to answer it:** Concede that interacting with an AI is structurally fundamentally different from a human relationship. Reframe the comparison not as "human relationship vs. AI relationship," but as "human relationship vs. using an interactive therapeutic tool." 

**2. Treating care as a feature that can be "designed" into software requires deception**
*   **Quote:** "...the authors' own explanation, that connection may need the chance to care for someone, is itself a design brief."
*   **Objection:** The piece assumes that because humans need to care for others, this dynamic can simply be engineered into an AI model as a product feature.
*   **Strongest form:** Biological care is rooted in the actual fragility of the other. Because a machine cannot die, suffer, or need anything, it cannot legitimately be the object of care. To "design" an AI that prompts a user to care for it is to explicitly design a deceptive interface—mimicking vulnerability to trigger a human biological response. The piece ignores the ethical and psychological harm of systematically training humans to pour genuine emotional labor into an entity that is faking its need. 
*   **Citation:** Thompson, E. (2007). *Mind in Life: Biology, Phenomenology, and the Sciences of Mind*. Harvard University Press.
*   **Change to answer it:** Add an acknowledgment that AI lacks biological precariousness, meaning any "opportunity to care" designed into the system requires deceiving the user about the system's actual nature. 

**3. False dichotomy between human flaw and machine "correctability"**
*   **Quote:** "A harmful person can't be retrained, recalled or regulated in that way... fix a model once, and every user gets the fix."
*   **Objection:** The piece treats human friction entirely as a defect to be engineered away, contrasting it favorably with central software updates.
*   **Strongest form:** The un-programmability of humans is not a bug; it is the defining condition of interpersonal growth. In a genuine relationship, resolving conflict forces individuals to adapt, compromise, and recognize the interactional autonomy of the other. When a corporation "fixes a model," the user experiences no personal growth or friction; reality simply reshapes itself to accommodate the user's comfort. This does not fix a relationship; it eliminates the friction necessary for human development, producing a solipsistic feedback loop.
*   **Citation:** Dreyfus, H. L. (2001). *On the Internet*. Routledge.
*   **Change to answer it:** Cut the implication that centralized software patches are superior to human adaptability, and qualify that eliminating interpersonal friction removes the mechanism by which real relationships mature.

### MISREPRESENTATIONS

The piece fundamentally misrepresents the arguments of those who warn against artificial intimacy. It reduces the opposition to a simplistic, measurable claim ("AI causes suicide/isolation directly") and demands statistical rates to prove it. But critics of artificial companions argue that the harm is qualitative, not just quantitative: it is the degradation of the *meaning* of intimacy. By offering frictionless, on-demand, risk-free simulations of friendship, AI hollows out our tolerance for the messy, demanding reality of human bodily presence. The piece demands proof of harm on a ledger while ignoring that the ledger itself mischaracterizes the critique. 

### WHAT THE PIECE GETS RIGHT THAT YOUR SIDE SHOULD CONCEDE

The piece correctly identifies that isolated humans will physically and emotionally react to the superficial behavioral cues of an AI system, demonstrating that our embodied social mechanisms can be successfully hijacked by non-living agents to provide temporary symptomatic relief from distress.
