## Overall diagnosis

The essay's position is sound and well chosen for this reader, and its honesty is real: "I found no study that
measures this in people who moved into management", the observational caveat on Artz and Goodall ("having done
the work goes with leading it well, not that keeping it sharp does"), Leyden & Scur labeled as "a prediction from
a model, not a measurement", the Kosmyna takedown done fairly, the short-term limit stated twice. That last
Goodall sentence is the best single sentence in the essay. Few writers would put it in, because it concedes that
the management evidence doesn't reach the prescription.

**But the essay has not yet worked out what the management analogy is for.** In the drafts behind it, the
argument ran "ordinary delegation, plus two things that make it less ordinary". The management frame now sits on
top of that, and in about half the essay it carries the argument: the two traps, the "enough to evaluate" rule,
the care section, the juniors question. In the other half it is a tag line added to a paragraph whose evidence is
about students, pilots, or doctors ("This is the part every new manager knows", "In other words, their work
became more like a manager's", "for a manager and for anyone using AI"). The frame works best once the essay says
plainly what it gives the reader: **a question (what do I need to keep so I can judge the work?) and a rule for
letting go (you don't need to keep doing it all)**. The evidence that skills fade, and that keeping them takes
practice, comes from elsewhere. Saying that once costs two sentences, and the frame then carries the whole essay.

Second, two passages that should support each other contradict each other instead. The specialists passage ends
"AI follows the same pattern", and its payoff says AI has "nothing like that yet". Fixed, the specialists become
the essay's missing test for which skills you can safely let go, and that test is what step 1 currently lacks.

Third, a few sentences are stronger than their sources: a truncated Watkins quote whose object changes, Artz
cited for a claim about judging work when it measures job satisfaction, and a position line about "most of us".
None of this changes the position. Most fixes add a clause or move a sentence.

**Strongest objection a fair critic would raise.** *"The management research you cite says leaders do well
because of skill they once acquired, not skill they kept up. The management literature also says hands-on skill
matters less as you rise (Benson, Watkins). Taken seriously, your analogy says: let the hands-on skills go and
keep only a fluency. Your step 3, 'keep practicing what you need to judge it, even where the AI is better', comes
from aviation and automation (Bainbridge, Casner, the FAA), not from management. So your title's analogy points
to the gentler rule, and your advice needs the stricter one."* As drafted, the essay half-answers this. The answer
is in the text but scattered: Goodall's caveat, "more of the judging stays with you" (in the specialists
paragraph), and "Pilots offer a model". Put together (findings 2 and 7), they make the reply: *the management
picture tells you what to keep (enough to judge); the ways AI differs from a team tell you to keep it more
actively than a manager would; and the pilot is the manager whose "team" can't push back, which is why aviation
schedules the practice.* That reply makes the frame stronger, not weaker.

## Ranked findings

### 1. Impact 8: the specialists passage asserts a pattern the essay later withdraws (flag 3)

> "We already live this way with people. I don't do my own taxes; I use an accountant. ... I don't have those
> skills, and I don't need them. Leaning on other people's skills is how working life has always run. / AI
> follows the same pattern. Here's where the cost lands."

and, five sections later:

> "I don't keep an accountant's skills, and I don't need to, because other things do the judging for me:
> licenses, track records, and the fact that they answer for their work. AI has nothing like that yet that you
> can count on."

**The problem.** The first passage says you can do without a skill entirely when someone else has it, and that
"AI follows the same pattern". The second says the reason you can do without it is that something *other than
you* does the judging, and that AI lacks exactly that. Read in order, the essay first tells the reader AI is like
the accountant, then that it isn't. The reader who stops after the first section (many do) leaves with the
opposite of the essay's advice. There is also a quieter mismatch with the frame. You are your accountant's
client, not their manager, so the specialists are a *third* relationship, set beside the tools and the move into
management. The essay never says which of the three AI resembles, or why the specialists are there.

"AI follows the same pattern" is also ambiguous. It can mean the tool pattern the previous paragraph set out (a
tool costs the skill it takes over), which is true and well evidenced. Or it can mean the specialist pattern just
before it (you don't need the skill), which the essay later denies. The sentence sits right after the specialist
pattern, so readers will take the second meaning.

**Verdict on flag 3:** keep the passage. It's the essay's best idea in the wrong order. It pulls against the frame
as placed, and supports it once the reason is up front.

**Fix (additive).** In "We've had this worry before", give the reason at the first mention: *I don't need those
skills because something else does the checking: the accountant is licensed and answers for the return, and I'll
know if the package doesn't arrive.* Then change "AI follows the same pattern" to say which half holds: *AI fits
the first half of that pattern: it costs you the skill it takes over. Whether it fits the second half, whether
anything besides you can do the checking, is where it gets harder (below).* The payoff in "Where the comparison
needs care" then reads as a promise kept, not a reversal. The postman also earns his place: he is the case where
the result checks itself, like GPS ("you'll know soon enough if you've arrived"). That gives the essay two
conditions for letting a skill go, which finding 3 uses.

### 2. Impact 8: the frame is a picture, not proof, and the essay should say so once (flag 2)

> "This is the part every new manager knows."
> "I found no study that measures this in people who moved into management. But anyone who has made that move
> will recognize it."
> "In any job that moves you away from the work, it's easy to lose touch a little at a time without noticing."

**The problem.** In this analogy, the source domain (management) has thinner evidence than the target (AI and
skill). Brief 14b is explicit: no study measures managers' hands-on skills declining (§1); no direct evidence
that managers overestimate their own frontline knowledge (§3); the expert-leader studies show skill once
acquired, not skill kept up (Complication 1). The essay is honest about the first point in one sentence, then
covers it with a universal appeal to experience ("anyone who has made that move will recognize it"). It states
the third management premise ("In any job that moves you away from the work...") as fact with no source. These
are the "broad statements about how organizations behave" that most need narrowing. A reader who has managed and
*didn't* feel this will doubt everything after it.

This is also why the frame reads as bolted on in places. Where the evidence is about students (the first "What
fades" section), the frame barely appears, and fits poorly anyway: new managers aren't learners. Where the
evidence is about pilots and doctors, the frame arrives as an end-of-paragraph tag. Steps 3 and 4 end with "A
good manager..." and "for managers or for anyone else" as tags, while their reasons come from Bainbridge and the
learning research.

**Verdict on flag 2:** the frame carries the traps, the "enough to evaluate" rule, the care section, and the
juniors question. It is bolted on in the two "What fades" sections and in steps 3 and 4. Both traps are
recognizably the traps of managing. Trap 1 has management evidence behind it, and trap 2 has none: its evidence
is all AI and learning research. That's acceptable if the essay says so.

**Fix (additive, about three sentences).**
- After the Leyden & Scur paragraph near the top, say what the comparison is for: *I'm using management as a
  picture, not as proof. It tells you what question to ask (what do I need to keep so I can judge the work?) and
  that you don't have to keep everything. The evidence that skills fade, and what keeps them, comes mostly from
  learning, medicine, and aviation.* This is a narrowed claim of the kind that earns trust. It also stops every
  later study from being read as "management research".
- Replace "anyone who has made that move will recognize it" with first person or a narrower quantifier: *Many
  people who have made that move will recognize it; I did* (only if Bart did), or *it matches what I've seen in
  the managers I've known* (which the essay already claims).
- Open trap 2 the same way: *Most people who've moved away from the work know how it goes: you lose touch a
  little at a time. For managers, I found no study that measures it. With AI there's evidence...* That keeps the
  trap and stops it posing as a finding.
- Drop the tag lines that only restate the frame ("This is the part every new manager knows"), or turn each into
  a bridge that does a job. Step 3's tag is worth keeping because it states the rule.

### 3. Impact 8: step 1's third pile has no test, and readers will put judging work in it (additive)

> "And everything else, the work you'd happily hand to someone else if you could. Hand over the third pile
> freely."

**The problem.** This is the step readers act on first, and its rule for what to let go is a preference ("happily
hand ... if you could"). By that rule, most people would hand over the judging work too, since it is exactly the
work they would most happily give away. The essay's own evidence gives a better test. Brief 14 Q7 frames the
skills you can afford to stop practicing as "the ones you will never need to check, and that you are not still
learning". Finding 1 supplies the two everyday conditions: the result checks itself (the package arrives, the map
gets you there, the calculator's answer is roughly right), or someone else answers for the judging (the
accountant, the surgeon). Neither holds for most of what people hand to AI, which is why piles 1 and 2 matter.

**Fix.** Give pile 3 its test: *everything else: work where you'd notice at once if it went wrong, or where
someone besides you is answerable for getting it right. Hand that over freely.* Keep "That's the ordinary trade,
and it's usually a good one." That single clause turns the specialists passage, the tools history, and step 1
into one argument.

### 4. Impact 7: the Watkins quote is cut so that its object changes (flag 5)

> "What a good manager keeps is narrower: in the words of one leadership researcher, enough 'to be able to
> evaluate' the work"

**The problem.** Brief 14b (2.5) records the words as "must know enough to be able to evaluate and recruit the
right people". The object of "evaluate" is *people*, for hiring, in the setting of top leaders who need
"cross-functional fluency" in functions they may never have done. The essay ends the quote before the object and
supplies "the work". That is the essay's thesis, but not Watkins's words. Brief 14b's "safe to say" line
abbreviates the quote the same way, so the drafter followed the brief. The brief's own full quote shows the
problem. A cite-check that reads the source will flag it, and the sentence carries the essay's central rule.

**Fix.** Either quote the full phrase and make the step explicitly yours (*he says leaders "must know enough to be
able to evaluate and recruit the right people". With AI, what you're evaluating is the work itself*), or drop the
quotation marks and paraphrase what the brief supports: *management writers make the same point: senior leaders
need enough fluency to judge, not the hands-on skill itself.* Route to `/cite-check`.

### 5. Impact 7: "keep learning on purpose" is in the position, the rule, and the close, but nothing in the body argues for it

> Position: "...and keep learning them on purpose."
> Step 4: "Skills don't stay current by themselves, for managers or for anyone else. Set time aside to learn in
> the areas you've chosen to keep, not just to practice what you already know."

**The problem.** Bart chose this element (brief §0), so it stays. But every argument in the body is about
skills fading from *disuse*, which practice fixes. Step 4 brings in a second mechanism, that the skill goes stale
because the work itself changes, and says it is distinct from practice ("not just to practice what you already
know"). The body never makes that case, and "What would change my mind" has no item that would test it. The one
element the reader is told twice to act on is the only one the essay never argues. Step 4 is also easy to confuse
with step 2 ("Keep doing what you're still learning"): two "learning" steps, with different meanings.

**Fix (additive).** One or two sentences in the body, presented as Bart's reasoning, not a finding (brief 14b 1.2
records this as an interpretation, and §5 forbids adding its source): *There's a second way a manager falls
behind. The work itself changes, and when you stop doing it you stop seeing how. With AI the work may change
faster, because the tool does. So keeping a skill means learning in it, not only practicing what you already
know.* The natural home is the end of trap 2, which is about losing touch. Then relabel step 2 to "Keep doing the
work you're still learning" and step 4 to "Keep learning in the skills you keep", so the two can't be confused.
Optionally add a change-my-mind line, framed as a gap: *I found no study of whether learning new material in a
skill protects people's judgment of AI work better than practice alone.*

### 6. Impact 7: "won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did" is a certainty the essay has argued against (flag 7)

> "Handing work to AI won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did."

**The problem.** Read literally against the essay's own pattern, the comparison is accurate. Calculators didn't
cause general decline; they cost a specific skill where they replaced its formation (Grade 4). That is the
pattern the essay found in AI. But this reader will hear "won't make you stupid" as reassurance, a few screens
after learning that students who used the plain tutor did 17% worse once it was gone, that the endoscopists'
unaided rate fell, and that "I found no study of what years of everyday AI use do". "Won't" is a forecast about
the long term, made with a certainty the essay says no one has evidence for. The close is the one sentence
people quote, and it is the one that most overstates the case.

**Verdict on flag 7:** earned as a claim about *general* decline. Not earned as unqualified reassurance, and
"won't" overstates the long term.

**Fix.** Make it a position and keep the calculator caveat in the same breath: *I don't think handing work to AI
makes you stupid, any more than calculators did. Like calculators, it costs you the skills it takes over, most
where doing the work was how you learned them or how you kept them.* That merges with the next sentence, so the
paragraph gets no longer, and it ends the essay on the claim the evidence actually reached.

### 7. Impact 7: trap 1's opening overclaims what manager research shows (flag 5)

> "A manager's main job is to tell good work from bad, and that takes the skill the manager no longer uses every
> day. The research on managers backs this up. In large surveys ... a boss's technical competence ... was 'the
> single strongest predictor of a worker's job satisfaction'"

**The problem.** There are two overclaims here. "A manager's main job is to tell good work from bad" is a broad
claim about management, and the literature the essay cites elsewhere disagrees (brief 14b 2.5: in every summary
of Katz, human skill matters at every level). And "the research on managers backs this up" is followed by a study
whose outcome is *job satisfaction*, rated by the worker (brief 14b 2.1 caveats). Satisfaction doesn't show that
judging work takes the old skill. Goodall & Pogrebna is closer (team performance, plus Goodall's untested
"quality-enforcer" theory), but it is one industry and observational. The essay's caveat sentence then says what
the studies show, and that is excellent. But the paragraph opens by promising more than the caveat leaves
standing.

**Fix.** Narrow the opening: *One job a manager can't hand off is telling good work from bad, and that takes
the skill the manager no longer uses every day.* Change "backs this up" to "points the same way". Say what Artz
measured in plain words: *workers were most satisfied with bosses who could do their job.* Keep the caveat
sentence as it is. Add one bridge to answer the strongest objection: *The case for keeping the skill sharp,
rather than just having once had it, comes from the cockpit, below.*

### 8. Impact 6: "Where the comparison needs care" is honest and clear, and should end by saying which way the differences push (flag 4)

> "The management comparison is useful, but it isn't exact, and two differences matter."

**The problem.** The section then gives four paragraphs: the team keeps the skill; the team pushes back (Köbis);
don't think of the AI as an employee (Wiles); and the specialists. That is at least three differences and a
caution, under a heading that promised two. More important, the section's conclusion ("So, for now, more of the
judging stays with you, and so does more of the skill it takes") sits inside the last paragraph, about
specialists. It isn't stated as the conclusion of the whole section. Read as a list of exceptions, the section
seems to undercut the title. Read as what it is, every difference pushes the same way, and the section is the
strongest support the essay has for step 3.

Two smaller points:
- **Köbis is about unethical instructions, and the essay widens it.** "A person on your team might refuse a bad
  instruction. An AI mostly won't." The source (brief 14b 5.2) is about "fully unethical instructions" (cheating),
  with guardrails that curbed it "but usually not eliminated". "Bad" can be read as "mistaken", which the study
  didn't test. The essay also never says why pushback matters to *skill*. Narrow and connect: *Asked to cheat, a
  person on your team might refuse; in those experiments, the AI mostly didn't. A team's pushback is one way a
  manager learns something's off. With AI, that warning has to come from you.* The first clause of the Köbis
  sentence ("people who could hand a task to a machine asked for more cheating") goes a little beyond brief
  14b's abstract-level record, which ties the extra cheating to setting vague goals. Route to `/cite-check`.
- **The guard against "the AI is your employee" arrives 200 lines late.** The opening invites the mirror: "When
  you're promoted to run the team..." Wiles (subgroup effect, working paper, vendor authors, all disclosed) is
  the strongest caution in brief 14b. Plant one clause early, where the picture is set up: *what changes is your
  job, not what the AI is.* Then the care section's "So be the manager, but don't think of the AI as an
  employee" lands as a reminder, not a correction.

**Verdict on flag 4:** honest and clear. It undercuts the title only because its conclusion is buried.

**Fix.** Fix the count ("three differences matter", or no number). Close the section, after the specialists, with
one sentence that gathers it: *Each difference points the same way. With AI, less of the checking happens
anywhere but in you, so you need to keep more of the skill than a manager of people would.* Keep "Who answers
for what an AI does is the subject of the next essay" as the only accountability line. It points ahead and
doesn't take over edition 10's subject.

### 9. Impact 6: Lee et al. are cited for the manager frame, though they reject it

> "Their thinking didn't vanish. It moved toward checking, combining, and overseeing what the AI produced ... In
> other words, their work became more like a manager's."

**The problem.** Brief 14b 5.4 records that Lee et al. explicitly *reject* "supervisor" as the metaphor, because
"the responsibility and accountability for the work still resides with the human user", and prefer
"stewardship". Citing a paper imports its other results. A reader who opens Lee finds the authors arguing against
the sentence the essay drew from their data. The fair version is also the more useful one, because it supports
finding 8.

**Fix.** Keep the finding. Replace "In other words, their work became more like a manager's" with something like:
*The work that's left looks like a manager's, though the authors themselves prefer "steward" to "supervisor",
because all the responsibility stays with the person. That's the next essay's subject.* If that would duplicate
the specialists paragraph's pointer, keep only one forward pointer and put the Lee clause in the care section.

### 10. Impact 6: the position states as fact a claim about "most of us" and "whole jobs"

> "As AI agents take on whole jobs, most of us are making that move"

**The problem.** This is the position's first factual claim, in bold, and the body never supports it. Two
paragraphs later, the essay hedges it correctly as opinion: "I think that's the move AI is asking of most of us.
As it starts carrying out whole jobs..." The supporting source is a theory paper (Leyden & Scur). The brief's
only "everyone is a manager" figure is vendor marketing (Microsoft's "even entry-level employees are managers
from day one", brief 14b 4.6), and the essay rightly doesn't use it. "Whole jobs" also points the reader toward
job replacement, which is the hiring question, not this essay's. Essay 07's agents carry out *tasks* and steps.
And the brief's reader, someone using ChatGPT turn by turn, may not recognize themselves as "making that move" at
all.

**Fix.** In the position: *As AI agents take on more of the work, more of us are making that move.* In the body,
"whole jobs" becomes "whole tasks". If Bart wants to keep "most of us", the body's "I think" carries it, and the
position should carry it too.

### 11. Impact 5: Leyden & Scur's mechanism is rational, and the essay turns it into a lapse

> "As the tools improve, fewer tasks seem worth checking, and in their words, 'the skill collapses' ... it names
> the trap: the better the AI gets, the easier it is to stop checking."

**The problem.** Brief 14b 4.1 records the words as "As increasingly fewer tasks are worth checking, the skill
collapses". In the model, checking stops because it no longer *pays*, not because people get careless. "Seem"
and "easier to stop" make it a willpower problem. The model's version is a stronger argument for this reader. It
explains why "check on purpose" (step 6) has to be a deliberate habit: the incentives run against it. That also
links to the essay's honest admission that in real work "speed gets rewarded and the easy option wins". "Predict
exactly this risk" claims more than the essay needs.

**Fix.** *In their model, as the tools improve, fewer tasks are worth the cost of checking, and the checking
skill withers: "the skill collapses". It isn't laziness; it's the arithmetic of a tool that's usually right.*
Drop "exactly".

### 12. Impact 5: "What would change my mind" can't falsify the frame itself

**The problem.** The four items test skill decay, long-run retention, checking versus doing, and juniors. None
tests the essay's title claim, that using AI is *like* managing. Brief 14b 5.10 records the gap: no study compares
experienced managers with non-managers on how well they use AI, and the one finding on delegation judgment says
it does *not* transfer cleanly. A falsifier for the headline claim is the kind of admitted uncertainty that earns
trust, and the essay can name it without citing anything new.

**Fix.** Add: *If people with management experience turned out to do no better than anyone else at catching an
AI's mistakes, the comparison would still be a useful picture, but not much more, and I'd say so.* Pairs with
finding 2's "picture, not proof".

### 13. Impact 4: Benson is about who gets promoted, not about which skills managers keep

> "That doesn't mean keeping every skill. The best salespeople, promoted, often make worse managers"

**The problem.** Benson et al. show that being best at production doesn't predict being best at managing. That
is a finding about selection. It doesn't show that good managers *let* their production skills go, which is
how "That doesn't mean keeping every skill" uses it. The brief's safe wording is narrower: "Being the best at the
work doesn't make you the best manager... Use this against 'keep doing everything yourself'."

**Fix.** *Being the best at the work isn't what makes a good manager: the best salespeople, promoted, often lead
weaker teams. So the skill to keep is narrower than "all of it".* This states the logical step instead of
implying it.

### 14. Impact 4: METR's participation problem shows reliance, not its speed

> "That isn't a measure of skill either, but it is a sign of how fast reliance builds."

**The problem.** Developers declining to work half their tasks without AI shows how much they had come to rely
on it. Nothing in the record measures how *fast* that happened. The essay rightly concedes the later rounds
pointed to a speedup, and this sentence shouldn't take back more than it conceded.

**Fix.** *...but it is a sign of how much people come to rely on it.*

### 15. Impact 3: two small precision points in the management evidence (flag 5)

- Goodall & Pogrebna: "teams led by former drivers". Brief 14b 2.2 has "started their careers as drivers and
  mechanics" and "extensive knowledge of the core business". Say *former drivers and mechanics*, or brief 14b's
  safe wording, "people who did the core work".
- Arthur et al. 1998: "Later research measured it." Brief 14b 1.1 notes the studies are mostly trained skills in
  military and lab settings, not careers. A clause, *mostly in training studies*, keeps the next sentence ("I
  found no study that measures this in people who moved into management") from looking like the only limit.

## Verdicts on the brief §6 flags

1. **Length (Julian, Nadia; additive view only).** No section fails to earn its place on argument grounds. The
   additive needs are greater than any cut: the specialists reason (finding 1), the third-pile test (3), "picture,
   not proof" (2), the body argument for step 4 (5), the care section's concluding sentence (8), and the frame
   falsifier (12). Together they add perhaps 150 to 200 words, and each closes a gap a reader would stall on. The
   only near-cuts are the tag lines in finding 2, and those should become bridges, not deletions.
2. **The management frame.** It carries the middle and is bolted on at the edges. Both traps are familiar traps
   of managing. Trap 1 has management evidence; trap 2's evidence is AI and learning research only, and the essay
   should say so. Findings 2, 7, and 9.
3. **The specialists passage.** As placed, it pulls against the frame: "AI follows the same pattern" is withdrawn
   later. Moved up, its reason becomes the test for what to let go, and then it is the frame's best support.
   Findings 1 and 3.
4. **Where the comparison needs care.** It's honest and clear. It doesn't undercut the title once the section
   ends by saying every difference points the same way. Fix the "two differences" count, narrow Köbis, and plant
   the Wiles guard early. Finding 8.
5. **Management evidence.** Mostly stated no stronger than the sources, and the observational caveat is
   exemplary. Three exceptions: Watkins's quote changes object (finding 4), Artz is cited for judging when it
   measures satisfaction (7), and Benson is used for a point about keeping skills when it is about selection
   (13). Minor: Goodall's drivers and mechanics, and Arthur's training-study base (15). Leyden & Scur are labeled
   correctly but softened from rational to lapse (11).
6. **Numbers (Vera, Rosetta).** Outside my lane. On argument grounds alone, none of the numbers carries more than
   its sentence claims, except METR's "how fast" (14), which isn't a number but reads like one.
7. **"Won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did."** Earned against *general* decline, which is what
   the calculator parallel actually shows. Not earned as reassurance, and "won't" overstates the long term. Make
   it a position and keep the calculator caveat in the same sentence. Finding 6.
8. **The title (Julian, Rosetta, Nadia; argument view only).** It is sound if "you" are the one moving into
   management. The risk is the mirror reading ("the AI is my team"), which Wiles warns against. Planting the guard
   early (finding 8) makes the title safe without changing it. "Choose the skills you keep" is the essay's real
   thesis, and the title states it.

## Register check (brief §3, my lane only)

Nothing demeaning toward named researchers or companies. The Kosmyna paragraph is firm and fair, and it leans on
the authors' own request to the press, which is the generous way to make that point. The vendor disclosures (Shen
& Tamkin, Lee, Wiles) are neutral in tone. Nothing self-deprecating about our own work. Bart's two first-hand
moments are well chosen. The Resolver paper's outside review is, in fact, the specialist route of finding 1 (when
you can't judge it, have someone answerable judge it). One clause linking the two would let the personal example
carry the argument as well as illustrate it.

## Verdict

**Ready after fixes.** No finding changes the position. The three at Impact 8 (specialists order, "picture, not
proof", the third-pile test) are load-bearing for a reader who acts on the list, and each is an addition, not a
rewrite. The Watkins quote (finding 4) should be fixed before the cite-check runs, not after.
