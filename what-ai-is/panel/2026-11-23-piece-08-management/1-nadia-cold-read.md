# Cold read: essay 08, "Using AI is like moving into management. Choose the skills you keep." (Nadia Okonkwo), 2026-11-23

Read once, straight through, as an everyday AI user on a phone. I have not read the series, briefs 14 or 14b,
the options file or any other background. Where I say "I stalled", I mean I stopped and went back.

## What I took away

Handing work to AI is like getting promoted off the tools: you stop doing some of the work, and the skills you
stop using fade. That's normal and mostly fine. The cost lands in two places: things you're still learning
(the math students who used plain ChatGPT did worse once it was gone) and things you used to do every day (the
colonoscopy doctors found fewer growths on their own within three months). The two traps are the manager's
traps: you still have to judge work you don't do any more, and you won't feel yourself slipping. So sort my work
into piles, keep doing the parts I'm learning and the parts I need to judge with, make my own first pass before
looking at the AI's, and test myself now and then. The "AI rots your brain" headlines come from a weak study.

That's clear and I'd act on parts of it. The lines I'd repeat: the colonoscopy numbers, the developers who
thought they were 20% faster and were 19% slower, "Reading an AI's answer is the rereading. Working it out is
the test," and the FAA one, "They kept the tool and scheduled the practice." I liked that it credits Bainbridge
from 1983 instead of acting like nobody noticed this before ChatGPT. That buys a lot of goodwill from me.

What I brought to it: "we're all managers now" is a line I've seen on LinkedIn a dozen times, usually from
someone selling agents. The title made me brace for that. The second sentence of the title, and the fact that
the essay is about *my skills* rather than about managing agents, is what got me past it.

I read to the end. On a phone I'd have drifted in two places: the developer-study paragraph in "The second
trap" and the learning-evidence paragraph in "What about people just starting out?". I'd have come back at
the pilots and the FAA.

## Where I stalled

1. **The specialists paragraph.** "I don't have those skills, and I don't need them. Leaning on other people's
   skills is how working life has always run." Then: "AI follows the same pattern." My first read: so I don't
   need to keep the skills AI takes over either, and this essay is going to reassure me. That's the opposite of
   the title. It got resolved, but at "The specialists I rely on are different again," near the end. I carried
   the contradiction for most of the essay.
2. **The salespeople sentence.** The paragraph before says bosses who could do your job are better bosses. Then
   "The best salespeople, promoted, often make worse managers." I went back. Which is it: does doing the work
   make you a good manager or not?
3. **"the researchers' later rounds pointed to a speedup instead."** I'd just been given the best number in the
   essay (expected 24% faster, felt 20% faster, actually 19% slower) and this sentence made me think the number
   had been overturned. Then it pivots to developers refusing to take part. I re-read to work out whether I was
   still allowed to believe the 19%.
4. **"So be the manager, but don't think of the AI as an employee."** The whole essay has told me AI makes me a
   manager. A manager of what, if not employees? I stopped here.
5. **"Check on purpose, not by feel."** I read the bold as "check the AI's output", and only the second sentence
   told me it means "check yourself".

## What I'd act on

Step 5 (my own rough pass first) and step 1 (the three piles), tonight. Step 6 maybe. Step 4 I can't act on as
written; see finding 2.

## What I didn't believe or didn't understand

- "As AI agents take on whole jobs, most of us are making that move." I use ChatGPT to fix emails and explain
  things. Nobody has promoted me. See finding 4.
- "The research on managers backs this up." What follows is about whether workers are *satisfied* with their
  boss, and Formula One results. Neither is about telling good work from bad. See finding 5.
- The research-paper story. He couldn't check part of his own paper, so he had an outside expert check it. I
  understand what happened; I don't know which lesson I'm meant to take. It reads as the accountant route (get
  someone else to judge), which is the route the essay just said AI doesn't have.

## Verdicts on the drafter's flags (mine: 1, 3, 8)

**Flag 1, length (a view).** It earns most of its length. The history, the two "What fades" sections and the
pilots are the best parts and I wouldn't cut them. The paragraph that doesn't earn its place is "Throughout this
series, 'it' has meant..." right after the hook; it's housekeeping where I was just getting interested. Two
paragraphs pile caveats until I lose the finding (Becker/METR, and the customer-support learning evidence);
they need restructuring, not deleting. Where it's under-explained: step 4, the juniors section's lack of
anything to do, and the bridge from the specialists passage to its payoff. Net, this needs a few more words,
not fewer.

**Flag 3, the specialists passage (a view).** It pulls against the frame on first read, hard. I don't keep my
surgeon's skills and I don't manage my surgeon, so it's both a second analogy and a counter-argument, and it
lands before the essay has given me the reason it doesn't apply to AI. The payoff ("licenses, track records, and
the fact that they answer for their work") is good and I believed it. It's just 160 lines too late. See
finding 1.

**Flag 8, the title (a view).** Works, with a warning. The first sentence sounds like the agent-hype posts I
scroll past; the second sentence is what makes it worth opening, because it gives me something to do. Don't
drop or soften the second half. "Choose the skills you keep" read clearly as "keep" meaning "hold on to".

## Findings, ranked

### 1. The specialists passage argues against the thesis, and the answer comes near the end

> "I don't have those skills, and I don't need them. Leaning on other people's skills is how working life has
> always run.
>
> AI follows the same pattern. Here's where the cost lands."

**What went wrong.** On first read this says: like my accountant and my surgeon, AI can hold skills I don't
need. That undercuts "choose the skills you keep" before the argument has started. "AI follows the same
pattern" is ambiguous: the pattern of the tools (cost the skill it takes over) or the pattern of the specialists
(no cost, because I don't need it)? I took the second. The resolution, "other things do the judging for me:
licenses, track records... AI has nothing like that yet," is in "Where the comparison needs care", far away.

**Impact: 8.**

**Fix.** Add one or two sentences in the specialists paragraph saying why that works for people: something else
does the judging (licenses, records, someone who answers for the work), and AI doesn't have that yet, which is
why this essay exists. Keep the fuller version where it is or fold it in. Change "AI follows the same pattern"
to name which pattern: the tools' one. This adds words; it stops the reader from carrying a contradiction for
most of the essay.

### 2. Step 4 is the essay's one rule, and it's the vaguest step

> "4. **Keep learning on purpose.** Skills don't stay current by themselves, for managers or for anyone else.
> Set time aside to learn in the areas you've chosen to keep, not just to practice what you already know."

**What went wrong.** The position, the title's second half and the close all end on "keep learning them on
purpose." It's the thing I'm meant to walk away with, and it's the one step with nothing I could do tomorrow.
"Set time aside" how, and doing what? It also blurs into step 3 ("keep practicing") and step 6 ("do a piece of
the work yourself"); I couldn't tell where practicing stops and learning starts. Everything else in the list
has a concrete move; this one has a sentiment.

**Impact: 7.**

**Fix.** Say what learning on purpose looks like for an ordinary AI user, in one or two examples drawn from what
the essay already showed works (using the AI as a tutor that questions you in the areas you've kept; producing
something first). Say in one line how it differs from step 3: practice keeps what you have, learning keeps it
current as the work changes. If 3 and 6 are really one habit, merge them so 4 stands out.

### 3. "Don't think of the AI as an employee" reads as taking back the title

> "So be the manager, but don't think of the AI as an employee. In a study of 1,261 managers reviewing the same
> drafts with planted errors, the label made little difference on average. But in organizations that already
> put AI agents on their org charts..."

**What went wrong.** Two things. First, the frame: I've been told for 3,000 words that I'm a manager now, and
this says the thing I'm managing isn't staff. I don't know what I'm managing instead. Second, a skeptical
reader notices the evidence is "little difference on average" plus a subgroup, in a working paper from people
who sell AI consulting. A firm-sounding rule on a "but" in a subgroup reads as stronger than its support.

**Impact: 6.**

**Fix.** Say plainly what you are the manager *of*: the work, not a colleague. The essay already has the line:
"Work that used to need a skill in us becomes a tool we use and manage." Point back to it. Then soften the rule
to match the study ("one study suggests the label can lower your guard, at least where firms already treat AI
as staff") or keep the rule and say it's the essay's advice, with the study as a warning sign.

### 4. The frame assumes I hand over whole jobs; I mostly ask ChatGPT things

> "As AI agents take on whole jobs, most of us are making that move..."

**What went wrong.** The reader this is for uses a chat assistant to draft, summarize and explain. They don't
feel promoted, and "most of us are making that move" sounds like the agent-hype line. If the frame only fits
people running agents, everyday readers will decide it isn't about them and skim.

**Impact: 6.**

**Fix.** One or two sentences early on that make the move land at chat size: every time you accept a draft you
didn't write, or a summary of something you didn't read, you're judging work you didn't do. That's the manager's
position in miniature, and agents just make it bigger. This adds words; it's what keeps the everyday reader in
the essay.

### 5. "The research on managers backs this up" points at the wrong thing, then the salespeople line seems to contradict it

> "A manager's main job is to tell good work from bad... The research on managers backs this up. ... a boss's
> technical competence... was 'the single strongest predictor of a worker's job satisfaction'... Over sixty years
> of Formula One, teams led by former drivers tended to do better."
>
> "That doesn't mean keeping every skill. The best salespeople, promoted, often make worse managers"

**What went wrong.** The claim is about judging work. The evidence is about worker satisfaction and team results.
I noticed the gap. Then the salespeople sentence made me go back: does having done the work help or not? The
link (being best at the doing isn't the same as being good at the judging) is in the writer's head, not on the
page.

**Impact: 5.**

**Fix.** Make the claim match the evidence ("bosses who know the work do better, and their people are happier
with them") or say what the studies suggest about judging and stop there. Add one sentence before Benson that
makes the turn: being the best at the work isn't what counts; knowing enough to judge it is. Then Watkins lands.

### 6. The developer paragraph undoes its own best number before moving on

> "In fact, with AI they took 19% longer. That study measured speed, not skill, and the researchers' later rounds
> pointed to a speedup instead. But they also reported a problem with running those rounds..."

**What went wrong.** The 24/20/19 finding is the sharpest evidence for "you won't feel yourself slipping",
because it's about the gap between feeling and fact. The next sentence makes me think it's been overturned. The
point that survives, that people *felt* faster than they were, isn't restated, so I left unsure what I was
allowed to believe.

**Impact: 5.**

**Fix.** Restructure, don't cut. Say what the number shows (the gap between how fast they felt and how fast they
were), then the later rounds and what they did and didn't overturn, then the refusal to work without AI as its
own point. End on the feeling-versus-fact gap so the paragraph does the trap's job.

### 7. The juniors section raises the worry parents and managers have most, then gives them nothing to do

> "I found no study that follows juniors who started their careers with AI and compares them with those who
> didn't. For now, this is a forecast, not a finding."

**What went wrong.** The essay says this is the worry it hears most. The surgeons story (trainees reached
competence by finding practice outside the approved routes) is striking. Then the section ends, and the six
steps are written for someone who already has skills to keep. As a manager of juniors, I wanted one sentence on
what to do for them, and didn't get it.

**Impact: 5.**

**Fix.** Add a line, in the section or as a note on steps 1 and 2: for someone starting out, most of the work is
still in the first pile, so the AI should be a tutor more often than a ghostwriter, and whoever manages or raises
them should make room for the groundwork that the surgeons had to find on their own. That stays inside what the
essay already showed. The cap limits packaging, never the message.

### 8. The best personal story is at the very end, and the other one's lesson is unstated

> "And in my own work, I've had AI tools report that a job was done when it hadn't happened. What caught it was
> knowing what the work should have produced."

**What went wrong.** This is the most relatable thing in the essay: everyone has had an AI tell them it did
something it didn't. It's the first trap in one sentence, and it arrives after the list, where I'm skimming.
The paper story next to it reads as "when I couldn't judge, I got an expert," which is the accountant route the
essay just said AI lacks; the lesson isn't said.

**Impact: 4.**

**Fix.** Move the "done when it hadn't happened" story into "The first trap", or open the personal paragraph
with it. For the paper story, say the lesson in a sentence: know which parts you can't judge, and when you
can't, get someone who can and answers for it, rather than calling your own read a check.

### 9. "Won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did" needs its other half

> "Handing work to AI won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did."

**What went wrong.** I read the fourth-grade part. Calculators did cost something, at the age the skill was
forming, and that's the essay's whole argument in miniature. As a closing reassurance, the line is a bit
stronger than the colonoscopy and juniors evidence I just read.

**Impact: 4.**

**Fix.** Keep the callback; add the turn in the same breath: not stupid, but calculators did cost fourth
graders their arithmetic when they took over the practice, and that's the cost to watch for.

### 10. The series housekeeping paragraph interrupts the hook

> "Throughout this series, 'it' has meant an AI assistant you work with turn by turn, such as Claude or ChatGPT,
> or a fuller setup that carries out steps on its own."

**What went wrong.** I'd just got the frame and the Leyden & Scur quote, and then a definitions note for a series
I haven't read. Same small bump at "the kind of AI the last essay called an agent" and "as essay 06 showed".

**Impact: 3.**

**Fix.** Fold the definition into the sentence where agents first appear, or move it to the notes. For "as essay
06 showed", say the fact and drop the pointer, or keep the pointer after the fact.

### 11. The customer-support learning evidence buries its point under caveats

> "But that held only for agents who had followed the AI closely, the outages were brief and not random, and the
> authors call the estimates noisy."

**What went wrong.** Three caveats in one sentence, on a phone; I lost what the finding was. "Both can be true"
rescued me.

**Impact: 3.**

**Fix.** Split it: the finding in one sentence, the caveats in a second, plainer one ("it's a weak sign: the
outages were short, not random, and the authors call the numbers noisy").

### 12. Step 6's bold reads as "check the AI"

> "6. **Check on purpose, not by feel.**"

**What went wrong.** Everything around it is about checking the AI's work, so I read it that way. It means check
yourself.

**Impact: 3.**

**Fix.** Put "yourself" in the bold: "Check yourself on purpose, not by feel."

## Verdict

Ready after fixes: the argument is clear, the evidence is honest about its limits, and I'd act on it, but bring
the specialists payoff up to where the passage is, make step 4 something I can do, and say what I'm managing if
not an employee before it goes out.
