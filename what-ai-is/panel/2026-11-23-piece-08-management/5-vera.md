## 2. Ranked findings

### F1 (Impact 7). The Watkins quote has been moved from people to work

> "What a good manager keeps is narrower: in the words of one leadership researcher, enough 'to be able to
> evaluate' the work"

- **What's wrong.** Watkins's sentence, confirmed on the IMD page, is: "enterprise leaders must know enough to be
  able to evaluate **and recruit the right people to lead functional areas** in which they are not experts."
  He means a senior leader judging *people*, in functions they never worked in. The essay cuts the quote just
  before its object and supplies a new one, "the work". As written, the reader takes away that a researcher said
  managers should keep enough skill to judge the work. He didn't. Brief 14b's "safe to say" list repeats the
  same compression, so the drafter took it from there. Brief 14b also marks Watkins as practitioner writing
  based on interviews, not a measured study, and "leadership researcher" doesn't tell the reader that.
- **Why it matters.** This is the one line in the essay that ties "keep the skills you need to judge" to the
  management literature. It's also the thesis sentence of the first trap.
- **Fix.** Either quote him whole and say what it is ("a senior leader, one management writer argues, must know
  enough 'to be able to evaluate and recruit the right people' for areas they've never worked in"), and let
  Bart draw the step to judging the work in his own voice. Or drop the quotation marks and make it Bart's own
  reading of where the management writers point. Benson (F9) already does the "not every skill" job. This adds
  one clause and moves nothing.

### F2 (Impact 6). Flag 7: "won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did"

> "Handing work to AI won't make you stupid, any more than calculators did."

- **Verdict: right in direction, stronger than the evidence in certainty.** In the essay's own sense the
  comparison is apt. Calculators didn't make anyone generally stupid, but sustained use in fourth grade did cost
  a particular skill, and that's exactly the pattern the essay argues for. Three things make "won't" too strong:
  (a) "won't" is a forecast, and the essay itself says no study covers years of everyday use (l.280–281);
  (b) "stupid" is a general-intelligence claim that no source here measured, either way; (c) as written, "any
  more than calculators did" can be read as "calculators did no harm", which the essay disproved in its own
  second section.
- **Fix.** Keep the line's force and make it something the evidence can back: "Nothing I found shows that
  handing work to AI makes you stupid, any more than calculators did. Like calculators, it costs you the
  particular skills it takes over." That adds one sentence, carries the fourth-grade lesson into the landing, and
  turns "won't" into what was actually found. If Bart wants to keep "won't", add "on the evidence so far".

### F3 (Impact 6). Flag 5: Artz is said to back a claim it didn't measure

> "A manager's main job is to tell good work from bad, and that takes the skill the manager no longer uses every
> day. The research on managers backs this up. … 'the single strongest predictor of a worker's job
> satisfaction'"

- **What's wrong.** The quote is exact. What it's said to back isn't. Artz et al. measured workers' **job
  satisfaction**, and "competent" was the **worker's own rating** of the boss (14b 2.1 caveats). Nothing in it
  measures whether the boss judged work well. Goodall & Pogrebna is closer: team results, plus Goodall's
  *untested* "quality-enforcer" theory. The essay's caveat sentence (l.150–151) is good, but it answers a
  different gap (having done the work, not keeping it sharp). It doesn't cover this one.
- **Fix.** Say what was counted: "Workers were happier with a boss they rated as able to do their job; in large
  American and British surveys it was 'the single strongest predictor' of job satisfaction." Then "backs this
  up" becomes "points the same way". This adds about ten words and tells the reader what the study counted. It
  also answers flag 5 for the other studies: Arthur, Artz and Goodall are stated no stronger than their marks
  once F3 is in; Benson needs F9; Watkins needs F1; Leyden & Scur is labelled ("a prediction from a model").

### F4 (Impact 5). Lehmann: our inference reported as the study's result

> "Overall, the effect came out at zero, because the two habits cancelled out"

- **What's wrong.** Two separate results are joined by a "because" the source doesn't give. The zero is the
  pre-registered lab result. The produce-vs-explain split comes from **exploratory** analyses and a field study
  (14 1.3). "The overall effect can be zero while the effect of how someone uses it is not" is the brief's
  **[ours]** bearing. It doesn't say the two cancelled. The paper is also a preprint, which the essay doesn't
  say here.
- **Fix.** "Overall, the researchers found no effect on learning. Their closer look suggests why it can come out
  that way: the two habits pull in opposite directions." Add "(an early, unreviewed paper)" the way the essay
  does for Shen & Tamkin. This adds one clause and keeps the point.

### F5 (Impact 5). Lee et al.'s authors reject the frame the essay reads into them

> "In other words, their work became more like a manager's."

- **What's wrong.** It's presented as Bart's gloss ("In other words"), which is fair. But brief 14b 5.4 records
  that these authors explicitly reject "supervisor" as the metaphor, preferring "stewardship", because "the
  responsibility and accountability for the work still resides with the human user". A reader who follows the
  link will find the authors saying the opposite of the gloss.
- **Fix.** Keep the gloss and add the authors' caveat. It is the essay's own "Where the comparison needs care"
  point, from a source already cited: "The authors themselves prefer 'stewardship' to 'supervisor', because the
  responsibility stays with you." This adds one sentence and makes the citation work for the essay rather than
  against it.

### F7 (Impact 4). Brynjolfsson outages: three words beyond the check

> "after a few months with it, some handled chats faster during the short outages when it was down. … the
> outages were brief and not random"

- **What's wrong.** The three-source check says "AI outages are **rare** and **not necessarily** random". The
  essay has "brief" (a different property) and "not random" (stronger). "After a few months with it" isn't in
  the check or the brief.
- **Fix.** "the outages were rare and not necessarily random", and drop "after a few months" unless the drafter
  confirms it in arXiv 2304.11771. These are word swaps, with no change in length.

### F8 (Impact 4). Leyden & Scur: "seem" and the unnamed skill

> "As the tools improve, fewer tasks seem worth checking, and in their words, 'the skill collapses'"

- **What's wrong.** The abstract says fewer tasks **are** worth checking. In the model, letting checking go is
  the rational response, not a misperception, and "seem" turns it into a perception trap. That's the essay's
  reading, not theirs. And "the skill" in their sentence is the **verification** skill. The reader may hear
  "skill" in general.
- **Fix.** "As the tools improve, fewer tasks are worth checking, and in their words, 'the skill collapses': the
  skill of checking." Bart's own gloss that follows ("the easier it is to stop checking") is fine as his.

### F9 (Impact 4). Benson: "often" is stronger than the abstract, and no design label

> "The best salespeople, promoted, often make worse managers"

- **What's wrong.** The abstract says "the best worker is not always the best candidate for manager". The team
  sales decline comes from a digest **[secondary]**. The brief marks the design observational, quasi-causal, and
  the essay labels Artz and Goodall but not Benson.
- **Fix.** Brief 14b's safe wording: "Being the best at the work doesn't make you the best manager: in one large
  study of sales firms, top sellers promoted to manager often did worse in the role than others." That keeps
  "often" only if the drafter confirms it in the paper. Otherwise use "weren't the best managers".

### F10 (Impact 4). Bart's paper: the outside review hasn't happened yet

> "The paper sends that part to a named outside expert review instead"

- **What's wrong.** The paper's wording (14 A.1) is "routed to the named **future** expert audit". The essay's
  present tense reads as if the review exists. This is Bart's first-hand moment, but the published record says
  "future", and a reader who opens the DOI will see that.
- **Fix.** "The paper names an outside expert review for that part, still to come." It's two words longer and
  it's accurate. The example is stronger for being honest about it.

### F11 (Impact 3). Köbis: comparative turned absolute

> "A person on your team might refuse a bad instruction. An AI mostly won't."

- The source is comparative ("far more likely than human agents"). The brief gives no compliance rate, and
  guardrails curbed it. **Fix:** "An AI is far less likely to."

### F12 (Impact 3). Budzyń: "three letters questioning it"

- The letters were **[record: content not read]**, and what the critics argue is **[secondary]**. "Questioning"
  is probably right but isn't verified. **Fix:** "the journal printed three letters in response, and critics
  have raised doubts about it", or keep it as is once the drafter has read one letter.

### F13 (Impact 3). Stanković: "a published comment"

- It's an arXiv comment (2601.00856), listed among brief 14's preprints. The everyday reader hears "published"
  as "peer-reviewed". The essay is otherwise careful about this line, and here it gives the critic a status
  it denies the critiqued paper. **Fix:** "a comment posted by other researchers questions its methods".

### F14 (Impact 3). METR: "how fast reliance builds"

- The source shows that an increased share of developers won't work without AI: dependence, not its speed.
  **Fix:** "a sign of how much people now rely on it".

### F15 (Impact 3). Notes undercount the vendor-affiliated studies

> "Two of the studies I rely on are by AI companies' own researchers"

- The body correctly says three of Wiles et al.'s four authors work at BCG, "which sells AI consulting". Brief
  14b marks that as vendor. The disclosure note should match. **Fix:** "Two … by AI companies' own researchers
  (Anthropic, Microsoft), and a third mostly by consultants who sell AI services (BCG)." This adds one clause.

### F16 (Impact 2). Minor, optional

- **Arthur 1998.** Brief 14b says the studies are "mostly of trained skills, military and lab, not careers".
  The essay's "I found no study that measures this in people who moved into management" covers the point. A
  short "mostly in military and lab training" would make it exact.
- **Goodall & Pogrebna.** "Former drivers" is narrower than "drivers and mechanics", but "the longest driving
  careers" supports it. No fix needed.

- **Flag 7 ("won't make you stupid").** Earned in direction, stronger in certainty than the evidence allows.
  Reword as F2 to "nothing I found shows …", and carry the calculator's real cost into the landing.

**Verdict: ready after fixes.** No figure is wrong. The fixes are rewording, and several add a clause. None
needs a new source.
