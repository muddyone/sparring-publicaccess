# Miriam Vale: argument review of "It learns. The better question is what kind of learner it is."

## Overall diagnosis

The minimal claim earns belief: an assistant with persistent memory can learn, and "it's just a note in a file" is not a refutation. The piece claims three things that are larger than that, and those it has not earned:

- that its definition settles the question ("plainly learns");
- that Gopnik "leaned on form";
- that the follow-up shows "Claude formed the expectation Gopnik's children form."

All three reviewers found the same weak point from different directions. The piece calls every difference that matters "function" and every difference it dislikes "form," so the thesis wins by how it sorts. Fixed honestly, the piece gets stronger. Its real contribution is a rule about who has to prove what, plus one testable question. It is not a verdict.

**Strongest element.** The H.M. section, and the piece's admission that "we're pattern machines too" was itself an argument about form. All three readers credited these.

**Biggest credibility risk.** The payoff. The model that drafted the piece wins its own test on the third framing tried, and the piece reports two framings. Details are in item 3 of the revision list.

**Biggest risk of losing the reader.** The thread breaks at "Both of us overreached… we each leaned on form." Nadia lost it there, and Rhea shows the sentence is wrong.

### What the "both of us leaned on form" section becomes

Rhea is right. The honest arc is:

1. Her soundbite ("don't learn the way we do") and Bart's comeback ("we're pattern machines too") were both about form.
2. Her published case is not. It is functional, and the piece agrees with it.
3. Her case doesn't show that models fail to learn. It shows they are a different *kind* of learner: they receive more than they discover.

This removes the contradiction, credits her accurately (and the accurate version is the stronger one, as it has been in every case like this I've reviewed), and turns the section into the bridge to the piece's own open question. The judging-by-function framing then stops being a weapon against Gopnik and becomes the way her real claim gets heard. The "dissolve" paragraph shrinks to one honest point: both kinds of learner are statistical, and the difference lies in what evidence they learn from and who chooses it, which *is* her point.

## 2. The strongest honest version of the central claim

> If learning means a lasting change, caused by experience, that a system itself uses to guide what it does later, then an assistant that decides what to record from what happened and acts on it later does learn, and objections that rest only on where or in what material the change is stored don't defeat that. "Function, not form" is a rule about who must prove what: a difference in how learning is built (stakes, automaticity, a body) counts as soon as someone shows what it changes, and Gopnik's published contrast between receiving knowledge and discovering it is exactly such a claim, one this framing should adopt rather than answer. Whether written notes can carry a rule a system discovered for itself is testable, and one small exploratory probe suggests, for one model and short of proof, that they can.

Compared with the current draft, this drops "plainly," drops upgrades from the learner, stops claiming that Gopnik leaned on form, and narrows the test result.

## 3. Structural problems the reviewers did not raise

**S1. The lead example is the weakest one, and the strongest is buried. Impact 8.** The nickname test is a case of being *told*, which is receiving, the very thing Gopnik grants models do well. It is also the case the contacts-app objection fits best, because a contacts app is told things too. The piece's best evidence is the follow-up, where Claude wrote "treat 'both needed' as the default rule" from its own experiments, unprompted on content. That is the answer to the sticky note: the system chose what to keep from its own experience. The piece never makes that connection.

**S2. The payoff answers a different question than the opening promised. Impact 8.** The opening promises "discover or only receive." The study is framed twice as "the learning-profile gap put to a test" (does writing it down work as well as keeping it inside the learner?). Yet the unnamed-rules result is, in a small way, evidence about *discovery*: the rule had to be found, not received. The piece has its payoff and labels it as something else. "What kind of learner" has two separate parts, what it can keep (the profile) and where its knowledge comes from (receiving or discovering), and the piece runs them together without saying so.

**S3. The form/function split, as written, can't lose. Impact 7.** Every difference that turns out to matter gets relabelled as function ("the one place where form may change function"), so "form doesn't matter" is true by construction. Saying openly that this is a rule about who must prove what removes the circularity and answers Anselm's objection 3 at the same time.

**S4. The follow-up's reference numbers don't apply to the condition that produced the result. Impact 6.** "A perfect explorer who doesn't know the rule gets about half the machines right, and one who knows it gets 93%" comes from `budget_sim.py`, which searches the named two-rule space (15 pairs times 2 rules). The positive result came in the unnamed condition, where the README says the models also considered "exactly one" rules. That is a larger hypothesis space, so those reference figures don't describe it. In the same condition, Claude's no-notes baseline (2 of 10) sits far below the 53% figure. The README itself warns that this "lowers its no-notes baseline… and so enlarges the room notes can fill." The piece quotes 10 against 2 and leaves that warning out.

**S5. "Plainly." Impact 5.** Under the minimal definition the claim is plain but small. Under a richer definition it is the open question. "Plainly" leans on the reader in both cases.

**S6. The "change my mind" list doesn't match the piece's own exposures. Impact 5.** One bullet would support him (continual updating). Missing are stakes, which the piece raised, and a locked replication that includes a *reversal* test (a "needs one" machine after a run of "needs two"). Such a replication could fail even if notes carry the rule.

## Verdict

The piece's best idea survives. Judging learning by what a system can do lets you hear Gopnik's real claim, and that claim can be tested. What doesn't survive is the scaffolding built to make the argument a win: Gopnik as someone who leaned on form, upgrades counted as the system learning, "plainly," and a payoff that is reported more generously than its own record allows. Items 1 to 3 have to land before this is public. With them the piece is narrower and harder to dismiss.
