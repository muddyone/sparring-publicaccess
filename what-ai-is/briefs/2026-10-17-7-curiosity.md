# Curiosity: do AI systems have it, and can it be designed in? (as of 2026-10-17)

_Research brief for the yardstick position piece (`positions/01-the-yardstick.md`). Gathered
2026-10-17 by web search. Marks as in brief 6: **[abstract]**, **[record]**, **[secondary]**,
**[report]** (already cite-checked in an earlier what-ai-is report)._

## The claim under test

Bart, 2026-10-17: the real missing ingredient is curiosity. There is little evidence of natural
curiosity in these systems, and curiosity is at the heart of the human-learning discussion. A
system could be designed to be curious. But most of what looks like curiosity, absent
instructions that mimic it, is explained by ordinary reasoning.

## Bottom line

- **Curiosity is hard to define even for people.** Kidd & Hayden (2015) propose studying it
  behaviourally, through the motivations for information-seeking. (The phrase "a drive state for
  information" comes from coverage of the paper; the cite-check didn't find it in the abstract.) That suits the functional
  test: curiosity counts if the system *seeks information without being asked to*.
- **Language models don't explore reliably by default.** In simple bandit tasks, GPT-3.5, GPT-4
  and Llama 2 explored adequately in only one configuration: GPT-4 with step-by-step reasoning and
  an externally summarized history (Krishnamurthy et al. 2024). Our own pilot fits the same
  picture: models explored efficiently, but only when the task told them to explore. Gopnik's
  group found models explore less efficiently than people (Samiei et al. 2026).
- **Curiosity can be designed in, and has been for decades,** mostly in reinforcement learning:
  - Schmidhuber's 1991 proposal of curiosity and boredom as rewards for improving a world model
  - Oudeyer & Kaplan's 2007 typology of "intrinsic motivation"
  - Pathak et al.'s 2017 curiosity reward from prediction error, which explored games with no
    external reward
- **Designed curiosity has known failure modes.** Prediction-error curiosity can be captured by
  randomness it can never learn to predict (Burda et al. 2019, the "noisy TV" problem).
- **Language-model agents now borrow curiosity from people.** OMNI uses a language model as a
  model of what humans find interesting, to choose what an agent should learn next (Zhang et al.
  2024). Voyager has GPT-4 propose its own next tasks in Minecraft, based on what it has explored
  so far (Wang et al. 2023). Both are curiosity by design. OMNI's version is explicitly derived
  from human notions of interestingness, which fits Gopnik's "cultural technology" framing.
- **So Bart's observation holds, with a qualification:** no *natural* curiosity is evident in
  today's assistants, but *engineered* curiosity is a live research line. Whether engineered
  curiosity counts is the same functional question as everywhere else in these pieces: judge it by
  whether the behaviour shows up.

## Q1. What is curiosity, operationally?

**1.1** Kidd, C., & Hayden, B. Y. (2015). The psychology and neuroscience of curiosity. _Neuron_,
88(3), 449–460. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.010 **[abstract]**
- There is no widely accepted definition of curiosity, and separating it formally from
  information-seeking has proven difficult. The authors propose treating curiosity as "a drive
  state for information" and studying its function, evolution, mechanism and development.
- Bearing: a behavioural definition, so the question "does an AI have it?" can be asked
  functionally.

**1.2** Yiu, E., Kosoy, E., & Gopnik, A. (2023). Transmission versus truth, imitation versus
innovation. _Perspectives on Psychological Science_ 19.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11373165/ **[report]**
- Contrasts children as truth-seekers who experiment on the world with models as cultural
  technologies that transmit what people wrote. "Children choose what to explore; a model never
  chooses its own experience" is the learning synthesis's paraphrase, stronger than the abstract
  (cite-check, 2026-10-17).

## Q2. Do current language models explore on their own?

**2.1** Krishnamurthy, A., Harris, K., Foster, D. J., Zhang, C., & Slivkins, A. (2024). Can large
language models explore in-context? _NeurIPS 2024_. https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15371 **[abstract
+ secondary]**
- Five-armed bandit tasks, with the environment and history given in the prompt. GPT-3.5, GPT-4
  and Llama 2 were tested across many prompt designs. Only GPT-4 with chain-of-thought and an
  externally summarized interaction history explored satisfactorily. The authors conclude that
  non-trivial interventions may be needed for LLMs to explore in more complex settings.
- Bearing: the closest direct test of "natural" exploration. It finds the behaviour absent by
  default.

**2.2** Samiei, M., Yiu, E., … Gopnik, A., et al. (2026). Human adults and LLMs as scientists: Who
benefits from active exploration? arXiv:2606.06464. https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06464 **[report]**
- State-of-the-art models approach human accuracy on an active causal-learning task, but explore
  less efficiently. The abstract doesn't name the models.

**2.3** The locked Gopnik pilot (this project, 2026-10-17). All three models identified every
"needs two" machine within their test budgets, but every test was one the task asked for.
`pilots/gopnik-memory-overhypothesis-2026-10-16/result.md`. **[internal]**

## Q3. Can curiosity be designed in?

**3.1** Schmidhuber, J. (1991). A possibility for implementing curiosity and boredom in
model-building neural controllers. In J.-A. Meyer & S. W. Wilson (Eds.), _From Animals to
Animats: Proceedings of the First International Conference on Simulation of Adaptive Behavior_
(pp. 222–227). MIT Press. **[record + secondary]**
- An early proposal: reward a learning system for improving its own world model ("curiosity"),
  and reduce the reward as predictions stop improving ("boredom").

**3.2** Oudeyer, P.-Y., & Kaplan, F. (2007). What is intrinsic motivation? A typology of
computational approaches. _Frontiers in Neurorobotics_, 1, 6.
https://doi.org/10.3389/neuro.12.006.2007 **[abstract]**
- Synthesizes psychological accounts of intrinsic motivation and argues that, read
  computationally, they are not operational and sometimes inconsistent. Proposes a formal
  typology of computational approaches, for developmental robotics.

**3.3** Pathak, D., Agrawal, P., Efros, A. A., & Darrell, T. (2017). Curiosity-driven exploration by
self-supervised prediction. _ICML 2017_, 2778–2787. https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363 **[abstract]**
- Curiosity as an intrinsic reward: the error in predicting the results of its own actions, in a
  learned feature space. Tested in VizDoom and Super Mario Bros, including exploration with no
  external reward at all.

**3.4** Burda, Y., Edwards, H., Pathak, D., Storkey, A., Darrell, T., & Efros, A. A. (2019).
Large-scale study of curiosity-driven learning. _ICLR 2019_. https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355
**[abstract]**
- Purely curiosity-driven agents, with no external reward, across 54 benchmark environments
  including the Atari suite. The authors show limits of prediction-based curiosity in random
  (stochastic) settings.
- Bearing: designed curiosity has its own failure shapes. An agent rewarded for surprise can fixate
  on noise it can never learn to predict. The "noisy TV" label is the field's name for this; the
  abstract states the limitation, not the label.

## Q4. Curiosity in language-model agents

**4.1** Zhang, J., Lehman, J., Stanley, K., & Clune, J. (2024). OMNI: Open-endedness via models of
human notions of interestingness. _ICLR 2024_. https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01711 **[abstract]**
- Open-ended learning needs tasks that are interesting, not just learnable. OMNI uses a language
  model as a "model of interestingness", since such models have absorbed human notions of what's
  interesting. This improved open-ended learning over sampling tasks uniformly or by learning
  progress alone.
- Bearing: curiosity by design, borrowed from human culture. A neat fit with Gopnik's
  cultural-technology view.

**4.2** Wang, G., Xie, Y., Jiang, Y., Mandlekar, A., Xiao, C., Zhu, Y., Fan, L., & Anandkumar, A.
(2023). Voyager: An open-ended embodied agent with large language models. arXiv:2305.16291
(_TMLR_ 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16291 **[abstract]**
- An automatic curriculum proposes progressively harder tasks, based on the agent's exploration
  progress and state, alongside a skill library and iterative prompting with GPT-4. It reported
  many more unique items and much faster progress than earlier methods. The exact multiples
  differ between versions of the abstract, so they are not quoted here.
- Bearing: a deployed design in which the system chooses its own next experience, the thing
  Gopnik credits to children.

## Gaps and cautions

- **No study found that tests unprompted information-seeking in today's assistant products.**
  Bart's observation is first-hand, and Krishnamurthy et al. is the nearest controlled evidence.
- **"Explained by normal reasoning" is an interpretation.** Separating curiosity from goal-directed
  information-seeking is exactly what Kidd & Hayden say is hard, even in people.
- **Schmidhuber 1991** is verified only by record and secondary description.
