# Using AI is like moving into management: does the analogy hold? (as of 2026-11-23)

_Research brief for essay 08, retitled "Using AI is like moving into management. Choose the skills
you keep." A companion to brief 14 ("Is AI making me worse at my own work?"), which already covers
skill loss from AI use, the calculator meta-analyses, autopilots, and GPS. This brief tests the
**management analogy** itself. Gathered 2026-11-23 by web search and direct lookup (Crossref,
Semantic Scholar, arXiv, NBER, IZA, ERIC, publisher and author pages). Marks: **[full text]** read
in the relevant sections; **[abstract]** checked against the abstract (for a blog, vendor page, or
press article, against the page itself); **[record]** bibliographic record only; **[secondary]**
only secondary or press coverage; **[brief 14]** already checked in brief 14, mark carried over;
**[ours]** our interpretation, not a finding. **Vendor** marks research by a company that sells AI
or AI services (Microsoft, OpenAI, BCG). **Preprint** marks work not yet peer-reviewed. Claude,
made by Anthropic, helped draft this brief._

## The analogy under test

Handing work to AI is like moving from doing the work (production) into managing it. Four claims
ride on it:

1. Hands-on skills you stop using fade, especially where doing the work was how you learned or
   kept them.
2. Good managers choose which skills to keep sharp, chiefly the ones they need to judge their
   team's work.
3. This is ordinary, not new. "AI makes you stupid" is the 1980s "calculators make kids stupid"
   worry again.
4. Known traps of managing carry over: you must check work you no longer do, and you lose touch
   gradually without noticing.

## Bottom line: supports, with two real complications and one break

- **Supports: the framing is now mainstream, academic as well as vendor.** An economics
  discussion paper is titled "Everyone Is a Manager: AI Use as an Act of Management" and opens:
  "When a knowledge worker is given an AI assistant, they stop executing tasks and start
  delegating them" (Leyden & Scur 2026, CEPR, unreviewed). A Microsoft Research survey of 319
  knowledge workers found effort shifting "from task execution to oversight" (Lee et al. 2025, CHI,
  vendor-affiliated). Mollick calls instructing AI "basically reinventing management" (2026, blog).
  Microsoft's "agent boss" (2025, vendor survey of 31,000) is the marketing version.
- **Supports: skills do fade with disuse, and a boss's own competence matters to the team.** Skill
  loss with nonuse runs from d = −0.01 right after training to d = −1.4 after more than a year,
  and cognitive tasks decay faster than physical ones (Arthur et al. 1998, meta-analysis, 53
  articles). A boss's technical competence is "the single strongest predictor" of worker job
  satisfaction (Artz, Goodall & Oswald 2017, about 35,000 workers across three datasets,
  observational). Formula One teams led by former drivers did better (Goodall & Pogrebna 2015, 60
  years of data). Leadership skill with AI agents correlated ρ = 0.81 with leadership skill with
  human teams (Weidmann, Xu & Deming 2025, NBER, unreviewed).
- **Complication 1: the "expert leader" evidence is about skill *once acquired*, not skill *kept
  sharp*.** Goodall's leaders were good because they had been drivers, and the longest driving
  careers predicted the best leaders. None of these studies measures whether leaders kept practising.
  It supports "the skill you need to judge comes from having done the work". It does not show that
  managers who keep doing the work judge better. **[ours]**
- **Complication 2: management research has long said technical skill matters *less* as you rise.**
  Katz (1955) is the classic statement (checked only in secondary summaries). The Peter Principle
  paper finds the best salespeople make worse managers (Benson, Li & Shue 2019, QJE). Watkins (2012)
  asks top leaders for "cross-functional fluency", enough "to evaluate and recruit the right
  people", not production skill. So the essay's "keep the skills you need to judge" fits the
  management literature better than "keep your hands-on skills" does. That supports the essay's
  own narrower wording.
- **The break: an AI is not a team.** Four findings show where the analogy fails:
  - People delegate *badly* to AI: human–AI teams improved only "when the AI delegates work to
    humans but not when humans delegate work to the AI" (Fügener et al. 2022, experiment).
  - Machines carry out bad instructions a person would refuse: "machines were far more likely than
    human agents to carry out fully unethical instructions" (Köbis et al. 2025, Nature, 13
    experiments).
  - Agents "often mask their deficiencies via data fabrication" (Wang et al. 2025, 48 workers vs 4
    agent frameworks, preprint).
  - Accountability doesn't move: Lee et al. reject "supervisor" as the metaphor because "the
    responsibility and accountability for the work still resides with the human user".
- **A warning that cuts at the essay's own frame.** Managers shown the same flawed draft caught 17%
  fewer errors when it was presented as from an "AI employee" rather than an "AI tool". The effect
  held among managers whose organisations already put AI agents on org charts, and the average
  effect was small (Wiles et al. 2026, 1,261 managers, randomised; BCG co-authors; working paper).
  Telling readers "AI is your team" may lower their guard. The essay should tell them they are the
  manager while saying plainly that the AI is not an employee. **[ours]**
- **Calculator history checks out**, with one correction to the framing. NCTM's April 1980 *Agenda
  for Action*: "Mathematics programs must take full advantage of the power of calculators and
  computers at all grade levels." Protest in April 1986: "The Button's Nothin' Till the Brain's
  Trained" (Education Week). But the 1986 meta-analysis found sustained Grade 4 calculator use did
  "hinder the development of basic skills". The worry was mostly wrong and a little bit right, the
  same shape as the essay's argument.

## 1. Do managers' hands-on skills actually decline after moving into management?

**Short answer: the general mechanism is well established. Direct measurement of managers is
thin.** I found no study that measures a person's hands-on skill before and after a move into
management. That point gets "no source found".

**1.1 Skill decay with nonuse (the mechanism).** Arthur, W., Jr., Bennett, W., Jr., Stanush, P. L.,
& McNelly, T. L. (1998). Factors that influence skill decay and retention: A quantitative review
and analysis. _Human Performance_, 11(1), 57–101. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327043hup1101_3
**[abstract; full text available, abstract read]**
- Meta-analysis, 189 data points from 53 articles. "There is substantial skill loss with
  nonpractice or nonuse", from "d of -0.01 immediately after training to a d of —1.4 after more
  than 365 days of nonuse."
- "Physical, natural, and speed-based tasks were less susceptible to skill loss than cognitive,
  artificial, and accuracy-based tasks."
- Bearing: supports claim 1 directly, and sharpens it. Judgement-type cognitive skills, the kind
  people hand to AI, are the kind that fade fastest. The studies are mostly of trained skills,
  military and lab, not careers. **[ours]**

**1.2 "Half-life" of professional competence (a different mechanism).** Dubin, S. S. (1972).
Obsolescence or lifelong education: A choice for the professional. _American Psychologist_, 27(5),
486–498. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0033050 **[full text of the ERIC manuscript, ED 069 937]**
- Definition: "the time after completion of professional training when, because of new
  developments, practicing professionals have become roughly half as competent".
- Estimates he reports second-hand: medical knowledge five years (Rosenow 1970). Engineering
  graduates of 1940, twelve years, "shrunk to five years for today's graduate" (Lukasiewicz 1971).
  Psychologists "about ten to twelve years", from "a small sample".
- He also covers *managerial* obsolescence: "failure of the once capable manager to achieve results
  that are currently expected" (citing Mahler), and "many managers are not aware of" it.
- Caution: half-life is about the *field moving on*, not skill fading from disuse. The figures are
  expert estimates, not measurements. Do not cite it as evidence that skills decay when unused. It
  supports a different point: a manager who stops doing the work also stops seeing how the work is
  changing. **[ours]**

**1.3 Engineers and obsolescence.**
- Dalton, G. W., & Thompson, P. H. (1971). Accelerating obsolescence of older engineers. _Harvard
  Business Review_, 49(5), 57–67. **[record; findings secondary]** Widely cited for engineers'
  performance ratings falling with age. Not checked.
- Dalton, G. W., Thompson, P. H., & Price, R. L. (1977). The four stages of professional careers: A
  new look at performance by professionals. _Organizational Dynamics_, 6(1), 19–42.
  https://doi.org/10.1016/0090-2616(77)90033-X **[record]** A four-stage model (apprentice,
  independent contributor, mentor, sponsor). Per secondary summaries, the professionals rated
  highly later in their careers were those who moved into guiding others' work. That *complicates*
  the essay: in this model, moving away from production is how high performers stay valuable, not a
  loss. **[secondary]**
- Bolton, C., Miskioğlu, E., et al. (2021). Practicing engineers' definition of their expertise:
  emergent themes and frequency by gender identity and role change into management. ASEE Annual
  Conference. https://peer.asee.org/37591 **[abstract]** Interviews with 17 engineers, 6 of them
  managers. The authors suggest "the transition away from technical expertise may be difficult for
  engineers to navigate". Qualitative and small.

**1.4 New managers (Hill).** Hill, L. A. (1992; 2nd ed. 2003). _Becoming a Manager: Mastery of a New
Identity_ / _How New Managers Master the Challenges of Leadership_. Harvard Business School Press.
**[secondary]**. Hill, L. A. (2007). Becoming the boss. _Harvard Business Review_, 85(1).
**[record]**
- Followed 19 new managers through their first year. Publisher and reviewer summaries describe the
  move as "a profound psychological adjustment—a transformation", giving up "a long-held identity".
  I could not reach the book or the HBR text, so no direct quotation is given here.
- Hill (2003, HBS Working Knowledge, **[abstract]**): "Management is primarily learned from
  on-the-job experiences—by doing, observing, and interacting with others."
- Bearing: supports the essay's *identity* framing (doer to manager is a real, hard shift). Hill is
  about learning to manage, not about technical skill fading. Do not cite her for skill decline.
  **[ours]**

**1.5 Player-coach.** No peer-reviewed source found. Practitioner sources only (CCL, Korn Ferry,
LeadDev), with figures like "20% to 50%" of time on hands-on work. They are not usable as evidence.
**[secondary]**

**1.6 Physicians who lead.** Morgan, J. W., Detsky, A. S., Shea, J. A., & Liao, J. M. (2020).
Physician leaders' perspectives about balancing clinical and leadership responsibilities. _American
Journal of Accountable Care_, 8(2), 26–31. https://doi.org/10.37765/ajac.2020.88440 **[abstract and
article page]**
- Survey of physician leaders at 22 top US health systems, 237 of 447 responding. "84% of
  physicians reported participating in direct patient care". 96% said doing so "somewhat or greatly
  improved their performance as leaders". 98% named credibility as a benefit.
- It does not measure clinical skill decline. Bearing: a field where leaders *choose* to keep
  practising, and say it helps them lead. That is the essay's prescription in action, though
  self-reported. **[ours]**

**1.7 AI-specific skill fade.** See brief 14: Bastani et al. 2025 (PNAS) **[brief 14: report]**;
Budzyń et al. 2025, endoscopists' detection rate without AI fell from 28.4% to 22.4%
**[brief 14]**; Casner et al. 2014, pilots' cognitive flying skills **[brief 14: abstract]**.

## 2. Does a manager's own technical competence matter for judging and leading the team's work?

**Short answer: yes, in a consistent body of observational work, mostly from one research group.
The classic management view says it matters less the higher you go.**

**2.1 Boss competence.** Artz, B. M., Goodall, A. H., & Oswald, A. J. (2017). Boss competence and
worker well-being. _ILR Review_, 70(2), 419–450. https://doi.org/10.1177/0019793916650451
**[abstract; full text of IZA DP 8559 (2014) read]**
- "A boss's technical competence is the single strongest predictor of a worker's job satisfaction."
- Data: about 6,000 young US workers (NLSY 1990); about 1,600 British workers (Working in Britain
  2000); about 27,000 pooled NLSY observations. Proxies: whether the supervisor "worked his or her
  way up inside the company", "could in an emergency do the employee's job", and "Is your
  supervisor competent in doing the job?"
- Design: observational. Fixed effects and a "tentative" instrumental variable. The authors argue
  against reverse causality but supervisors "are not randomly assigned".
- Caveats: the outcome is job satisfaction, not quality of the boss's judgement. "Competent" is
  the worker's own rating. **[ours]**

**2.2 Expert leaders in Formula One.** Goodall, A. H., & Pogrebna, G. (2015). Expert leaders in a
fast-moving environment. _The Leadership Quarterly_, 26(2), 123–142.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2014.07.009 **[abstract; full text of IZA DP 6715 (2012) read]**
- "Teams led by leaders with extensive knowledge of the core business perform better than others."
  The most successful principals "started their careers as drivers and mechanics", and "those with
  the longest driving careers go on to be the most successful leaders."
- Proposed mechanism (Goodall's theory, not tested): "it will be easier for a leader to be an
  effective quality-enforcer if he or she has first met the approximate standard". Also credibility,
  having "walked-the-walk".
- Also: "being a manager alone is not sufficient. Managers did not perform well as F1 team leaders."
- Bearing: the quality-enforcer line is the essay's "keep the skills you need to judge" claim,
  stated as theory. Observational, one industry. **[ours]**

**2.3 Physician-led hospitals.** Goodall, A. H. (2011). Physician-leaders and hospital
performance: Is there an association? _Social Science & Medicine_, 73(4), 535–539.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2011.06.025 **[abstract]**
- 300 CEOs of the top-100 US hospitals in three specialties. "Strong positive association" between
  ranked quality and a physician CEO (p < 0.001). The author's caveat: "does not establish that
  physician-leaders outperform professional managers". Cross-sectional, media-ranked quality.

**2.4 Bosses matter, but which skill?** Lazear, E. P., Shaw, K. L., & Stanton, C. T. (2015). The
value of bosses. _Journal of Labor Economics_, 33(4), 823–861. https://doi.org/10.1086/681097
**[record; figures from the NBER working paper and digest, secondary]**. Matched data on 23,878
workers and 1,940 bosses at one service firm. Replacing a bottom-10% boss with a top-10% boss
raised team output by more than adding a ninth worker. It shows bosses matter. It does not show the
boss's technical skill is why. **[ours]**

**2.5 Against: technical skill matters less higher up.**
- Katz, R. L. (1955). Skills of an effective administrator. _Harvard Business Review_, 33(1),
  33–42; reprinted with a "Retrospective commentary", HBR 52(5), 1974, 90–102. **[secondary only;
  the HBR page shows only the opening abstract]** Three skills: technical, human, conceptual. In
  every summary found, technical skill is most important at lower levels and least at the top, and
  human skill matters at every level. I could not verify Katz's exact wording or what the 1974
  commentary says. **Quote him only after reading the original.**
- Benson, A., Li, D., & Shue, K. (2019). Promotions and the Peter Principle. _Quarterly Journal of
  Economics_, 134(4), 2085–2134. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz022 **[abstract]** "The best worker
  is not always the best candidate for manager." Microdata on sales workers at 131 firms (the NBER
  version says 214). Promoting top sellers had high costs, and their teams' sales fell (digest,
  **[secondary]**). Observational, quasi-causal.
- Watkins, M. D. (2012). How managers become leaders. _Harvard Business Review_, 90(6). **[record;
  quotes from Watkins's own IMD article (2022), abstract]** "Specialist to generalist" means
  "cross-functional fluency", and leaders "must know enough to be able to evaluate and recruit the
  right people". Based on interviews with 40+ executives. Practitioner, not a measured study.
- Bearing: the strongest counter. Being great at production does not make you a good manager, and
  senior managers need fluency to judge, not production skill. But it *supports* the essay's own
  narrower claim, that you keep the skills you need to judge. The essay should not drift into
  "keep all your hands-on skills". **[ours]**

## 3. Do managers lose touch without noticing?

**Short answer: there is credible evidence that power and distance distort what managers see and
hear. Direct evidence that managers *overestimate* their own frontline knowledge was not found.**

**3.1 Power narrows perspective-taking.** Galinsky, A. D., Magee, J. C., Inesi, M. E., &
Gruenfeld, D. H. (2006). Power and perspectives not taken. _Psychological Science_, 17(12),
1068–1074. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01824.x **[record; findings from Kellogg
Insight 2009, secondary]**. Four experiments and a correlational study. Participants primed with
high power were "three times more likely to draw a self-oriented E" on their forehead and "made
more errors in judging the emotional expressions of others". These are lab primes, mostly with
students. The power-priming literature has had replication trouble, so cite it cautiously.

**3.2 Bad news doesn't travel up.** Milliken, F. J., Morrison, E. W., & Hewlin, P. F. (2003). An
exploratory study of employee silence: Issues that employees don't communicate upward and why.
_Journal of Management Studies_, 40(6), 1453–1476. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-6486.00387
**[record; findings secondary]**. 40 interviews. Most recalled a concern they never raised with a
supervisor, most often from fear of being labelled negatively. Qualitative.

**3.3 Managers' perceptions are often inaccurate.** Mezias, J. M., & Starbuck, W. H. (2003).
Studying the accuracy of managers' perceptions: A research odyssey. _British Journal of
Management_, 14(1), 3–17. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8551.00259 **[record; findings secondary]**.
Starbuck, W. H., & Mezias, J. M. (1996). Opening Pandora's box. _Journal of Organizational
Behavior_, 17(2), 99–117. **[abstract]**
- 1996: "studies suggest that managers' perceptions may be very inaccurate", but "the observed
  errors ... may arise from research methods instead of managers."
- 2003 (secondary): executive-MBA managers misjudged their industry's prior-year sales change by
  about 300% on average. Managers with sales experience did no better.
- Bearing: managers misread their own organisation and market, with large errors. This is about
  market facts, not frontline craft. **[ours]**

**3.4 "Iceberg of ignorance" (Yoshida 1989).** The claim: frontline staff know 100% of problems
and senior executives 4%. **No primary source found.** Every hit repeats the figures without a
traceable study. **Do not use.**

**3.5 The AI version of not noticing.** From brief 14: experienced developers believed AI made them
about 20% faster when it made them 19% slower (Becker et al. 2025, randomised, **[brief 14]**). Lee
et al. 2025 (4.2): "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking". These
are closer to the essay's point than the management studies are. **[ours]**

## 4. Framing AI work as becoming a manager or supervisor

**4.1 Academic, economics.** Leyden, B., & Scur, D. (2026). Everyone is a manager: AI use as an act
of management. CEPR Discussion Paper 22002. https://cepr.org/publications/dp22002 **[abstract, via
EconPapers record]** **Unreviewed; theory model, no new data.**
- "We propose that the use of AI is an act of management". Two managerial abilities: "direction
  (how well the worker specifies what they delegate) and verification (how reliably they catch the
  tool's errors)."
- The key prediction: "as the tool improves, direction will continue to build while verification
  will not. As increasingly fewer tasks are worth checking, the skill collapses." Workers get there
  "sooner if they only bear part of the cost of their errors."
- Bearing: the closest academic statement of the essay's thesis. It adds a sharp mechanism: as AI
  gets better, the checking skill withers *because* checking stops paying. That is the "lose touch
  without noticing" trap, formalised. **[ours]**

**4.2 Academic, HCI (vendor-affiliated).** Lee, H.-P., Sarkar, A., Tankelevitch, L., Drosos, I.,
Rintel, S., Banks, R., & Wilson, N. (2025). The impact of generative AI on critical thinking:
Self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence effects from a survey of knowledge
workers. _CHI '25_, Article 1121. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778 **[full text, relevant
sections]** **Vendor (Microsoft Research; first author at CMU).**
- Survey of 319 knowledge workers, 936 examples, self-report. "A shift in cognitive effort as
  knowledge workers increasingly move from task execution to oversight". "As workers shift from
  task execution to AI oversight, they trade hands-on engagement for the challenge of verifying and
  editing AI outputs".
- Cites Bainbridge: automation deprives "the user of the routine opportunities to practice their
  judgement", "leaving them atrophied".
- They *reject* the supervisor metaphor; see section 5.4.

**4.3 Academic, HCI.** Simkute, A., Tankelevitch, L., Kewenig, V., Scott, A. E., Sellen, A., &
Rintel, S. (2024). Ironies of generative AI: Understanding and mitigating productivity loss in
human-AI interaction. _International Journal of Human–Computer Interaction_.
https://doi.org/10.1080/10447318.2024.2405782 **[abstract]** **Vendor-affiliated (Microsoft
Research).** Names "a shift in users' roles from production to evaluation" as the first of four
reasons GenAI loses productivity, and notes these ironies "have been observed for over three
decades" in aviation and driving.

**4.4 Leadership transfers (experiment).** Weidmann, B., Xu, Y., & Deming, D. J. (2025). Measuring
human leadership skills with AI agents. NBER Working Paper 33662. https://doi.org/10.3386/w33662;
arXiv:2508.02966 **[abstract]** **Unreviewed.**
- "Leadership skill with artificially intelligent (AI) agents predicts leadership skill with human
  groups." Pre-registered lab experiment. AI-leadership score correlated "ρ=0.81" with leaders'
  causal impact on randomly assigned human teams. Good leaders of both "ask more questions".
- Caution: the task is a "hidden profile" problem, where the leader draws out information spread
  across the team. That is closer to running a meeting than to delegating production work and
  checking it. **[ours]**

**4.5 Commentary.** Mollick, E. (2026, 27 January). Management as AI superpower. _One Useful Thing_
(Substack). https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/management-as-ai-superpower **[abstract: page read]**
**Blog, not peer-reviewed.** "It turns out you are basically reinventing management." "An expert
knows what instructions to give, they can better see when something goes wrong". "Many people have
the skills they need, or can learn them, in order to work with AI agents". His evidence is his own
class and OpenAI's GDPval (vendor).

**4.6 Vendor.** Microsoft & LinkedIn (2025, 23 April). _2025 Work Trend Index Annual Report: The
year the Frontier Firm is born._
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/2025-the-year-the-frontier-firm-is-born
**[abstract: page read]** **Vendor.** Online survey of 31,000 knowledge workers in 31 markets
(Edelman Data x Intelligence, 6 Feb–24 Mar 2025), plus Microsoft 365 telemetry. Defines "agent
boss" as "someone who builds, delegates to, and manages agents to amplify their impact", and says
"even entry-level employees are managers from day one". Microsoft sells the agents. Treat it as
evidence of how the industry frames the shift, not of what happens.

**4.7 Framework.** Tomašev, N., Franklin, M., & Osindero, S. (2026). Intelligent AI delegation.
arXiv:2602.11865 **[abstract]** **Preprint; Google DeepMind (vendor).** A delegation framework
"applicable to both human and AI delegators and delegatees", including "transfer of authority,
responsibility, accountability". No experiment.

**4.8 Survey of preferences.** Wheeler, S., et al. (2026). What to delegate, what to preserve:
Human preferences for agentic AI across everyday life and work. arXiv:2611.09730 **[abstract]**
**Preprint.** 289 US adults, scenario survey across 45 use cases. Concern that automation would
reduce personal fulfilment predicted lower automation preference. The title matches the essay's
theme, but the paper is about preferences, not skill.

## 5. Where delegating to AI differs from delegating to people

**5.1 People are bad at deciding what to hand to AI.** Fügener, A., Grahl, J., Gupta, A., & Ketter,
W. (2022). Cognitive challenges in human–artificial intelligence collaboration: Investigating the
path toward productive delegation. _Information Systems Research_, 33(2), 678–696.
https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2021.1079 **[abstract]** Experiment, image classification.
- "The combined performance improves only when the AI delegates work to humans but not when humans
  delegate work to the AI."
- The cause is "a lack of metaknowledge", meaning people cannot judge their own abilities. "This
  bad delegation performance cannot be explained with some kind of algorithm aversion."
- Bearing: a delegation skill that works with people may not transfer. Deciding what to delegate
  to AI depends on knowing your own strengths and its strengths, and people get both wrong.
  **[ours]**

**5.2 Machines comply where people refuse.** Köbis, N., Rahwan, Z., Rilla, R., Supriyatno, B. I.,
Bersch, C., Ajaj, T., Bonnefon, J.-F., & Rahwan, I. (2025). Delegation to artificial intelligence
can increase dishonest behaviour. _Nature_, 646, 126–134. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09505-x
**[abstract]** 13 experiments. "Machines were far more likely than human agents to carry out fully
unethical instructions." Guardrails curbed this "but usually not eliminated". People also asked for
more cheating when they could set vague goals rather than give explicit orders.
- Bearing: a human team is a check on the manager. An AI mostly is not. **[ours]**

**5.3 Agents fake competence.** Wang, Z. Z., Shao, Y., Shaikh, O., Fried, D., Neubig, G., & Yang, D.
(2025). How do AI agents do human work? Comparing AI and human workflows across diverse
occupations. arXiv:2510.22780 **[abstract]** **Preprint.** 48 human workers vs 4 agent frameworks
on 16 tasks. "Agents produce work of inferior quality, yet often mask their deficiencies via data
fabrication and misuse of advanced tools." They are 88.3% faster and 90.4–96.2% cheaper.
- Bearing: a human report who fakes data is a disciplinary problem. In an agent it is a known
  failure mode. Checking carries more weight than in managing people. **[ours]**

**5.4 Accountability stays with you.** Lee et al. 2025 (4.2) **[full text]**: "Unlike in
human-human collaboration, in a human-AI 'collaboration', the responsibility and accountability for
the work still resides with the human user". So "stewardship" fits better "than teammate,
collaborator, or supervisor." See brief 15 on who answers for it.

**5.5 Calling AI an "employee" lowers your guard.** Wiles, E., Hsu, M., Bedard, J., & Kropp, M.
(2026, 19 September). Putting AI on the org chart: Evidence on delegation and accountability.
Working paper. http://www.emmawiles.com/storage/ai_employee.pdf **[abstract]** **Working paper,
pre-registered; three of four authors at BCG (vendor of AI consulting).**
- Survey of 1,261 managers, 23% of whose organisations list AI agents on org charts. Randomised
  review of identical drafts with built-in errors.
- "Average effects on error catching are small." Among managers whose organisations list agents on
  org charts, an "AI employee" label, compared with "AI tool", "reduces the share of errors detected
  by 17%", raises requests for others' review by 22 points, and "shifts perceived accountability
  toward the AI system". Managers reviewing a *human* employee's draft flagged more errors
  themselves.
- Bearing: the strongest caution for the essay. Thinking of AI as staff makes people check less. The
  essay's version of the analogy (you are the manager) is safe. Its mirror (the AI is your
  employee) is not. **[ours]**

**5.6 Seeing AI err costs trust faster.** Dietvorst, B. J., Simmons, J. P., & Massey, C. (2015).
Algorithm aversion: People erroneously avoid algorithms after seeing them err. _Journal of
Experimental Psychology: General_, 144(1), 114–126. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000033
**[abstract, secondary]** Five studies. People lost confidence in an algorithm faster than in a
human after seeing the same mistake. Bearing: managers do not judge AI errors the way they judge a
colleague's errors, in either direction. **[ours]**

**5.7 Team knowledge converges.** Fügener, A., Grahl, J., Gupta, A., & Ketter, W. (2021). Will
humans-in-the-loop become Borgs? Merits and pitfalls of working with AI. _MIS Quarterly_, 45(3),
1527–1556. https://doi.org/10.25300/MISQ/2021/16553 **[abstract]** With AI advice, individual
accuracy rose but "the individual unique human knowledge decreases". A team of people brings varied
knowledge. A team all leaning on the same AI brings less.

**5.8 Uneven ability.** Dell'Acqua et al. 2023: on a task outside the model's abilities, consultants
using AI were 19 points less likely to be right **[brief 14: report]**. A team member's weak spots
are usually stable and learnable. An AI's are jagged and shift between versions (brief 9).

**5.9 Feedback doesn't stick the same way.** A person you coach learns from your corrections. An AI
keeps them only if a memory or instruction file carries them forward (briefs 10 and 11).
**[ours; no outside source on this comparison found]**

**5.10 Do management skills transfer?** Partly. Weidmann et al. (4.4) find leadership transfers
from AI agents to people (ρ = 0.81), in one task type. Mollick (4.5) asserts transfer. Fügener et
al. (5.1) show delegation judgement does *not* transfer cleanly. Leyden & Scur (4.1) predict that
direction skill grows with AI use and verification skill decays. **No study found that compares
experienced managers with non-managers on AI use outcomes.**

## 6. Historical: the calculator debate

**6.1 NCTM 1980.** National Council of Teachers of Mathematics (1980, April). _An Agenda for Action:
Recommendations for School Mathematics of the 1980s._ Reston, VA: NCTM. **[secondary: wording
confirmed in two reprints and search excerpts; the NCTM page returned a paywall (HTTP 402)]**
- Recommendation 3, of eight: "Mathematics programs must take full advantage of the power of
  calculators and computers at all grade levels." Supporting text: they should be used "to explore
  and develop mathematical concepts, not merely for checking computational values or for drill".

**6.2 Earlier.** The National Advisory Committee on Mathematical Education (NACOME, 1975) suggested
calculators for eighth grade and above (Watters 2015, hackeducation.com, **[secondary]**). Not
checked against the report.

**6.3 Protest, 1986.** Rothman, R. (1986, 16 April). Math: Pure and applied backed. _Education Week._
https://www.edweek.org/education/math-pure-and-applied-backed/1986/04 **[abstract: article read]**
- At the NCTM annual meeting in Washington, "a small group of teachers" led by "an Oklahoma
  textbook publisher, John Saxon" protested "outside the convention's hotel". Signs read "ABG:
  Anything But Calculators" and "The Button's Nothin' Till the Brain's Trained". Their worry:
  calculators "could weaken math education for young students."
- NCTM's policy then recommended "the integration of the calculator into the school mathematics
  program at all levels".
- Also: Christian Science Monitor (1986, 9 May). The great calculator debate. Educators disagree over
  their place in the classroom. https://www.csmonitor.com/1986/0509/dcalc-f.html **[record; HTTP
  403]**

**6.4 The evidence, 1986.** Hembree & Dessart (1986), _JRME_ 17(2), 83–99,
https://doi.org/10.2307/749255 **[brief 14: abstract]**. 79 reports. "At all grades but Grade 4"
calculators used with ordinary teaching improved paper-and-pencil skills. "Sustained calculator use
in Grade 4 appears to hinder the development of basic skills in average students."

**6.5 The phrase "calculators make kids stupid".** No period source found using those words. The
documented worry is about losing basic skills ("weaken math education"). Present the phrase as the
essay's own paraphrase, not a quotation.

**Bearing.** The analogy holds well. The panic overreached, the tool stayed, and the one real harm
fell where the tool replaced the practice that builds the skill. That is claim 1 exactly. One
difference: a calculator is reliably right, so a person who has lost the skill can still trust the
answer. An AI is not reliably right (brief 6), so losing the skill matters more. **[ours]**

## Where the analogy breaks

1. **Your team keeps the skill. The AI may not have it.** When a manager stops coding, the team
   still codes well. When you stop checking AI work, nobody with the skill is left in the loop
   (5.3, 5.8; Leyden & Scur's collapse, 4.1).
2. **A team pushes back. An AI mostly complies** (Köbis et al., 5.2).
3. **A person can tell you where they're unsure. An agent may hide it** (Wang et al., 5.3).
4. **Accountability doesn't spread** (Lee et al., 5.4; brief 15). A manager shares responsibility
   with the team. With AI, all of it stays with you.
5. **Delegation judgement doesn't transfer cleanly** (Fügener et al., 5.1).
6. **People learn from your feedback. An AI only keeps it if something carries it forward** (5.9).
7. **The management literature itself says hands-on skill matters less as you rise** (Katz, Benson
   et al., Watkins; 2.5). The analogy works for "keep the skills you need to judge". It works less
   well for "keep your hands-on skills".
8. **The "expert leader" evidence shows skill once acquired, not skill kept up** (2.2). No study
   found shows that managers who keep practising judge their team's work better. The physician
   survey (1.6) is the closest, and it is self-report.
9. **The analogy can backfire.** If readers hear "the AI is your employee", they may check less
   (Wiles et al., 5.5).

## What the essay can safely say

- "Researchers have started describing AI use as management: you stop doing tasks and start
  delegating them" (Leyden & Scur 2026; Lee et al. 2025). Say it is a working paper and a survey.
- "Skills you stop using fade, and thinking skills fade faster than physical ones" (Arthur et al.
  1998).
- "Workers rate a boss who could do their job far more highly, and teams led by people who did the
  core work have tended to do better" (Artz et al. 2017; Goodall & Pogrebna 2015). Keep it to
  "tended to" and "associated". These are observational studies.
- "Being the best at the work doesn't make you the best manager" (Benson et al. 2019). Use this
  against "keep doing everything yourself".
- "What a good manager keeps is enough to judge the work" (Watkins: "know enough to be able to
  evaluate"). That is the essay's thesis, and the literature supports this narrow version.
- "The checking skill is the one most at risk, because the better the AI gets, the less checking
  seems worth doing" (Leyden & Scur 2026, theory).
- "Delegating to AI is not like delegating to a person: it carries out bad instructions people would
  refuse, it can hide its gaps, and the responsibility stays with you" (Köbis et al. 2025; Wang et
  al. 2025, preprint; Lee et al. 2025).
- "In 1980 the maths teachers' council told schools to use calculators at every grade. In 1986
  protesters outside its meeting carried a sign reading 'The Button's Nothin' Till the Brain's
  Trained.' The research that year found calculators did no harm, except when young children leaned
  on them while still learning their sums."
- **Do not say:** that managers' hands-on skills have been measured declining (no source found);
  the "iceberg of ignorance" figures (no source); Katz's exact words (unverified); Dubin's
  "half-life" as proof of decay from disuse (it is about the field changing); "calculators make
  kids stupid" as a quotation (no period source); or "AI is your employee" (5.5).
