# Is AI making me worse at my own work? Skill, dependence, and handing over the thinking (as of 2026-10-19)

## The claim under test

The question a reader asks: Is AI making me worse at my own work? Put more fully: what happens to
my skills, and to my thinking, when I hand parts of my work to an assistant?

Working claim, to test rather than defend: **"Handing a task to AI costs you the skill only where
the doing was the learning; elsewhere it is ordinary delegation, the same trade people have always
made with tools and colleagues — so the question is which parts of your work you can afford to
stop practising."**

The claim has three parts, tested separately below:
1. *Handing over a task costs the skill only where the doing was the learning* (Q1, Q2, Q4).
2. *Elsewhere it is ordinary delegation, the same trade as with tools and colleagues* (Q5).
3. *So the real question is which parts of your work you can afford to stop practising* (Q3, Q6).

The yardstick from essays 00 and 01 applies: judge by role, not category; ask "compared to
what?", meaning whoever or whatever would otherwise fill the role, as they actually are; keep the
known separate from the unknown; describe it accurately even when a warmer story sells better.
Here "compared to what?" has two answers, and the brief uses both: the older tools people have
handed thinking to (writing, calculators, search, GPS, autopilots), and the colleague or junior
you would otherwise hand the task to.

## Bottom line

- **The core of Part 1 holds, and it is the best-supported finding here.** When people *learning*
  a skill let AI do the work, they perform better while they have it and learn less.
  - Nearly a thousand high-school maths students with an unrestricted GPT-4 tutor did better on
    practice problems (grades up 48%), then did 17% worse than students who never had it once it
    was taken away (Bastani et al. 2025, PNAS).
  - Developers learning a new programming library with an AI assistant scored lower on a
    comprehension quiz afterwards (50% against 67%), with no significant time saving (Shen &
    Tamkin 2026, Anthropic authors, preprint).
  - Students who used a model to produce solutions covered more topics but understood each less;
    those who asked it for explanations understood more (Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting 2024,
    preprint).

  The mechanism is old and well established: what you generate, retrieve and struggle with, you
  keep (Bjork 1994; Slamecka & Graf 1978; Bertsch et al. 2007; Roediger & Karpicke 2006).
- **But "only where the doing was the learning" is too narrow. Skills you already have can fade
  too, when the doing was also the practice that kept them.**
  - Experienced endoscopists' detection rate on colonoscopies done *without* AI fell from 28.4%
    to 22.4% in the three months after AI was introduced at their centres (Budzyń et al. 2025;
    observational, contested).
  - Airline pilots kept their hand-flying skills under automation but showed problems with the
    *thinking* tasks of manual flight, linked to how often their minds wandered while the
    automation flew (Casner et al. 2014, 16 pilots).
  - Bainbridge's 1983 "Ironies of automation" said it first: skills "deteriorate when they are not
    used", and an operator's working knowledge "develops only through use and feedback about its
    effectiveness".

  A fairer Part 1: **handing over a task costs you the skill where the doing was how you learned
  it, or how you kept it.** **[ours]**
- **Handing over changes the thinking as well as the skill.** In a survey of 319 knowledge workers
  (936 examples), higher confidence in AI went with less critical thinking, and higher confidence
  in oneself with more. The thinking that remained moved toward checking, combining and
  overseeing the AI's output (Lee et al. 2025, Microsoft Research authors; self-report). Students
  researching with ChatGPT reported less mental effort and produced weaker reasoning than students
  using Google (Stadler, Bannert & Sailer 2024).
- **The most-quoted alarm is the weakest evidence.** The MIT "Your Brain on ChatGPT" EEG study
  (Kosmyna et al. 2025) is a preprint, not peer-reviewed as of its project page, with 54
  participants and only 18 in the session that tested what happens afterwards. Its own authors
  ask the press not to say "brain rot", "harm" or "damage", and a published comment raises
  concerns about its sample, its EEG analysis and its reporting (Stanković et al. 2025). The
  correlational survey often cited beside it (Gerlich 2025) cannot show cause.
- **Compared to what? The older tools mostly support Part 2, with the same exception.**
  - Calculators used alongside ordinary teaching did not harm basic skills at most grades, but
    sustained use in Grade 4, while those skills were being formed, did (Hembree & Dessart 1986,
    79 studies).
  - Heavier GPS users have worse spatial memory, and in a small three-year follow-up their memory
    declined faster (Dahmani & Bohbot 2020). London taxi drivers who navigated from memory had
    larger posterior hippocampi (Maguire et al. 2000).
  - The famous "Google effect" on memory (Sparrow, Liu & Wegner 2011) failed to replicate in the
    part that was retested (Camerer et al. 2018; Hesselmann 2020).
  - Plato's Socrates made the same complaint about writing: "this invention will produce
    forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it" (Phaedrus 275a, Fowler translation).

  The pattern: a tool costs the specific skill it replaces, most when it replaces the practice
  that forms the skill. Panics about general decline have tended to overreach. **[ours]**
- **Where AI differs from the older tools, and from a colleague.** **[ours, from the sources
  cited]**
  - It does judgement and reasoning, not only storage or arithmetic, and across almost any task,
    so more of your work is open to handing over.
  - You still have to check its work, and checking takes the skill you stopped practising.
    Bainbridge called this the irony of asking the operator to monitor a system installed because
    it does the job better. Shen & Tamkin frame skill loss as a threat to "the skills required to
    effectively supervise AI".
  - Its abilities are uneven in ways people do not predict: on a task outside its abilities,
    consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to be right (Dell'Acqua et al.
    2023). A colleague keeps the skill and answers for the result; the tool does neither.
  - You cannot feel the loss as it happens. Experienced developers believed AI had made them about
    20% faster when it had made them 19% slower (Becker et al. 2025). Students who reread rather
    than tested themselves were more confident and remembered less (Roediger & Karpicke 2006).
- **Who gains, who loses.** Novices gain most in output: less experienced customer-support agents
  improved most (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025), and so did lower-ability writers (Noy & Zhang
  2023). Whether they *learn* is less clear. Brynjolfsson et al. report evidence of learning, while
  Lehmann et al. found AI widened the gap between students with more and less prior knowledge. The
  worry about juniors (if they never do the groundwork, how do seniors form?) has one strong
  pre-AI case study, robotic surgery, where most residents lost the practice they needed (Beane
  2019), and one early labour-market finding: 13% lower employment for 22-to-25-year-olds in the
  most AI-exposed jobs (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen 2025, working paper). **For AI's effect on how
  juniors develop, I found no direct evidence; it is still a forecast.**
- **What mitigates has decent short-term evidence.**
  - A tutor designed to guide rather than answer removed most of the harm (Bastani et al.'s "GPT
    Tutor"), and a carefully designed AI tutor beat a strong active-learning class (Kestin et al.
    2025).
  - Showing people an AI's *explanation without its answer*, so they had to decide for
    themselves, produced learning, where answer-plus-explanation did not (Gajos & Mamykina 2022).
  - An AI that asked questions, rather than gave explanations, improved people's own reasoning
    (Danry et al. 2023).
  - Forcing people to think before seeing the AI's answer reduced over-reliance, though they liked
    it least (Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos 2021).
  - In the coding study, three of six ways of using the AI preserved learning (Shen & Tamkin
    2026; small groups, not causal).

  The common thread: the person still does the generating.
- **Almost all of this is short-term.** The longest AI-specific follow-up found is four months
  (Kosmyna et al., small and unreviewed); most studies measure learning within days or minutes.
  The only multi-year evidence is about GPS (13 people, three years). **I found no study of what
  years of everyday AI use does to a working professional's skills.**

## Q1. Learning while using AI: does the doing still teach?

The question here is skill *acquisition*: people learning something new, with AI available.

**1.1 The field experiment.** Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman,
R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school
mathematics. _Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences_, 122(26), e2422633122.
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122 **[abstract]**
- "We study this question via a field experiment where we provide nearly a thousand high school
  math students with access to generative AI tutors." Two tutors: one "that mimics a standard
  ChatGPT interface ('GPT Base')" and one "with prompts designed to safeguard learning ('GPT
  Tutor')".
- With access: "48% improvement in grades for GPT Base and 127% for GPT Tutor". After access was
  removed: "students actually perform worse than those who never had access (17% reduction in
  grades for GPT Base)". "These negative learning effects are largely mitigated by the safeguards
  in GPT Tutor." Students "attempt to use GPT-4 as a 'crutch'".
- Venue verified: published in PNAS (June 2025, issue 26); earlier versions circulated on SSRN
  (4895486). **A correction notice exists** (PNAS 122(34), e2518204122, 20 August 2025, per its
  Crossref record); its content could not be fetched (PNAS refused automated access). Check it
  before quoting any figure.
- Bearing: the cleanest evidence for "the doing was the learning". Same students, same problems.
  An unrestricted assistant improved practice and harmed learning; a tutor built to make them do
  the work did not. **[ours]**

**1.2 The coding experiment (vendor research).** Shen, J. H., & Tamkin, A. (2026). How AI impacts
skill formation. arXiv:2601.20245. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245 ; summarised in Anthropic
(2026, 29 January), How AI assistance impacts the formation of coding skills,
https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills **[abstract; blog read; Anthropic
authors]**
- "We find that AI use impairs conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging abilities,
  without delivering significant efficiency gains on average. Participants who fully delegated
  coding tasks showed some productivity improvements, but at the cost of learning the library."
- The abstract's framing: "how this assistance affects the development of skills required to
  effectively supervise AI remains unclear".
- Blog figures: 52 software engineers, "mostly junior"; the AI group "averaged 50% on the quiz,
  compared to 67%"; it finished about two minutes faster, not statistically significant.
- Limitations, in the authors' words: "Our sample was relatively small, and our assessment
  measured comprehension shortly after the coding task."
- Anthropic makes Claude, which helped draft this brief. A study by an AI company finding a cost of
  its own product cuts against its commercial interest, which counts somewhat in its favour; it
  is still a preprint. **[ours]**

**1.3 Substitute or complement.** Lehmann, M., Cornelius, P. B., & Sting, F. J. (2024). AI meets
the classroom: When do large language models harm learning? arXiv:2409.09047.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047 **[abstract]**
- "In two pre-registered and incentivized laboratory experiments, we find no effect of LLMs on
  overall learning outcomes." In exploratory analyses and a field study: "Students who substitute
  some of their learning activities with LLMs (e.g., by generating solutions to exercises)
  increase the volume of topics they can learn about but decrease their understanding of each
  topic. Students who complement their learning activities with LLMs (e.g., by asking for
  explanations) do not increase topic volume but do increase their understanding." Also: "LLMs
  widen the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge."
- Preprint; the usage-pattern findings are exploratory, and the pre-registered result is a null.
- Bearing: the overall effect can be zero while the effect of *how* someone uses it is not. That
  matches Part 3 of the claim, choosing which parts to hand over. **[ours]**

**1.4 Better essays, no more learning.** Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y.,
Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative
artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. _British Journal of
Educational Technology_, 56(2), 489–530. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544 **[abstract]**
- A randomised experiment with 117 university students on a writing task: "ChatGPT group
  outperformed in the essay score improvement but their knowledge gain and transfer were not
  significantly different". The authors suggest ChatGPT "may promote learners' dependence on
  technology and potentially trigger 'metacognitive laziness'". That phrase is the authors'
  interpretation, not a measured outcome. (Published online December 2024.)

**1.5 Less effort, weaker reasoning.** Stadler, M., Bannert, M., & Sailer, M. (2024). Cognitive
ease at a cost: LLMs reduce mental effort but compromise depth in student scientific inquiry.
_Computers in Human Behavior_, 160, 108386. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2024.108386
**[abstract, as reproduced on repository pages; full text not read]**
- 91 university students randomly assigned to ChatGPT-3.5 or Google to research a science
  question. The ChatGPT group "experienced significantly lower cognitive load" but "demonstrated
  lower-quality reasoning and argumentation in their final recommendations". The diversity of
  recommendations did not differ.
- A short task (about 20 minutes, per press coverage **[secondary]**).

**1.6 The EEG study, and its critics.** Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao,
X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation
of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task. arXiv:2506.08872.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872 **[abstract]**
- Three groups (LLM, search engine, "Brain-only") wrote essays over three sessions; in a fourth,
  groups swapped. "A total of 54 participants took part in Sessions 1-3, with 18 completing
  session 4." "LLM users displayed the weakest connectivity." "LLM-to-Brain participants showed
  reduced alpha and beta connectivity, indicating under-engagement." "LLM users also struggled to
  accurately quote their own work." "Over four months, LLM users consistently underperformed at
  neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels."
- **Status:** a preprint, first posted 10 June 2025, revised (v2) 31 December 2025; no journal
  reference on arXiv as of today. The project page says "it has not yet been peer-reviewed, thus
  all the conclusions are to be treated with caution and as preliminary" (MIT Media Lab project
  page **[abstract: page read]**). The same page asks: "Please do not use the words like 'stupid',
  'dumb', 'brain rot', 'harm', 'damage'", and to avoid "LLMs make you stop thinking".
- **Critique:** Stanković, M., Hirche, E., Kollatzsch, S., & Doetsch, J. N. (2025). Comment on:
  Your brain on ChatGPT. arXiv:2601.00856. https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856 **[abstract]**. Concerns
  about "(i) study design considerations, including the limited sample size; (ii) the
  reproducibility of the analyses; (iii) methodological issues related to the EEG analysis; (iv)
  inconsistencies in the reporting of results; and (v) limited transparency". It also praises the
  dataset and asks for more conservative interpretation.
- Bearing: suggestive, and the press coverage outran it. Lower brain connectivity while a tool
  does part of the task is what you would expect whether or not anything is lost. The finding
  that bears on skill is the session-4 result, with 18 people. Do not lean on it. **[ours]**

## Q2. Skills people already have: does using AI wear them down?

**2.1 Endoscopists after AI.** Budzyń, K., Romańczyk, M., Kitala, D., Kołodziej, P., Bugajski, M.,
Adami, H. O., Blom, J., Buszkiewicz, M., Halvorsen, N., Hassan, C., Romańczyk, T., Holme, Ø.,
Jarus, K., Fielding, S., Kunar, M., Pellise, M., Pilonis, N., Kamiński, M. F., et al. (2025).
Endoscopist deskilling risk after exposure to artificial intelligence in colonoscopy: a
multicentre, observational study. _The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology_, 10(10), 896–903.
https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-1253(25)00133-5 **[abstract]**
- "We conducted a retrospective, observational study at four endoscopy centres in Poland." "The
  ADR of standard colonoscopy decreased significantly from 28·4% (226 of 795) before to 22·4% (145
  of 648) after exposure to AI, corresponding with an absolute difference of -6·0% (95% CI -10·5 to
  -1·6; p=0·0089)." Interpretation: "Continuous exposure to AI might reduce the ADR of standard
  non-AI assisted colonoscopy, suggesting a negative effect on endoscopist behaviour."
- The title as published says "deskilling *risk*". That 19 experienced endoscopists took part comes
  from press coverage **[secondary]**, not the abstract.
- **Contested.** Three letters in the December 2025 issue respond to it (Lam 2025; Levartovsky &
  Kopylov 2025; van de Sande et al. 2025) **[record: content not read]**. Secondary summaries say
  critics point to differences between the before and after patient groups, unreported withdrawal
  times, the small number of endoscopists, and secondary outcomes that did not fall
  **[secondary]**.
- Bearing: the best real-world evidence found that AI can erode an expert's existing skill, and
  fast (three months). But it is one observational study of one task, it measures performance
  without AI rather than skill directly, and the effect could be lowered effort or attention
  rather than lost skill. **[ours]**

**2.2 Knowledge workers' own account (vendor research).** Lee, H.-P. (H.), Sarkar, A.,
Tankelevitch, L., Drosos, I., Rintel, S., Banks, R., & Wilson, N. (2025). The impact of
generative AI on critical thinking: Self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence
effects from a survey of knowledge workers. In _Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human
Factors in Computing Systems_ (pp. 1–22). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778
**[abstract; Microsoft Research authors]**
- "We survey 319 knowledge workers"; "Participants shared 936 first-hand examples of using GenAI in
  work tasks." "Higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking, while higher
  self-confidence is associated with more critical thinking." "GenAI shifts the nature of critical
  thinking toward information verification, response integration, and task stewardship."
- Self-report, cross-sectional, and its title says so ("Self-Reported Reductions"). It shows how
  people describe their thinking, not a measured change in skill. Microsoft sells Copilot.
- Bearing: the thinking does not vanish, it moves to checking and overseeing. Whether checking is
  done well depends on the skill underneath (Q5.6). **[ours]**

**2.3 A correlational survey, often over-quoted.** Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts
on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. _Societies_, 15(1), 6.
https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006 **[abstract]**
- 666 participants; "a significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool usage and
  critical thinking abilities, mediated by increased cognitive offloading". Younger participants
  showed "higher dependence on AI tools and lower critical thinking scores".
- A survey with interviews. It cannot tell whether AI use lowers critical thinking or whether
  people who think less critically use AI more. Age is tangled with both. **[ours]**

**2.4 Believing you are faster.** Becker, J., Rush, N., Barnes, E., & Rein, D. (2025). Measuring the
impact of early-2025 AI on experienced open-source developer productivity. arXiv:2507.09089.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089 **[abstract]** (also in brief 13)
- A randomised trial: 16 experienced developers, 246 tasks in projects they knew well. "Developers
  forecast that allowing AI will reduce completion time by 24%. After completing the study,
  developers estimate that allowing AI reduced completion time by 20%." In fact, "allowing AI
  actually increases completion time by 19%".
- Follow-up: METR (Becker, J., Rush, N., Cunningham, T., Rein, D., & Mahamud, K.) (2026, 24
  February). We are changing our developer productivity experiment design.
  https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/ **[abstract: post read]**. Newer estimates point to
  a speedup ("-18% with a confidence interval between -38% and +9%" for returning developers;
  "-4%" for new ones, where negative means faster), but METR calls the design "likely a bad proxy"
  because "an increased share of developers say they would not want to do 50% of their work
  without AI" and so declined to take part.
- Bearing: this is about speed, not skill. It bears on skill in two ways. People's sense of how AI
  is affecting their work was wrong by a wide margin, so felt effects are not a safe guide. And
  developers declining to work without AI is itself a sign of dependence, whatever else it means.
  **[ours]**

**2.5 Uneven abilities.** Dell'Acqua, F., McFowland, E., Mollick, E. R., Lifshitz-Assaf, H.,
Kellogg, K., Rajendran, S., Krayer, L., Candelon, F., & Lakhani, K. R. (2023). Navigating the
jagged technological frontier: Field experimental evidence of the effects of AI on knowledge
worker productivity and quality. Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013. **[report]**
(cite-checked 2026-10-17 for brief 6; in essay 01)
- 758 consultants. On tasks inside the model's abilities, more done, faster, better. On a task
  outside them, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to be right.
- Bearing: a skill-loss question in disguise. If you keep the skill, you can spot when the task is
  outside the AI's abilities; if you lose it, you cannot. **[ours]**

**Longitudinal evidence.** The longest AI-specific follow-up found is Kosmyna et al.'s four months
(1.6), with 18 people in the decisive session. Budzyń et al. compare three months either side
(2.1). Bastani et al. (1.1) measure within a school term; Shen & Tamkin (1.2) minutes after the
task. **I found no multi-year study of AI use and professional skill.** The one multi-year result
in this brief is about GPS (5.3).

## Q3. Who gains and who loses: novices, experts, and the junior pipeline

**3.1 Customer support.** Brynjolfsson, E., Li, D., & Raymond, L. (2025). Generative AI at work.
_The Quarterly Journal of Economics_, 140(2), 889–942. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044 (NBER
working paper w31161, 2023) **[abstract]**
- "Data from 5,172 customer-support agents." Productivity up "15% on average". "Less experienced
  and lower-skilled workers improve both the speed and quality of their output, while the most
  experienced and highest-skilled workers see small gains in speed and small declines in quality."
  "We also find evidence that AI assistance facilitates worker learning and improves English
  fluency."
- How "learning" was measured was not checked (abstract only). Before the essay leans on it, read
  the section: whether agents kept their gains when the tool was unavailable is the test that
  matters here.
- Bearing: the strongest evidence *against* a simple "AI stops novices learning" story, in a
  setting where the AI suggested responses built from what top agents did. **[ours]**

**3.2 Professional writing.** Noy, S., & Zhang, W. (2023). Experimental evidence on the
productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence. _Science_, 381(6654), 187–192.
https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586 **[abstract]**
- 453 college-educated professionals, preregistered: time down 40%, quality up 18%; "Inequality
  between workers decreased". The editor's summary: "Participants with weaker skills benefited the
  most."
- The 2023 working-paper version (SSRN 4375283, 444 participants) adds: "ChatGPT mostly
  substitutes for worker effort rather than complementing worker skills" **[abstract]**. That
  sentence is not in the Science abstract; check the published text before quoting it.
- Bearing: a single session measures output, not learning. "Substitutes for effort" is exactly the
  condition under which, on Q4's account, less is learned. **[ours]**

**3.3 Widening gaps in learning.** Lehmann et al. 2024 (1.3): "LLMs widen the gap between students
with low and high prior knowledge." And Shen & Tamkin 2026 (1.2) frame their study around novices
"who rely heavily on AI to complete unfamiliar tasks".
- Bearing, with 3.1 and 3.2: novices gain most *in output*, while the learning evidence is mixed,
  and some of it points the other way. Both can be true: the novice's output looks like an
  expert's while the novice learns less. **[ours]**

**3.4 The junior pipeline: the pre-AI case.** Beane, M. (2019). Shadow learning: Building robotic
surgical skill when approved means fail. _Administrative Science Quarterly_, 64(1), 87–123.
https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839217751692 **[abstract]**
- A two-year, five-site ethnography plus interviews at 13 teaching hospitals. Robotic surgery
  "greatly limited trainees' role in the work, making approved methods ineffective". Only "a
  minority of robotic surgical trainees" reached competence, through "shadow learning", including
  "undersupervised struggle". Outcomes included "hyperspecialization and a decreasing supply of
  experts relative to demand".
- Published online January 2018. Not about AI. The "ten to twenty times less practice" figure
  quoted in press is Beane's own statement, not checked against the paper **[secondary]**.
- Bearing: the closest documented case of a technology that took over the work juniors learned
  from. The skill pipeline thinned, and the few who got through broke the rules to do it. That is
  evidence the mechanism exists, not that AI is doing it. **[ours]**

**3.5 The junior pipeline: early jobs data.** Brynjolfsson, E., Chandar, B., & Chen, R. (2025,
August). Canaries in the coal mine? Six facts about the recent employment effects of artificial
intelligence. Stanford Digital Economy Lab / SIEPR working paper.
https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/canaries-coal-mine-six-facts-about-recent-employment-effects-artificial
**[abstract: page read, via summary]**
- Early-career workers "(ages 22-25) in the most AI-exposed occupations" have "experienced a 13
  percent relative decline in employment"; more experienced workers in the same occupations were
  stable or growing. Declines concentrate where AI is more likely to "automate, rather than
  augment".
- A working paper; a later version may report a different figure (one press report gives 16%
  **[secondary]**). This is about hiring, not skill.
- Bearing: if entry-level jobs shrink, so do the places where the groundwork that forms seniors is
  done. That link is inference. **I found no study measuring how juniors who started their careers
  with AI develop compared with those who did not.** **[ours]**

## Q4. The mechanism: why the doing is often the learning

**4.1 Desirable difficulties.** Bjork, R. A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the
training of human beings. In J. Metcalfe & A. P. Shimamura (Eds.), _Metacognition: Knowing about
knowing_ (pp. 185–205). MIT Press. **[record; summaries only]**
- The source of the term "desirable difficulties": conditions that slow performance during
  training (spacing, mixing tasks, less frequent feedback) often improve long-term retention and
  transfer. Practice that feels smooth is not always the practice that teaches. Wording here is
  from secondary summaries; quote the chapter itself before the essay does.

**4.2 The generation effect.** Slamecka, N. J., & Graf, P. (1978). The generation effect:
Delineation of a phenomenon. _Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory_, 4(6),
592–604. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592 **[record]**
- Bertsch, S., Pesta, B. J., Wiscott, R., & McDaniel, M. A. (2007). The generation effect: A
  meta-analytic review. _Memory & Cognition_, 35(2), 201–210. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441
  **[abstract]**. "Subjects who generate information (e.g., produce synonyms) remember the
  information better than they do material that they simply read." Across 86 studies the effect
  was ".40—a benefit of almost half a standard deviation of generation over reading."
- Bearing: reading an AI's answer is the "read" condition; working it out is the "generate"
  condition. **[ours]**

**4.3 Retrieval practice, and the confidence trap.** Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D.
(2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. _Psychological
Science_, 17(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x **[abstract]**
- On delayed tests, "prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even
  though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the
  material."
- Bearing: the easier route made people *more* confident and *less* capable. The same gap between
  feeling and fact shows up in 2.4. **[ours]**

**4.4 The same point, from automation in 1983.** Bainbridge, L. (1983). Ironies of automation.
_Automatica_, 19(6), 775–779. https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8 **[abstract; text
read]**
- "Unfortunately, physical skills deteriorate when they are not used, particularly the refinements
  of gain and timing. This means that a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an
  automated process may now be an inexperienced one."
- On knowledge: "efficient retrieval of knowledge from long-term memory depends on frequency of
  use", and "this type of knowledge develops only through use and feedback about its
  effectiveness."
- "A more serious irony is that the automatic control system has been put in because it can do the
  job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working
  effectively."
- "Perhaps the final irony is that it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for
  manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training."
- "By taking away the easy parts of his task, automation can make the difficult parts of the human
  operator's task more difficult."
- Bearing: forty years before generative AI, the full argument of this brief: skill fades without
  use, knowledge forms only through use, and the person left to supervise needs the skill most
  when they have practised it least. **[ours]**

**What Q4 supports.** **[ours]**
- "The doing was the learning" has a well-tested mechanism: generating, retrieving and struggling
  build durable skill, and receiving the answer does not.
- It also says something the claim leaves out: the doing is often the *upkeep*. Retrieval depends
  on frequency of use (4.4), which is why already-learned skills can fade (2.1, 5.5).

## Q5. Compared to what? Older tools, and the deskilling debate

**5.1 Writing.** Plato. _Phaedrus_ 274c–275b. In _Plato in Twelve Volumes_, Vol. 9, trans. H. N.
Fowler (Loeb Classical Library). Harvard University Press; London: William Heinemann, 1925. Perseus
Digital Library, https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0174:text=Phaedrus:page=275
**[abstract: text read at 275a–b on Perseus]**
- The Egyptian king Thamus to the god Theuth, inventor of letters: "For this invention will produce
  forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it"; "You have invented an elixir not of
  memory, but of reminding"; "you offer your pupils the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom".
- Bearing: the oldest version of this essay's worry. It was partly right (people stopped
  memorising long texts) and largely outweighed (writing made most later knowledge possible). Its
  "appearance of wisdom" is the novice-output point in 3.3. **[ours]**

**5.2 Calculators.**
- Hembree, R., & Dessart, D. J. (1986). Effects of hand-held calculators in precollege mathematics
  education: A meta-analysis. _Journal for Research in Mathematics Education_, 17(2), 83–99.
  https://doi.org/10.2307/749255 **[abstract]**. 79 reports. "At all grades but Grade 4, a use of
  calculators in concert with traditional mathematics instruction apparently improves the average
  student's basic skills with paper and pencil." "Sustained calculator use in Grade 4 appears to
  hinder the development of basic skills in average students."
- Ellington, A. J. (2003). A meta-analysis of the effects of calculators on students' achievement
  and attitude levels in precollege mathematics classes. _Journal for Research in Mathematics
  Education_, 34(5), 433–463. https://doi.org/10.2307/30034795 **[record; findings from secondary
  summaries]**. Summaries describe improved operational and problem-solving skills when calculators
  were part of both teaching and testing. The "neither helped nor hindered" wording often attributed
  to it comes from her 2000 dissertation; not checked against the 2003 article.
- Bearing: the calculator debate's evidence lines up with Part 1. Used alongside teaching, no
  harm; used sustainedly at the age the skill is being formed, harm. **[ours]**

**5.3 GPS and spatial memory.**
- Dahmani, L., & Bohbot, V. D. (2020). Habitual use of GPS negatively impacts spatial memory during
  self-guided navigation. _Scientific Reports_, 10, 6310. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-62877-0
  **[abstract]**. 50 regular drivers: "people with greater lifetime GPS experience have worse spatial
  memory during self-guided navigation". Of these, "13 participants were retested three years after
  initial testing. Although the longitudinal sample was small", greater GPS use "was associated with
  a steeper decline in hippocampal-dependent spatial memory". Heavier users did not use GPS because
  they felt they had a poor sense of direction, which the authors read as GPS causing the decline.
- Maguire, E. A., Gadian, D. G., Johnsrude, I. S., Good, C. D., Ashburner, J., Frackowiak, R. S. J.,
  & Frith, C. D. (2000). Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi drivers.
  _Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences_, 97(8), 4398–4403.
  https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.070039597 **[abstract]**. "The posterior hippocampi of taxi drivers
  were significantly larger relative to those of control subjects", and volume "correlated with the
  amount of time spent as a taxi driver".
- Bearing: the clearest non-AI case of a tool taking over a skill and the skill (and the brain
  region that supports it) responding to use. Many people would call the trade worth it. That is
  Part 3's question answered for one skill. **[ours]**

**5.4 The "Google effect", and why to be careful.**
- Sparrow, B., Liu, J., & Wegner, D. M. (2011). Google effects on memory: Cognitive consequences of
  having information at our fingertips. _Science_, 333(6043), 776–778.
  https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745 **[abstract]**. "When people expect to have future access
  to information, they have lower rates of recall of the information itself and enhanced recall
  instead for where to access it."
- Camerer, C. F., Dreber, A., Holzmeister, F., Ho, T.-H., Huber, J., Johannesson, M., et al.
  (2018). Evaluating the replicability of social science experiments in Nature and Science between
  2010 and 2015. _Nature Human Behaviour_, 2, 637–644. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0399-z
  **[abstract]**. 21 studies replicated; 13 (62%) found a significant effect in the same direction.
  That Sparrow et al.'s Stroop experiment (the "computer words after hard questions" result) was
  among the failures is from secondary sources and from Hesselmann **[secondary]**.
- Hesselmann, G. (2020). No conclusive evidence that difficult general knowledge questions cause a
  "Google Stroop effect". A replication study. _PeerJ_, 8, e10325. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.10325
  **[abstract]**. "The 'Google Stroop effect' could not be replicated in two recent replication
  attempts as part of a large replicability project"; a design closer to the original also found "no
  conclusive evidence".
- Caution: the replications tested the Stroop experiment, not the memory experiments (expecting
  future access lowers recall). Those were not checked here. Do not say "the Google effect was
  debunked"; say "its best-known experiment did not replicate". **[ours]**
- Bearing: a reminder that a vivid "technology is rotting memory" finding can fail to hold. The
  same caution applies to 1.6. **[ours]**

**5.5 Autopilots: skill decay and complacency.**
- Bainbridge 1983 (4.4).
- Casner, S. M., Geven, R. W., Recker, M. P., & Schooler, J. W. (2014). The retention of manual
  flying skills in the automated cockpit. _Human Factors_, 56(8), 1506–1516.
  https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814535628 **[abstract]**. 16 airline pilots in a Boeing 747-400
  simulator. "We found pilots' instrument scanning and manual control skills to be mostly intact,
  even when pilots reported that they were infrequently practiced." But for "the cognitive tasks
  needed for manual flight (e.g., tracking the aircraft's position without the use of a map display,
  deciding which navigational steps come next, recognizing instrument system failures), we observed
  more frequent and significant problems", associated with "task-unrelated thought". Conclusion: the
  retention of cognitive skills "may depend on the degree to which pilots remain actively engaged in
  supervising the automation."
- Ebbatson, M., Harris, D., Huddlestone, J., & Sears, R. (2010). The relationship between manual
  handling performance and recent flying experience in air transport pilots. _Ergonomics_, 53(2),
  268–277. https://doi.org/10.1080/00140130903342349 **[abstract]**. Notes "anecdotal evidence" of
  manual skill decay and "a paucity of objective data"; finds "significant relationships ...
  between pilots' very recent flying experience and their manual control strategy".
- Federal Aviation Administration (2013, 4 January). Safety Alert for Operators (SAFO) 13002:
  Manual flight operations.
  https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf
  **[secondary: the PDF refused automated access; content from secondary quotation]**. Encourages
  operators to promote manual flight "when appropriate", citing an increase in manual handling
  errors in flight operations data; continuous autoflight use is described as not reinforcing manual
  skills. Quote the PDF itself before the essay does.
- Parasuraman, R., & Manzey, D. H. (2010). Complacency and bias in human use of automation: An
  attentional integration. _Human Factors_, 52(3), 381–410. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055
  **[abstract]**. Complacency "is found in both naive and expert participants and cannot be overcome
  with simple practice"; automation bias "occurs in both naive and expert participants, cannot be
  prevented by training or instructions".
- Bearing: aviation is the long-running human precedent for this essay, and its response was
  deliberate practice of the manual skill (SAFO 13002), not abandoning automation. Casner's finding
  that thinking skills faded more than hand skills, and faded most when attention wandered, is the
  closest pre-AI match to AI's case, since AI takes over thinking. **[ours]**

**5.6 Cognitive offloading, the general frame.** Risko, E. F., & Gilbert, S. J. (2016). Cognitive
offloading. _Trends in Cognitive Sciences_, 20(9), 676–688. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002
**[abstract]**
- Defined as "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task
  so as to reduce cognitive demand", from tilting your head to read rotated text to a phone reminder.
  The review asks what triggers offloading and "what are the cognitive consequences of this
  behavior?"
- Bearing: offloading is ordinary and mostly sensible. The question is never whether to offload, but
  what it costs in each case. That is Part 2's framing, and it has a research literature behind it.
  **[ours]**

**Where the analogy holds, and where AI differs.** **[ours]**
- *Holds:* every tool here cost the specific skill it took over (memorising, mental arithmetic,
  wayfinding, hand-flying), most when it took over the practice that forms or keeps the skill (5.2's
  Grade 4; 5.5's cognitive flying tasks). Broad claims of general decline overreached (5.4) or did
  not happen (writing). On this record, Part 2 of the claim is right.
- *Differs, 1, breadth:* a calculator replaces arithmetic and GPS replaces wayfinding. An assistant
  can draft, analyse, summarise, code and judge, so the set of skills open to handing over is most
  of knowledge work.
- *Differs, 2, judgement:* the older tools took over storage, calculation or control. AI takes over
  reasoning and judgement, the skills you need to check its output (2.2, 4.4). With a calculator you
  could check the answer roughly by estimating; with an AI's analysis, checking takes the analytical
  skill itself.
- *Differs, 3, fluency:* its output is fluent and confident whether or not it is right (brief 12), so
  the cue that would make you check is weaker than with a tool whose errors look like errors.
- *Differs, 4, compared with a colleague:* hand a task to a colleague and the skill still exists, in
  them, and they answer for the result. Hand it to AI and the skill exists nowhere you can rely on in
  the same way: its abilities are uneven (2.5), and it is accountable to no one (essay 01). Delegating
  to a colleague is a trade inside a team; delegating to AI moves the skill out of the team.

## Q6. What keeps the person thinking

**6.1 Guardrailed tutors.** Bastani et al. 2025 (1.1): the "GPT Tutor" with prompts "designed to
safeguard learning" largely removed the harm while still raising practice grades.

**6.2 A tutor built on teaching practice.** Kestin, G., Miller, K., Klales, A., Milbourne, T., &
Ponti, G. (2025). AI tutoring outperforms in-class active learning: An RCT introducing a novel
research-based design in an authentic educational setting. _Scientific Reports_, 15, 17458.
https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6 **[abstract]**
- "Students learn significantly more in less time when using the AI tutor, compared with the in-class
  active learning." The tutor's design "is informed by the same pedagogical best practices as
  employed in the in-class lessons."
- Secondary reports give 194 students, two weeks, at Harvard, with each student in each condition
  for one week **[secondary]**. One course, two topics, short term.
- Bearing: the tool is not the problem in itself. Designed to make students work, it taught well.
  **[ours]**

**6.3 Explanation without the answer.** Gajos, K. Z., & Mamykina, L. (2022). Do people engage
cognitively with AI? Impact of AI assistance on incidental learning. In _Proceedings of the 27th
International Conference on Intelligent User Interfaces (IUI '22)_ (pp. 794–806). ACM.
https://doi.org/10.1145/3490099.3511138 **[abstract]**
- Three experiments on nutrition decisions with simulated AI. Recommendation plus explanation:
  better decisions, "but they did not learn". Deciding first, then seeing the AI: "improved decisions,
  but no learning". "Just an AI explanation but no recommendation", so people had to decide
  themselves: "both more accurate decisions and learning gains".
- Simulated AI, not a language model, and lay decisions.

**6.4 An AI that asks.** Danry, V., Pataranutaporn, P., Mao, Y., & Maes, P. (2023). Don't just tell
me, ask me: AI systems that intelligently frame explanations as questions improve human logical
discernment accuracy over causal AI explanations. In _Proceedings of the 2023 CHI Conference on
Human Factors in Computing Systems_ (pp. 1–13). ACM. https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580672
**[abstract]**
- 204 participants. Compared with no feedback and "even causal AI explanations of an always correct
  system, AI-framed Questioning significantly increase human discernment of logically flawed
  statements". The authors' aim: AI as "a critical thinking stimulator rather than an information
  teller".
- One task (judging whether arguments are logically valid), measured during the task, not later.

**6.5 Forcing a pause, and its cost.** Buçinca, Z., Malaya, M. B., & Gajos, K. Z. (2021). To trust
or to think: Cognitive forcing functions can reduce overreliance on AI in AI-assisted
decision-making. _Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction_, 5(CSCW1), 1–21.
https://doi.org/10.1145/3449287 **[abstract]**
- N=199. "Cognitive forcing significantly reduced overreliance compared to the simple explainable AI
  approaches. However, there was a trade-off: people assigned the least favorable subjective ratings
  to the designs that reduced the overreliance the most." The benefit was larger for people "higher
  in Need for Cognition".
- Bearing: what protects thinking is the thing people like least, and it helps most those who need
  it least. A design that keeps people thinking will not win on user preference alone. **[ours]**

**6.6 How people use it.** Shen & Tamkin 2026 (1.2): six usage patterns; three "involve cognitive
engagement and preserve learning outcomes even when participants receive AI assistance". In the
blog, conceptual questions and asking for explanations alongside generated code scored 65% or more;
delegation and progressive reliance scored under 40%. Groups of 2 to 7 people; the authors say the
analysis "does not draw a causal link". Lehmann et al. 2024 (1.3): complementing beat substituting,
in exploratory analyses.

**6.7 Staying engaged while supervising.** Casner et al. 2014 (5.5): cognitive flying skills were
better kept by pilots who stayed mentally engaged while the automation flew. SAFO 13002 (5.5):
scheduled manual practice.

**What Q6 supports.** **[ours]**
- The common thread across 6.1–6.7: the person still generates something (a decision, an answer,
  an attempt, a question) before or instead of receiving the AI's. That is the generation and
  retrieval mechanism of Q4, built into the tool or the habit.
- The evidence is short-term and mostly from students and lab tasks. I found no study of these
  methods sustained over months at work.
- The cost is real: slower and less pleasant (6.5; 1.2's delegators were the fastest).

## Q7. Testing the claim with the yardstick [ours]

Our reading, to be argued, not a finding.

**Part 1: "Handing a task to AI costs you the skill only where the doing was the learning."** Right
at its core, too narrow as worded.
- *For:* the acquisition evidence is consistent and causal where it is randomised (1.1, 1.2, 1.3),
  and the mechanism is long established (4.1–4.3). The older-tool record shows the same: harm
  concentrated where the tool replaces skill formation (5.2).
- *Against "only":* skills already learned can fade when the doing was also the practice that kept
  them (2.1, 4.4, 5.3, 5.5). Experienced endoscopists and airline pilots were not learning; they were
  maintaining.
- *Also missing:* the change to thinking, not only to skill. Effort moves to checking and
  overseeing (2.2), and confidence in the tool goes with less critical thinking. That is not skill
  loss yet, but it is the condition under which skill erodes (5.5's "task-unrelated thought").

**Part 2: "Elsewhere it is ordinary delegation, the same trade people have always made with tools
and colleagues."** Largely right for tools; weaker for colleagues; with two real differences.
- *For:* offloading is ordinary and mostly sensible (5.6). Each older tool cost the specific skill it
  replaced and little else (5.1–5.4). Panics about general decline have overreached (5.4, 1.6).
- *Against "the same trade":* (a) you still have to check AI's work, and checking takes the skill
  you've stopped practising (4.4's monitoring irony; 1.2's "skills required to effectively supervise
  AI"; 2.5's uneven abilities). A calculator's user can check by estimating, and a colleague keeps
  the skill and answers for the result; with AI, neither safeguard is automatic. (b) You cannot feel
  the loss (2.4, 4.3). Delegating to a colleague does not hide from you what you've stopped doing;
  fluent AI output can.

**Part 3: "So the question is which parts of your work you can afford to stop practising."** The
strongest part, and the right question. Two additions.
- *Which parts:* the ones you will never need to check, and that you are not still learning. Skills
  you need in order to judge the AI's work are not affordable to drop, even when the AI does them
  better (4.4, "the final irony").
- *How to decide:* deliberately, and not by feel, since felt effects mislead (2.4). Aviation's answer
  was scheduled practice of the skill the automation covers (5.5); education's is tools that make the
  learner generate (Q6).

**A candidate restatement for Bart to test:** "Handing work to AI costs you a skill where doing the
work was how you learned it or how you kept it. Elsewhere it is ordinary delegation, the trade people
have always made with tools. Two things make it less ordinary. You still have to check the AI's work,
and checking takes the very skill you've stopped practising. And you won't feel the loss as it
happens. So decide on purpose which parts of your work you can afford to stop practising, and keep
practising the ones you need to judge its work."

**Compared to what?** Two comparisons, and they point different ways.
- *With older tools:* AI looks like more of the same, a tool that costs the skill it replaces.
  Writing, calculators and GPS all did, and in each case society mostly judged the trade worth it.
  That counts against alarm.
- *With a colleague or junior:* AI looks less like delegation, because the skill does not stay in the
  team and the delegate answers for nothing. That counts against complacency.
- *What no comparison settles:* how juniors who learn their trade alongside AI will turn out. The
  surgical case (3.4) shows the mechanism can thin the pipeline; the customer-support case (3.1)
  shows AI can also teach. Which wins, and where, is not yet known.

**What would change this picture.**
- Evidence that skills protected by deliberate practice still erode with heavy AI use. Then "decide
  what to keep practising" would not be enough.
- Multi-year evidence that professionals using AI keep their unaided skill. Then the maintenance
  worry (2.1) would be overstated.
- A cohort study of juniors trained with and without AI. Then the pipeline worry would become a
  finding or be dropped.
- A successful, peer-reviewed replication of the session-4 result in Kosmyna et al. (1.6) with a
  larger sample.

## A. The author's own work (internal; use carefully)

**What exists.** I found nothing in this repository (docs/ and pilots/ searched for deskilling,
skill loss or decay, cognitive offloading, complacency, over-reliance and automation bias) that
measures the author's, or anyone's, skill change from using AI. Essay 02's notes study is about what
the *model* keeps, not what the person keeps. The items below bear on the question only indirectly.

**Published and citable:** Paper 1 (the Resolver), Zenodo concept DOI
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21210264. Source read: `pilots/llm-judge-2026-05-02/v2/preprint-draft.md`
(read only). It is locked. Cite its findings as published; do not paraphrase them into claims it
doesn't make.

**A.1 Supervision is bounded by the supervisor's own skill (the paper's responsibility
statement).** "The author directed the work, reviewed all content, and verified what is mechanically
checkable; the domain-technical legitimacy he cannot personally adjudicate is routed to the named
future expert audit." And: "The author takes full responsibility for every claim, number, and
citation in this paper. The AI tools are not authors and bear no accountability."
- Bearing: a first-hand instance of Q7's main difference. Where the author lacked the skill to check
  the AI-assisted work, the paper did not treat his review as a check; it named an outside expert
  audit. That is the practice the evidence suggests, stated in a published record. It is a
  statement of practice, not a measurement. **[ours]**

**A.2 Role, not model: the producer's own blind spot (§6.3).** The paper states: "The distinction that
does the work here is **role, not model**." A fresh challenger caught more of the producer's own
material flaws than the producer reviewing its own output (figures and limits in brief 13, A.1:
n = 8 problems, flaws confirmed by an AI panel, no significance test).
- Bearing: only an analogy here, since the paper is about AI reviewers, not human skill. SPARRING's
  design asks a second party to challenge rather than to answer, the same shape as the "ask me" and
  "decide first" methods in Q6 (6.3, 6.4). I found no test in this repository of whether working
  through SPARRING keeps a *person's* own skills sharper than plain AI use. Do not imply one.
  **[ours]**

## Open questions

- What does years of everyday AI use do to a working professional's unaided skill? No multi-year
  study found on 2026-10-19.
- Do juniors who start their careers with AI develop more slowly, differently, or not at all
  differently? No cohort study found; the evidence is one pre-AI case (3.4) and hiring data (3.5).
- Is the endoscopy result (2.1) lost skill, lowered effort, or a difference between patient groups?
  The correspondence (three letters) was not read.
- What exactly did the Bastani et al. correction (1.1) change? Not read.
- Did the customer-support agents' gains (3.1) persist when the AI was unavailable? Abstract only.
- Do the methods that keep people thinking (Q6) survive months of real work, where speed is rewarded
  and the pleasant option wins? No workplace study found.
- Does checking AI output, done regularly, maintain the underlying skill, or is checking a weaker
  form of practice than doing? I found no study that separates the two.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Vendor research.** Shen & Tamkin (1.2) are Anthropic authors; Lee et al. (2.2) are Microsoft
  Research authors. Claude, which helped draft this brief, is made by Anthropic. METR (2.4) is an
  independent research organisation.
- **Preprints and unreviewed work:** Kosmyna et al. (1.6), Stanković et al. (1.6), Shen & Tamkin
  (1.2), Lehmann et al. (1.3), Becker et al. (2.4), Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen (3.5), Dell'Acqua
  et al. (2.5, a working paper).

- **Small samples:** Casner et al. (16 pilots), Becker et al. (16 developers), Dahmani & Bohbot's
  follow-up (13 people), Kosmyna et al.'s session 4 (18), Shen & Tamkin's usage patterns (2 to 7 per

- **Short-term:** almost everything in Q1, Q2 and Q6 measures within minutes, days or a few months.
- **Students and lab tasks dominate** Q1 and Q6. Workplace evidence is thinner (2.1, 2.2, 3.1).
- **Unverified specifics flagged inline:** the Bastani correction's content (1.1); the endoscopy
  correspondence and the 19-endoscopist figure (2.1); how learning was measured in Brynjolfsson et al.
