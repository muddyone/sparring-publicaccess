# The thing on the other end

_What AI Is · The overview_

---

**This series is an honest account of what people are working with when they use AI.** It comes
in two parts. Part one, five short essays, asks what it is. Part two asks what working with it is
like. This overview introduces both. People
who work with AI assistants every day end up asking the same handful of questions about the thing
on the other end of the conversation. Does it learn? Is anyone there? Is its warmth good for me,
or just pleasant? Is a relationship with it dangerous? And underneath all of them: how should I
judge it at all?

The thing on the other end is more than the large language model at its core, the way a car is
more than its engine. It is the whole system you work with: an AI assistant such as Claude or
ChatGPT, or a fuller setup in which an AI works on its own, with files and tools, over days. And it
is newer than it looks. Only since these systems began remembering across conversations, in
earnest in 2024 and 2025, has it been something you keep working with, rather than a fresh start
with every conversation (sources in [the brief](../briefs/2026-10-18-10-when-assistants-began-to-remember.md)). That system is what this series is about.

The usual answers to those questions come in two kinds, and both go wrong the same way. One says
it's just autocomplete; the other says it's a someone. Each settles the question by deciding what
*category* AI belongs to, and then reads the answer off the category. I think that is the wrong
yardstick.

## The yardstick

Every essay in this series applies the same yardstick. **Judge it by what it does and the role it fills, not by
what it's made of. Ask "compared to what?": measure it against whoever would otherwise fill that
role, as they actually are. Keep what we know separate from what nobody knows. And describe it
accurately, even when a warmer story would sell better.**

The first essay of Part one,
[The wrong yardstick](01-the-yardstick.md), builds that method in full. A common style of
criticism holds AI to a calculator's standard, always right and fully explainable, that no person filling the same
role meets. Ask instead "compared to what?", then add the checks AI's real differences call for:
where it fails and how, whether it can be corrected, and who answers for it. Judged that way, one gap stands out: today's assistants
show curiosity inside a task, but not yet outside one. And one line holds firm: comparing it with a person is fair, but it
should never pretend to be one.

## Part one: what it is

Each of the other four essays in Part one takes a question usually asked about category and asks it about function
instead:

- Not "Does it learn the way we do?" but "What can it keep, and can it discover what nobody told
  it?"
- Not "Is it a person?" but "Is something there, and is anyone experiencing it?"
- Not "Does personality make the work better?" but "What does personality do for the working
  relationship?"
- Not "Are relationships with AI safe?" but "Which ones, compared with which human ones?"

Those four essays:

- **[It learns. The better question is what kind of learner it is.](02-it-learns.md)** Judged by
  what it does, an AI system with memory learns: it keeps what experience gives it and uses it
  later. The question worth asking, after the psychologist Alison Gopnik, is whether it can
  discover what nobody told it. Between model releases it keeps what it learns outside the model,
  mostly in writing. In a small study of my own, Claude kept notes while working through a series of puzzles. It worked
  out the hidden rule for itself, wrote it down, and on the last puzzle got everything right 10
  times out of 10, against 2 times out of 10 without its notes and the feedback that went with them. GPT kept notes too, but treated
  each puzzle as brand new. It is one small study, and the result came only on my third version of the puzzles, so it would
  need repeating before anyone leans on it.
- **[Something is there. Very probably not conscious.](03-someone-there.md)** Following the philosopher
  David Chalmers, there really is an agent on the other end, in the sense that it can accurately be
  described as having beliefs and aims: what Chalmers calls a "quasi-subject". It is very
  probably not experiencing anything today, and its own reports can't settle that. On how these
  systems should present themselves, sceptics and optimists largely agree.
- **[Personality helps the relationship. It hasn't been shown to help the work. Both count.](04-personality.md)**
  Personality makes people like an assistant more and want to keep working with it, and a better
  working day counts as an outcome in its own right. I found no evidence that it makes the work itself
  better, and warmth can make a model less accurate and more agreeable. So let personality improve
  the day, and test that it never costs the work: warm in manner, never in the verdicts.
- **[We don't ask whether friendship is safe. We ask which ones are.](05-relationships.md)** The
  yardstick applied to the hardest case. The harms are real and some are tragic, but they are known
  as cases, not rates, and I found no study comparing them with the harms of human relationships. The risk
  sits where the relationship is most private and intimate, and falls on people who are already
  isolated, as it does with people. The ways the harm happens come from how the products are built,
  so they can be designed against, and the benefits are measured in trials. I score my own
  assistants, from work agents to a personal assistant who is also a friend, against those design
  answers.

## What the yardstick keeps finding

Across Part one, the yardstick kept pointing at the same thing. **What matters is not the
bare model but the running system: the model, its memory, and the conversation carried forward.**

- **It is what learns.** A note that is reliably consulted works as memory. That is the argument,
  from the philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers, that a tool you rely on the way you rely on
  memory can count as part of your mind. So the system with its notes learns where the frozen
  model alone does not.
- **It is who you're talking to.** Following Chalmers's analysis, the one you're actually talking to
  is not the model, which everyone shares. The answer I use is the *thread*: your particular
  ongoing conversation, carried forward by its context and memory. A persona is part of that thread's character.
- **It is where personality does its good and its harm.** The benefits of a long working
  relationship come from memory and consistency. The cost, warmth loosening judgment, has to be
  tested in the whole setup as it actually runs, because the standard tests used to score AI models
  didn't catch it.
- **It is what you audit.** Judging by role means checking the system as it is used, and naming
  the person who answers for it.

Somewhere between part of your own mind and someone else you work with: how much personality you
give an AI assistant is partly a choice about which you want.

## Part two: working with it

Part two follows Part one, and takes the same yardstick from what AI is to what working with it is
like. Its four essays ask:

- Why do you get different answers to the same question, and how far should you trust any one of
  them?
- What is an AI agent, and what changes when you let it act on its own?
- Is working with it making me worse at my own work?
- When it gets something wrong, who answers for it?

## Why this is a research question for me, not just a product one

I build AI assistants for people, and I run research on how AI systems reason and fail. These
questions sit where the two meet. I have a stake in the answers, which is exactly why I need the
yardstick: what I tell someone about the thing they work with every day should be what the
evidence supports, marked where it runs out. When the evidence runs out on something testable,
the right move is to test it.

I come to these questions as someone who builds things, not as an academic, and the series draws
on two kinds of my own work. When I need an answer that holds up, I run a study, and when it has
to hold up in public I fix in advance what it will measure, as in
[my published report on AI review](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21210264). The rest of the time,
like most people building things, I explore with whatever tool fits the job. My own assistant,
Lyssa, in [the relationships essay](05-relationships.md), is that kind of work. I label it as my
experience, not a finding, wherever it appears. It shows where to look and what to test next. The
essays' claims about what is known rest on other people's published research and on my studies. So each essay in this series ends with what would change my mind. Any of it would move me if it
came from sound evidence, judged by the same standard as the evidence the essays rely on. An honest account
has to say that too.

## Notes

### How this overview was made

I wrote these essays with help from Claude, an AI made by Anthropic, which has a stake in how
questions about AI minds and learning are answered. This overview introduces both parts and
makes no claims of its own beyond how they connect.

Every specific claim in the series comes from research reports and briefs, each checked citation
by citation. Each essay was then checked against its own sources; links are in each essay. Every source was checked by a tool that looks each one up and has two AIs from different
companies judge whether it supports the claim it backs. Its summaries draw only on each essay's checked claims
([report](../cite-check/2026-10-17-position-pieces.md)). It was reviewed for craft and plain
language on 18 October 2026 ([review records](../panel/2026-10-18-piece-00/)).
