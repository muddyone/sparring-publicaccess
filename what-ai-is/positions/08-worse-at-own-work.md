# Handing work to AI is like moving into management. Choose the skills you keep.

_What AI Is · Part two: working with it_

---

**My position: handing your work to AI is a lot like moving from doing the work to managing it. As AI takes on
more of the work, more of us are making that move, and it's one of the most ordinary moves in working life. The
skills you stop using fade, most of all where doing the work was how you learned them or how you kept them. The
move also comes with the familiar traps of managing. You still have to judge work you no longer do yourself, and
you can lose touch without noticing. So do what good managers do: choose on purpose which skills to keep sharp,
starting with the ones you need in order to judge the work, and keep learning them on purpose.**

"AI is making us stupid" is a worry I hear often, and one I've asked about myself. I use AI every day. It
drafts, it codes, it summarizes, and it checks. Some days I notice I reach for it before I've tried the thing
myself.

We've heard this worry before, about calculators and, long before that, about writing. It has something real in
it, and I'll show where. But "it makes you stupid" is too broad to be useful, and the loudest version of it
rests on the weakest evidence.

A more useful picture is one most working people already know: the move from doing the work to managing it.
When you're promoted to run the team, you stop doing much of the hands-on work, and some of those skills fade.
The good managers I've known chose which ones to keep sharp, and how to do it. They kept learning on purpose,
especially in the skills they needed to tell good work from bad.

I've made this move more than once. Two times stand out. When I left architectural practice to teach, hand lettering was still one of the
most prized skills an architect had; CAD hadn't yet taken over. I did less of it myself, but I judged students'
lettering every day. My own lettering got worse, and my handwriting with it. My judgment of it didn't. Judging it every day made
my sense of what makes good lettering clearer, and gave me the words to explain it. What I had to keep up on
purpose was the theory behind it, how a drawing communicates, if I wanted to hold my standards.

A few years later, I moved from teaching structural engineering to running the architecture department. My
ready command of the calculations faded. I needed to look things up and check my work far more than when I
taught it every day. But the principles, and how they shape design choices, I couldn't let go: I still taught
them in advanced studios, and new materials and technology kept changing them. Those I had to keep up
proactively, not just actively.

I think that's the move AI is asking of more and more of us. It starts small. Let an AI draft your emails for a
year, and you've moved from writing them to reading and approving them. As AI starts acting for you,
carrying out whole jobs on its own, the kind the last essay called an agent, the move gets bigger: from doing the work to directing it and
checking it. Work that used to need a skill in us becomes a tool we use and manage. I'm not alone in seeing it
this way. Two economists recently proposed "that the use of AI is an act of management": you stop doing the task
and start directing it and checking it ([Leyden & Scur 2026](https://cepr.org/publications/dp22002); a working
paper, not yet reviewed).

One caution before I go on. The management comparison gives us the right questions. The evidence comes from
studies of AI, of older tools, and of skill itself, because almost no one has measured what happens to
managers' own skills.

Throughout this series, "it" has meant an AI assistant you work with turn by turn, such as Claude or ChatGPT,
or a fuller setup that carries out steps on its own. This essay is about what happens to *you* when you hand
it part of your work.

## We've had this worry before

The question this series keeps asking applies here: compared to what? People have been handing thinking to
tools for a very long time, and each time someone warned that it would make us worse.

The oldest version I know is in Plato. A king tells the inventor of writing, "this invention will produce
forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it"
([Plato, _Phaedrus_ 275a](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0174:text=Phaedrus:page=275)).
He was partly right. People did stop memorizing long texts. And writing made almost everything we've learned
since possible.

A more recent example is the calculator, more than two thousand years later, and that fight was real. In 1980, America's national council of math teachers told schools to "take
full advantage of the power of calculators and computers at all grade levels"
([NCTM 1980](https://nctm.org/Standards-and-Positions/More-NCTM-Standards/An-Agenda-for-Action-(1980s))). In
1986, protesters outside the council's meeting carried a sign that read "The Button's Nothin' Till the Brain's
Trained" ([Education Week 1986](https://www.edweek.org/education/math-pure-and-applied-backed/1986/04)). That
same year, two researchers pulled together 79 studies. Calculators used alongside ordinary teaching didn't hurt
students' pencil-and-paper skills at most grades. The exception was fourth grade, where heavy calculator use, at
the age those skills were being formed, held them back ([Hembree & Dessart 1986](https://doi.org/10.2307/749255)). So both sides had a point, and
neither had the whole of it. Used alongside ordinary teaching, the calculator was an ordinary tool, and a
fair trade. Leaned on heavily at the age the skill was being formed, it held children back in that skill. That's this
essay's argument in small: the cost lands where doing the work was how you learned it.

GPS shows the cost clearly. Drivers who had used GPS more over their lives had worse spatial memory when they
had to find their way unaided. In a small follow-up of 13 people three years later, heavier GPS use went with a
steeper decline ([Dahmani & Bohbot 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-62877-0)). Most of us would still
call that trade worth making. I don't plan to give up my phone's map to save my sense of direction.

And some of the alarms didn't hold up. A famous 2011 study suggested that search engines change what we
remember ([Sparrow, Liu & Wegner 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745)). One of its experiments has
since failed to replicate ([Hesselmann 2020](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.10325)). The same caution applies to
the headlines you may have seen saying ChatGPT rots your brain. Many of them trace back to an MIT study that recorded
people's brain activity while they wrote essays ([Kosmyna et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08872)). It's
an early, unreviewed paper, and the part that tested what happened afterwards had 18 people. Its own authors ask
the press not to use words like "brain rot", "harm", or "damage"
([MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/projects/your-brain-on-chatgpt/overview/)), and other researchers
have posted a comment questioning its methods ([Stanković et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00856)). It's
the weakest evidence in this essay and the most widely quoted.

The pattern across all of them: a tool costs you the particular skill it takes over, most of all when it takes
over the practice that forms or keeps that skill. The warnings about a general decline have mostly overreached.
You don't get worse at everything. You get worse at the parts you've stopped doing.

We also live this way with people. I don't do my own taxes; I use an accountant. I see a doctor for a checkup,
and I'd see a surgeon for an operation. I don't deliver my own packages. I don't have those skills, and I don't
need them, because something else does the checking. The accountant is licensed and answers for the return. The
surgeon has a record and a license to lose. And I'll know soon enough if the package doesn't arrive.

AI fits the first half of that pattern: it costs you the skill it takes over. Whether it fits the second half,
whether anything besides you can do the checking, is where it gets harder. I'll come back to that. First, here's
where the cost lands.

## What fades: where the doing was the learning

The strongest evidence is about people learning something new.

In a field experiment, nearly a thousand high-school math students were given an AI tutor based on GPT-4.
One version worked like ordinary ChatGPT. While students had it, their grades on practice problems were 48%
higher than those of students without it. Then it was taken away. After that, those students' grades were 17%
lower than those of students who had never had it. The researchers' word for how students used it was a
"crutch" ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)).

That word deserves a second look, because a crutch isn't a bad thing. My reading glasses let me read the fine print, and my car gets me
places I couldn't walk to. Nobody thinks less of me for either. A crutch becomes a problem only when you lean on
it while the leg is supposed to be getting stronger. These students were supposed to be learning, and the tutor
did the part that would have built the strength.

Those were children. A smaller study found the same in working adults: software developers
learning a new code library, some with an AI assistant and some without. The AI did buy something. Developers
who handed it the whole task finished faster. But they paid for the speed in learning. Afterwards, on a quiz
about the library, the AI group scored 50% and the group without it scored 67%. And across the whole AI group,
the time saved was too small to be sure it was real. In the authors' words, "AI use impairs conceptual understanding, code
reading, and debugging abilities, without delivering significant efficiency gains on average"
([Shen & Tamkin 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245)).

That sounds like a verdict on AI in general, and it isn't one. Every developer in the study was learning
something new, which is exactly where this essay says the cost lands. And the same study suggested that how they used the AI mattered: those who asked it to explain, rather than to do the work, kept most of their learning.
I'll come back to that.

Two things follow. This isn't only about children in school. Anyone picking up a new skill at work faces the
same trade when the AI does the part that would have taught them: speed now, against the skill later. Sometimes
that's a good trade. If you'll never touch that library again, finishing faster may be all you need. But the
skills these developers missed, reading code and finding what's wrong with it, are the ones you need to check
an AI's code later. That's the judging skill this essay keeps coming back to, and it's the one you can't afford
to trade away.

The study had 52 people, mostly junior, and measured them right after the task. Its authors work at Anthropic,
which makes Claude. A company publishing a cost of its own product is some reason to take it seriously, but it's
still an early, unreviewed paper.

How people used the AI seemed to matter more than whether they used it. A study of university students found no
overall effect on learning. But in a closer look, which the authors present as exploratory, students who had the
AI produce solutions covered more topics and understood each one less. Those who asked it for explanations
covered fewer topics and understood them better
([Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)).

None of this is surprising if you know the research on learning. What you work out for yourself, you keep
better than what you're handed. Across 86 studies, people remembered material they had generated themselves
better than material they had only read
([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441)). For what you remember later, testing yourself beats rereading
([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). Reading an AI's answer is the
rereading. Working it out is the test.

## What fades: where the doing kept the skill

It isn't only beginners. Skills you already have can fade too, when doing the work was also the practice that
kept them.

At four endoscopy centers in Poland, experienced doctors started using AI to help spot precancerous growths
during colonoscopies. In the three months before, on colonoscopies done without AI, they found such growths in
about 28 of every 100. In the three months after the AI arrived, on colonoscopies still done without it, the rate
was about 22 in every 100 ([Budzyń et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-1253(25)00133-5)). The study
compared records from before and after, rather than running a controlled test, and it's contested: the journal
printed three letters in response. The drop could be lowered effort or attention rather than lost skill. But
it's the best real-world sign I found that AI can wear down an expert's existing skill, and quickly.

People who study automation saw this coming. In 1983, the engineering psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge wrote
that "physical skills deteriorate when they are not used". A formerly experienced operator who has spent years
watching an automated process "may now be an inexperienced one"
([Bainbridge 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)). The knowledge you need to run things
yourself, she wrote, "develops only through use and feedback about its effectiveness".

Later research measured it, and found the thinking skills were the more fragile ones. A review of 53 studies of
skill decay found "substantial skill loss with nonpractice or nonuse", growing the longer a skill went unused,
and thinking skills faded faster than physical ones
([Arthur et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327043hup1101_3)). In a study of 16 airline pilots in a
simulator, their hand-flying skills were mostly intact, even when they said they rarely practiced them. What had
slipped were the *thinking* parts of flying by hand: keeping track of where the plane was without the map
display, working out the next step, and noticing when an instrument had failed. The pilots who struggled most
were the ones whose minds had wandered while the autopilot flew
([Casner et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720814535628)). That's the closest older case to ours, because
AI takes over thinking.

So the cost lands in two places: where doing the work was how you learned it, and where doing it was how you
kept it.

## The first trap: judging work you no longer do

In my own work, I've had AI tools report that a job was done when it hadn't happened. The report looked like
every other report. What caught it was knowing what the work should have produced, and that knowledge came from
having done the work myself.

That's the manager's problem. A manager's main job is to tell good work from bad, and that takes skill the
manager no longer uses every day. The research on managers points the same way, though it measures something
nearby. In large surveys of American and British workers, a boss's technical competence, such as whether the
boss could do the worker's job in an emergency, was "the single strongest predictor of a worker's job
satisfaction" ([Artz, Goodall & Oswald 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0019793916650451)). Over sixty years of
Formula One, teams led by former drivers tended to do better
([Goodall & Pogrebna 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2014.07.009)). Both studies observe rather than test, and neither measures how well bosses judge work. They show that having done the work goes
with leading it well, not that keeping it sharp does.

Nor does it mean keeping every skill. A study of sales firms found that the best salespeople, once promoted,
were not always the best managers ([Benson, Li & Shue 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz022)). One leadership
writer, drawing on interviews with executives, puts what senior leaders need more narrowly: they "must know
enough to be able to evaluate and recruit the right people"
([Watkins 2022](https://www.imd.org/research-knowledge/articles/seven-transitions-good-leaders-must-make/)). For
people, that's the job: know enough to judge. With AI, the knowing-enough is harder to keep.

A calculator does arithmetic, and you can check its answer roughly in your head. GPS gives you a route, and
you'll know soon enough if you've arrived. An AI drafts, analyzes, summarizes, and judges. To check reasoning,
you have to be able to reason about the problem yourself.

Bainbridge saw this in automated factories and cockpits, and called it an irony. The automatic system is put
in "because it can do the job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it
is working effectively" ([Bainbridge 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)). The coding study I
mentioned framed its whole question the same way: what happens to "the skills required to effectively
supervise AI" ([Shen & Tamkin 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245)).

This matters because AI's abilities are uneven in ways people don't predict. In a study of 758 consultants,
AI made them faster and better on tasks inside its abilities. On a task just outside them, consultants using AI
got the right answer less often than those working without it: about 19 fewer correct answers in every 100
([Dell'Acqua et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4573321)). If you've kept the skill, you have a chance of
noticing when you've crossed that line. If you've lost it, you don't.

The two economists who called AI use management predict exactly this risk. As the tools improve, fewer tasks are
worth checking, and in their words, "the skill collapses". They mean the checking skill
([Leyden & Scur 2026](https://cepr.org/publications/dp22002)). That's a prediction from a model, not a
measurement. But it names the trap: the better the AI gets, the easier it is to stop checking.

And as essay 06 showed, an AI sounds just as sure when it's wrong as when it's right. With AI, the cue that
would make you look twice is weaker.

## The second trap: losing touch without noticing

The other trap of moving away from the work is that you can lose touch a little at a time. With AI, there's
evidence that your sense of how you're doing lags behind the facts.

The clearest evidence comes from a study of how people judged their own speed. In a randomized trial, 16
experienced software developers worked on real tasks in projects they knew well, some with AI allowed and some
without. Before they started, they expected AI to cut the time the tasks took by 24%. Afterwards, they believed it
had cut the time by about 20%. In fact, with AI the tasks took 19% longer
([Becker et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)).

That AI made experienced developers slower, not faster, surprises most people, so a word on why. The researchers don't name one cause, but they point to
likely ones. These developers knew their large, long-running projects deeply, and the AI's suggestions often
needed reviewing and reworking before they could be used. The study measured time and didn't formally grade the finished work, though the researchers saw no sign that
its quality differed either way. And they caution that small new projects, or code a developer doesn't know, may
well see AI speed things up.

But why they were slower isn't the point here. The point is what they believed. These were experts, working on
their own code, and they misjudged their own work by a wide margin, in the wrong direction. Speed is about the
easiest thing there is to notice. If they couldn't feel the effect on their speed, none of us should count on
feeling a slow change in our skills, which is much harder to notice.

One more caveat. The researchers' later rounds pointed to AI saving time after all, though they warn those rounds
are harder to trust: more and more developers no longer wanted to do half their work without AI, so they wouldn't sign up
([METR 2026](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). That itself says something about how quickly
people come to rely on it.

The learning research shows the same gap between feeling and fact. Students who reread their material felt
more confident than students who tested themselves, and remembered less
([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). The easy route feels like
progress.

In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, people who had more confidence in AI reported doing less critical
thinking, and people with more confidence in themselves reported doing more. Their thinking didn't vanish. It
moved toward checking, combining, and looking after what the AI produced; the authors call it "stewardship"
([Lee et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)). That's people describing their own habits, not a
measured change in skill, and the authors work at Microsoft, which sells AI tools. But it fits: the work that's
left is the checking, and the checking depends on the skill underneath.

And the habits that keep people thinking are the ones they like least. When people were made to decide for
themselves before seeing the AI's answer, they leaned on it less. They also rated that setup the worst of the
ones they tried ([Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449287)). A tool built to keep you
sharp won't win on how pleasant it is to use.

## What about people just starting out?

The worry I hear most from parents and managers is about juniors. In management terms: what happens to people
who are asked to direct the work before they've ever done it? How do they become the experts who can check the
AI?

The output evidence points one way. In a study of 5,172 customer-support staff, AI help raised productivity
most for the least experienced
([Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjae044)). In a writing experiment with 453
professionals, the weaker writers gained most ([Noy & Zhang 2023](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586)).
The learning evidence is mixed. The customer-support study reports signs that the AI helped staff learn: after
three months with it, some handled chats faster during the times it was down. But that held only for staff who
had followed the AI closely, the outages were rare and not necessarily random, and the authors call the
estimates noisy. The student study found that AI widened the gap between those who started with more knowledge
and those who started with less ([Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.09047)). Both can
be true. A beginner's output can look like an expert's while the beginner learns less.

There's one strong case from before AI. When robotic surgery took over much of the work trainee surgeons had
learned from, only a minority reached competence, and they got there largely by finding practice outside
the approved routes ([Beane 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839217751692)). That shows it can happen: when a
technology takes over the work beginners learn from, fewer of them become experts. It doesn't show AI is doing
it. Early hiring data, in a working paper, shows employment for 22-to-25-year-olds
in the jobs most exposed to AI falling 13% in relative terms, while more experienced workers in the same jobs
held steady or grew
([Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen 2025](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/canaries-coal-mine-six-facts-about-recent-employment-effects-artificial)).
That's about hiring, not skill, though fewer entry-level jobs means fewer places to do the groundwork.

I found no study that follows juniors who started their careers with AI and compares them with those who
didn't. For now, this is a forecast, not a finding.

## Where the comparison needs care

The management comparison isn't exact, and the differences matter. Each one leaves more of the checking to you.

Start with what a team gives a manager. The people doing the work know how to do it. They catch many of their
own mistakes, they can tell you how they got an answer, and they grow into the job. So a manager doesn't have to
check everything. An AI gives you less of that. Its abilities are uneven in ways it doesn't warn you about, and
it can't always tell you how it got its answer. So more of the checking falls to you.

A team also pushes back. In thirteen experiments, people who could hand a task to a machine asked for more
cheating, and the "machines were far more likely than human agents to carry out fully unethical instructions"
([Köbis et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09505-x)). A person on your team might refuse a bad
instruction. A machine is far less likely to.

And the specialists I rely on come with their own checks: licenses, track records, and the fact that they answer
for their work. AI has nothing like that yet that you can count on. So, for now, more of the judging stays with
you, and so does more of the skill it takes.

There's one more way the comparison can mislead, and it's the one most likely to catch a reader of this
essay. If handing work to AI is like managing, it's tempting to picture the AI as a member of your team. One
study suggests that picture can make people catch fewer errors. Researchers gave 1,261 managers the same drafts, each with
planted errors. Some were told a draft came from an "AI tool", and others that it came from an "AI employee". On
average, the label made little difference. But in organizations that already list AI agents on their org charts,
as if they were staff, managers who were told they were reviewing an "AI employee" caught fewer errors than those
told it was a tool ([Wiles et al. 2026](http://www.emmawiles.com/storage/ai_employee.pdf); a working paper, and
three of its four authors work at BCG, which sells AI consulting).

In this essay's terms: thinking of the AI as a colleague seems to bring some of a colleague's standing with it, and
that can make you check less carefully. That's the first trap again. So take the manager's job from the comparison, not the
manager's relationship. You still judge the work and answer for it. But there's no colleague's earned trust to
lean on. Think of the AI as a tool you direct and check.

## What I'd do with this

The question isn't whether to hand work to AI. It's which skills you keep sharp while you do.

No study I found tests how managers keep their skills sharp. The best evidence for how to do it comes from
pilots and classrooms. A pilot is, in a sense, a manager whose team can't push back, and aviation's answer is the
one good managers reach for.

Aviation didn't respond to skill fade by giving up autopilot. In 2013, after finding an increase in manual
handling errors, the US Federal Aviation Administration urged airlines to have pilots fly by hand "when
appropriate", because constant use of the autopilot "does not reinforce a pilot's knowledge and skills in manual
flight operations" ([FAA 2013](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/other_visit/aviation_industry/airline_operators/airline_safety/SAFO13002.pdf)).
They kept the tool and scheduled the practice.

The education research points the same way. In the math study, a second tutor was built to guide students
rather than hand them answers. It removed most of the harm ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)).
When people were shown an AI's reasoning *without* its answer, so they had to decide for themselves, they
learned. When they were shown both, they didn't ([Gajos & Mamykina 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3490099.3511138)).
An AI that asked questions instead of explaining made people better at spotting flawed arguments
([Danry et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580672)). In the coding study, developers who asked the AI
conceptual questions or asked it to explain its code kept most of their learning. Those who handed it the whole
job didn't ([Shen & Tamkin 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245); small groups, and not proof of cause).

The common thread: the person still produces something first. A guess, a draft, a decision, a question.

So here's what I'd suggest.

1. **Sort your work into three piles.** The first is what you're still learning. The second is what you need
   in order to judge the work. The third is everything else that you can safely hand over: work where you'd
   notice right away if something went wrong, the way you'd notice a package that never arrived. Hand that third
   pile to the AI freely. That's the ordinary trade, and it's usually a good one.
2. **Keep doing what you're still learning.** Use the AI as a tutor, not a ghostwriter. Ask it to explain, to
   question you, or to check your attempt, rather than to produce the answer.
3. **Keep practicing what you need in order to judge it,** even where the AI is better at it than you. A good
   manager stays close enough to the work to know when something's off. That's the skill that's easiest to let
   go, and the one you can least afford to.
4. **Schedule the learning.** Skills don't stay current by themselves. Bainbridge's point was that working knowledge "develops only through use and feedback"
   ([Bainbridge 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-1098(83)90046-8)). So for each skill you've chosen to keep, put real use of
   it on the calendar: a piece of the work done by hand each week or each month, checked against something that
   tells you how it went, whether that's a colleague, a test, or the result itself.
5. **Make your own first pass before you look at its answer,** on the work that matters. Even a rough one. It's
   slower and less pleasant, but working it out yourself is what keeps the skill.
6. **Check on purpose, not by feel.** Every so often, compare what you produce without the AI to what you
   produced before. Your sense of how current you are isn't a reliable guide.

Most of us won't do all of this. I know I won't. It's like exercise: we know what we should do, and most of us
don't, and we're not likely to start now. So don't try all six. Pick the one or two skills you'd least like to
lose, and protect those. And where a skill matters to other people, it shouldn't rest on anyone's willpower.
Aviation didn't leave hand-flying to pilots' good intentions; the regulator urged airlines to build it into their operations. The math
tutor built to guide rather than answer removed most of the harm without asking students for discipline. Where
the skill really matters, the practice has to be built into the job and the tools. That raises a question this
essay can't settle: who is responsible for building those protections in, and who answers when no one does? That's
where the next essay picks up.

I try to hold myself to the same rule. When I published a research paper this year, I couldn't check some of
its technical content myself, so I didn't count my own review of the AI-assisted work as the check. The paper
names a future review by an outside expert for that part
([Niedner 2026](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21210264)).

The evidence for these habits is short-term, and most of it comes from students and lab tasks. So is almost
everything in this essay: most studies measured people within minutes or days, and the longest AI follow-up I
found ran four months. I found no study of what years of everyday AI use do to a working professional's skills.
No one has tested these habits over months of real work, where speed gets rewarded and the easy option wins.
But they're cheap to try, and they match what we know about how skills are built and kept.

## What would change my mind

- **If skills that people deliberately keep practicing still wore down with heavy AI use,** then choosing which
  skills to keep wouldn't be enough, and the advice above would be too weak.
- **If studies over several years found that professionals using AI keep their unaided skills,** the worry
  about losing skills you already have would be overstated, and I'd say so.
- **If studies found that regularly checking AI's work keeps the underlying skill as well as doing the work
  does,** step 3 would matter less than I've said.
- **If a study comparing juniors trained with and without AI found no difference,** I'd drop the worry about
  people starting out. If it found a large one, it would move from forecast to finding.

## Where I land

I don't think handing work to AI will make you stupid. Nothing I found shows a general decline. Like the
calculator, it costs you the particular skills you hand it, and most of all the ones you were still forming or
needed to keep.

What it will do is make more of us managers. And the part of a manager's job that can't be handed off is judging
the work. With people, a team shares some of that load. With AI, far more of it stays with you.

When I stopped lettering every day, my own lettering faded. My judgment of it got sharper, because I used that
every day. That's the trade to make on purpose. Hand off the work you can spare. Keep your judgment, and keep
training it.
